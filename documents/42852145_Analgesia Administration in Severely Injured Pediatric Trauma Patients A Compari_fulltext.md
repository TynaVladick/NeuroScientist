# Analgesia Administration in Severely Injured Pediatric Trauma Patients: A Comparison of General and Pediatric Emergency Departments

- **Authors:** Shada Abu Ahmed Joffeh, Yoav Carsenty, Nataliay Meltsin, Riham Abu Salah Shibli, Gilad Chayen, Ron Jacob
- **Journal:** Journal of Emergencies, Trauma, and Shock
- **Year:** 2026
- **PMID:** 42852145
- **PMCID:** PMC13649211
- **DOI:** 10.4103/jets.jets_173_25
- **Keywords:** Analgesia; pediatric emergency; trauma

## Abstract

Introduction:: The purpose of the study is to compare analgesia and sedation administration among pediatric major trauma patients managed in the pediatric emergency department (PED) versus the general emergency department (GED).

Methods:: This retrospective cohort study included pediatric trauma patients (<16 years) with an injury severity score (ISS) >15 treated at a single trauma center. Between 2013 and 2015, patients were managed primarily by surgeons in the GED; between 2019 and 2023, by pediatric emergency medicine physicians in the PED, following a 3-year transition period. Data on demographics, injury characteristics, pain assessment, and analgesia/sedation were extracted from the trauma registry and medical records. The primary outcome was analgesia administration rate. Secondary analyses compared provider type and analgesia rates between pediatric and adult trauma patients treated in the GED.

Results:: Seventy-three pediatric patients met inclusion criteria (GED: 36; PED: 37). ISS scores were similar (GED: 16 [interquartile range (IQR) 16–19.5] vs. PED: 17 [IQR 16–21], P = 0.16). Analgesia was administered to 48.6% of PED patients versus 33.3% in the GED (P = 0.18). Opioid use was 19% versus 13.8%, and sedative use was 21.6% versus 8.3%, respectively. Pain score documentation was more consistent in the PED. Children treated in the GED received significantly less analgesia than adults with similar ISS scores treated in the same department (33.3% vs. 78.3%, P < 0.0001).

Conclusions:: Analgesia administration in severely injured children remained low across both emergency settings. While trends suggested improved management in the PED, findings may reflect broader changes in care over time, highlighting the ongoing need for standardized pain assessment and treatment protocols in pediatric trauma care.

## INTRODUCTION

Children with major trauma may be managed either in a general emergency department (GED) by general emergency and surgical teams or in a dedicated pediatric emergency department (PED) by pediatric emergency medicine (PEM) physicians, depending on institutional structure and resources. Previous studies have demonstrated that provider background and department specialization can influence various aspects of trauma care, including airway management, imaging rates, and clinical outcomes.[12]

Optimal pain management is a core principle of contemporary pediatric emergency care, reflected in the “Ouchless ED” paradigm. Acute pain in children is not only distressing but also has been associated with adverse psychological outcomes and other negative sequelae when undertreated, underscoring the importance of timely assessment and appropriate analgesic management.[34]

Despite advances in trauma systems, inadequate pain management in pediatric patients remains a persistent challenge. Studies suggest that younger children and those with more severe injuries are less likely to receive appropriate analgesia, potentially due to difficulties in pain assessment, provider concerns about side effects, and variability in training.[56] Prior studies have demonstrated inadequate pain management in major trauma, including adult trauma populations[7] and pediatric trauma patients, with persistent gaps in pediatric analgesia and sedation practices during severe injury and procedural care.[89] In addition, the underuse of opioid analgesia in children has been described across traumatic injuries and other acute pediatric conditions, highlighting systemic hesitancy and variability in pediatric pain treatment.[61011]

Children with multisystem trauma have better outcomes when treated at dedicated pediatric trauma centers, with advantages reported across morbidity and mortality, appropriate use of imaging, multidisciplinary decision-making, and long-term outcomes.[12131415] These benefits may extend to pain assessment and management, where specialized pediatric settings may be more likely to implement age-appropriate, timely, and effective analgesia.[15161718]

In Israel, multi-trauma and significant pediatric trauma cases were previously managed primarily by surgeons in the GED. However, with the development of the PEM specialty, a structural shift occurred, transitioning pediatric trauma management to PEM-led care in dedicated PEDs.

This study compares analgesia and sedation administration for severely injured pediatric trauma patients treated in these two settings to evaluate the impact of pediatric specialization on pain management practices.

## METHODS

### Study design and setting

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at HaEmek Medical Center, a regional trauma center in Israel with approximately 150,000 annual emergency department (ED) visits. The hospital includes both a GED and a PED, which share access to pharmacological and nonpharmacological pain management resources. The GED evaluates approximately 30,000 trauma-related presentations annually, with approximately 330 trauma activations. The PED has approximately 30,000 annual visits, with trauma-related presentations comprising approximately 40%. Trauma patients were managed by general and pediatric surgeons in the GED between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2015, whereas between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, they were managed by PEM physicians in the PED. The intervening years (2016–2018) were excluded due to a transition period. During this transition, the trauma resuscitation room for pediatric patients was relocated to the PED; it is located across the corridor from the general trauma room, with similar proximity and access to computed tomography imaging. No PEM specialists were present during the earlier period.

### Study population and ethics

The study included all pediatric trauma patients younger than 16 years with an injury severity score (ISS) >15 (major to severe trauma)[1920] who were managed in the GED between 2013 and 2015 and in the PED between 2019 and 2023. Patients with polytrauma were included, and no patients were excluded from the study. Ethics approval was obtained from the HaEmek Medical Center Institutional Review Board (approval number EMC-150-23).

### Pain assessment and protocols

During the study period, both GED and PED used institutional pain management protocols based on national pediatric pain and sedation guidelines from 2010.[21] Pain assessment in the GED was based primarily on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), while the PED utilized age-appropriate tools including the Wong–Baker FACES scale; face, legs, activity, cry, consolability (FLACC); and VAS. The first documented ED pain score (VAS, FLACC, Wong-Baker, as applicable) was extracted when available. For analyses involving pain severity, pain score was categorized as mild,[123] moderate,[456] and severe;[78910] missing pain scores were recorded as missing.

### Data collection and variables

Patients were identified through the institutional trauma registry which is prospectively maintained for the national trauma registry by a dedicated trauma nurse coordinator. Variables were extracted from the trauma registry and complemented by review of ED medical records when required. Data collected included demographics (age and sex), injury characteristics (mechanism and body region involvement), ISS, prehospital and ED procedures (intubation, chest tube, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and blood transfusion), and analgesia/sedation use. These variables are routinely captured in the national trauma registry and were included as indicators of injury severity/clinical instability and resource utilization. Trauma type was categorized as blunt or penetrating. Anatomical regions involved were recorded as head, torso, abdomen, pelvis, and/or limbs, as documented in the trauma registry. Prehospital analgesia and sedation administration were recorded when available in the trauma registry. Dose, route, timing, and indication were not consistently documented.

Analgesics were classified as opioids (fentanyl, morphine, oxycodone, and tramadol) or nonopioids (ibuprofen, paracetamol, and dipyrone). Sedatives included propofol, ketamine, midazolam, and etomidate. Medication selection, routes, and typical dosing followed institutional protocols aligned with national guidelines.[21] Analgesia and sedation were classified as “administered” if a medication was recorded as given; dosing adequacy was not assessed. Pain assessment documentation and the team leader’s specialty were also recorded. Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) on presentation was extracted when available.

The primary outcome was the rate of analgesia administration (any analgesia and opioid analgesia). Secondary outcomes included sedative administration, comparisons between provider types, as well as a comparison of pediatric versus adult trauma patients managed in the GED. The adult comparison included all adults with ISS >15 treated in the GED during 2013–2015.

### Statistical analysis

Comparisons were performed between groups (GED vs. PED) and between analgesia administration (received vs. not received). Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables were analyzed using the t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was carried out using R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). No priori sample size calculation was performed because all eligible patients during the predefined study periods were included.

## RESULTS

Seventy-three pediatric patients met the inclusion criteria: 36 managed in the GED and 37 in the PED. The two groups were comparable in ISS (GED: 16 [IQR 16–19.5] vs. PED: 17 [IQR 16–21], P = 0.16). The overall median age was 3 years; 83% were male. Most injuries were blunt trauma involving the head. Median GCS on presentation was 15 (IQR 14–15) in the GED and 15 (IQR 15–15) in the PED. Among children with head injuries in the PED cohort, GCS on presentation was 15. Prehospital interventions were rare, with one patient in each group requiring intubation. Pain management was also compared between provider types. Any analgesia was administered to 50.0% of patients managed by PEM physicians and 38.3% managed by surgeons (P = 0.425). Similarly, opioid analgesia was given to 20.0% versus 21.3%, respectively (P = 1.000). Sedatives were used in 25.0% of patients managed by PEM physicians compared to 12.8% in those managed by surgeons (P = 0.282). Prehospital analgesia was administered in 15.0% of cases under PEM physicians versus 19.1% under surgeons (P = 1.000).

In a prespecified subanalysis, we compared the 36 children treated in the GED with all adults with ISS >15 treated in the GED during the same period (n = 180). Any analgesia was more frequent in adults than in children (78.3% vs. 33.3%, P < 0.0001), with opioid use also higher in adults (52% vs. 13.8%, P < 0.0001). Prehospital analgesia was given to 21.6% of adults and 14% of children (P = 0.406), while sedatives were administered to 19.4% of adults and 8.3% of children (P = 0.174).

## DISCUSSION

This study explored pain management practices in pediatric major trauma, comparing patients treated in a GED to those managed in a PED. Although the difference in analgesia administration rates between groups did not reach statistical significance, a higher proportion of children in the PED received analgesia and sedatives. These findings align with previous literature indicating that children with major trauma are at higher risk of inadequate pain management, particularly when treated in nonspecialized settings.[56] They may also reflect evolving practices, provider training, and the use of pediatric-specific pain assessment tools.

The observed differences in pain management may be attributed to several factors. While both departments followed institutional pain management protocols, variability in adherence or clinical judgment may explain some of the differences observed, along with provider training and specialization.[2223] Although not statistically significant, the higher proportion of analgesia use by PEM physicians may further suggest that specialized pediatric emergency care is associated with improved pain management.[242526] Furthermore, adults in the GED were more likely to receive analgesia, including opioids, than children with similar ISS scores presenting to the GED. This may also reflect the well-documented issue of pediatric pain under-treatment, which has been previously attributed to provider hesitancy, safety concerns, and difficulties in pain assessment in younger populations.[2427]

Concerns about respiratory depression and hypotension may contribute to cautious use of opioids and sedative agents in pediatric trauma, particularly in children with physiologic instability. In our cohort, markers of instability were uncommon: prehospital intubation occurred in 1 patient in each group (2.7%), ED intubation was infrequent (GED 8.3% vs. PED 10.8%), and blood transfusion was rare (GED 5.5% vs. PED 2.7%). Neurologic status was largely preserved (median GCS 15 in both groups). While our study did not capture granular physiological parameters or specific adverse events related to medication administration, current trauma and pediatric analgesia-sedation guidelines emphasize the need for careful patient selection, dose titration, and close monitoring. This emphasis highlights the persistent lack of high-quality evidence available to guide safe and effective analgesic strategies for children with major trauma.[52628] In this context, ketamine (at analgesic doses) is frequently recommended when instability is a concern because it generally preserves respiratory drive and hemodynamics relative to many alternatives, whereas benzodiazepines and propofol may increase the risk of respiratory compromise and hypotension in vulnerable patients.[528] At the same time, available evidence suggests that procedural sedation in carefully selected, stable children (for example, nonintubated patients with preserved mental status) has a low rate of serious adverse events when delivered by trained teams with appropriate monitoring and airway capability.[59]

Differences in pain assessment documentation between departments may have further contributed to treatment variability. In the PED, consistent use of validated tools such as FLACC and Wong–Baker FACES scales may have enabled more effective recognition of pain. Conversely, incomplete pain documentation in the GED could reflect under-recognition and under-treatment, although our data cannot directly attribute causality. Moreover, our findings suggest that younger pediatric patients receive less analgesia than older children in both groups. This aligns with literature indicating that younger children often cannot accurately communicate their pain, and providers may be hesitant to administer analgesia due to challenges in interpreting pain scales and concerns over side effects.[5622] These factors likely contributed to the observed disparities and emphasize the importance of age-appropriate assessment and providing support.

Institutional factors, including shared protocols and resource availability, may help standardize treatment approaches, potentially minimizing individual provider differences. However, systemic barriers such as workload, time constraints, and ingrained practice patterns may still contribute to variations in pediatric pain management, emphasizing the need for continued efforts to optimize care. Addressing these barriers may require targeted education and policy changes to ensure that pediatric trauma patients receive equitable pain management.

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design inherently limits causal inferences, and despite efforts to adjust for confounders, unmeasured variables may have influenced the findings. The long time gap – nearly a decade – between the compared cohorts may confound interpretation, as observed differences could reflect broader improvements in emergency care over time rather than departmental specialization alone. Missing pain scores, particularly in the GED, further highlight the challenges in recognizing and addressing pain in pediatric trauma patients, reinforcing the need for standardized pain assessment protocols. We did not perform multivariable regression due to limited sample size, substantial missing pain score data in the GED, and low event counts for several potential confounders (e.g. intubation and transfusion), which would render a multivariable model underpowered and unstable. Markers of physiological instability were rare in this cohort, which may reduce but does not eliminate residual confounding. In addition, variations in individual provider practices and departmental workflow constraints were not fully accounted for. Importantly, the sample size may have limited the ability to detect modest between-group differences. Using the observed proportions (33.3% vs. 48.6%) as a planning estimate, approximately 161 patients per group would be required to detect a similar difference with 80% power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized pain assessment and management protocols are warranted to further investigate these trends and optimize pediatric trauma pain care.

## CONCLUSIONS

This retrospective study found that children received significantly less analgesia than adults in the GED, despite similar injury severity. While nonsignificant trends suggested higher analgesia and sedative use in the PED, the time gap between cohorts may also reflect broader improvements in care over time. Across both settings, overall analgesia rates were low, underscoring the need for further investigation into pediatric pain management and the potential role of standardized assessment and provider training.
