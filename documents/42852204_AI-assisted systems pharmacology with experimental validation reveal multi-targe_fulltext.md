# AI-assisted systems pharmacology with experimental validation reveal multi-target anticancer mechanisms of Ganoderma lucidum extracts against lung cancer

- **Authors:** Haiyan Wang, Leilei Chen, Siqi Zhang, Mohan Ju, Junfeng Feng, Zixiang Tang, Jinwei Li, Hesham R. El-Seedi, Yanbing Wang
- **Journal:** Mycology
- **Year:** 2026
- **PMID:** 42852204
- **PMCID:** PMC13647369
- **DOI:** 10.1080/21501203.2026.2634522
- **Keywords:** Ganoderma lucidum; lung cancer; polypharmacology; tumor microenvironment; PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway; STAT3 signaling; immune modulation; multitarget therapy

## Abstract

The medicinal fungus Ganoderma lucidum is rich in bioactive triterpenoids and polysaccharides. The complexity and adaptive resistance of lung cancer necessitate therapeutic strategies that simultaneously disrupt multiple oncogenic pathways. We employed a systems pharmacology approach that integrates network analysis and artificial intelligence to elucidate the multitarget anticancer mechanisms of G. lucidum compounds. Bioactive metabolites were screened, and their targets were integrated with lung cancer-associated genes to construct a compound-target-pathway network. AI-based molecular docking validated key interactions. The top-predicted multitarget compounds were functionally validated in H1299 nonsmall cell lung cancer cells using viability, migration, and Western blot assays. A network of 67 synergistic targets, including PIK3CA, STAT3, EGFR, and TP53, concurrently modulates proliferation, apoptosis, and immune evasion pathways. Triterpenoids, such as lucidumol A and ganoderic acid A, act as key drivers capable of binding multiple signaling hubs. These metabolites and a standardized G. lucidum extract synergistically disrupt oncogenic signaling by inhibiting cell proliferation (extract IC50 = 1669 μg/mL), suppressing migration, and reducing STAT3 phosphorylation and c-Myc expression. These fungal metabolites exhibit potent multitarget tumor-suppressive properties.

## Introduction

Given the established therapeutic potential of fungal extracts, research has identified Ganoderma lucidum as a particularly promising medicinal mushroom. G. lucidum has been frequently and widely used as food, nutritional supplement, or part of Chinese medicine formula to prevent or treat various ailments (Lau et al. 2024). A large, edible mushroom with a dark red, glossy cap, and a woody texture, G. lucidum has more than 1800 years of documented use in promoting health and longevity in East Asia (Yang 1998). Revered as “immortal mushroom” for treating various diseases and nurturing life, G. lucidum, called lingzhi in China and reishi or mannentake in Japan, is not only consumed as tea, cooked with meats or grains, or used as functional raw material of alcoholic beverages but also prescribed as part of Chinese medicine formula to treat various diseases, such as inflammation, insomnia, coughing, immunodeficiency, and cancer. This mushroom grows saprophytically and parasitically on deciduous trees and has a wide native range, spanning forested regions of Asia, Europe, and the Americas (Siwulski et al. 2015).

Globally, lung cancer is both the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the deadliest, responsible for an estimated 2.48 million new cases and 1.81 million deaths in 2022 (Bray et al. 2024). The disease is classified into two main subtypes: the predominant nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), which comprises 10%–15% of diagnoses. The usual treatments for NSCLC, such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, are associated with various side effects and may not always be effective (Miller and Hanna 2021). The most prominent treatment type, tumor immunotherapy, benefits less than 30% of patients. Its adverse effects, including gastrointestinal liver diseases, pneumonitis, and endocrine diseases, create serious health problems (Sánchez de Cos Escuín 2017). Therefore, the efforts to look for effective alternative therapies and prevention with minimal adverse effects are urgent.

The search for novel anticancer agents has increasingly focused on natural products, particularly the bioactive compounds derived from medicinal mushrooms. G. lucidum has become a prominent source of pharmacologically active molecules, including triterpenoids, polysaccharides, and sterols, which have demonstrated significant anticancer properties in various studies (Sliva 2003; Boh et al. 2007; Oludemi et al. 2018; Martínez-Montemayor et al. 2019; Ahmad 2020). While the efficacy of these compounds against lung cancer cells—through mechanisms, such as apoptosis induction, proliferation and migration inhibition, and pathway modulation—has been established, a systematic and comprehensive analysis of the entire G. lucidum bioactive compound repertoire remains unexplored (Tang et al. 2006; Ahmad 2020).

Concurrently, artificial intelligence (AI) and graph-based computational methods have emerged as transformative tools in drug discovery, facilitating target identification and the prediction of compound efficacy and toxicity (Fleming 2018; Sellwood et al. 2018; Chan et al. 2019; Jiménez-Luna et al. 2020). The application of these integrated bioinformatics approaches to the study of G. lucidum compounds presents a novel strategy to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying their antilung cancer effects. Therefore, this study aims to bridge this gap by consolidating currently disparate data on G. lucidum bioactives and employing a multi-methodological framework. This study aims to utilize mathematical modeling, computational analysis, and AI-driven molecular docking to investigate their therapeutic potential against lung cancer, with subsequent validation through in-vitro cell experiments.

## Materials and methods

### Bioactive compounds screening from G. lucidum

G. lucidum active compounds were extracted from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP, https://tcmspw.com/index.php) (Ru et al. 2014) and ETCM database (http://www.tcmip.cn/ETCM/) (Xu et al. 2019). G. lucidum active compounds were assessed with the cutoff value as oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug likeness of (DL) ≥ 0.18 in the current study or with “moderate” or “good” drug likeness. All identified compounds’ PubChem CID and canonical smiles were obtained using PubChem pug rest API (Kim et al. 2018).

### G. lucidum compound target prediction

Respective targets for each compound were predicted using Batman http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/ (Liu et al. 2016) with target prediction score cutoff of 20 and adjusted p-value of 0.05, SEA Similarity ensemble approach (SEA) http://sea.bkslab.org (Keiser et al. 2007) with MaxTC ≥ 0.4 and species =HUMAN, PubChem https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ (Kim et al. 2021), and PharmMapper http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/ (Wang et al. 2017) with z’-score > 2. Gene targets were standardized using UniProt (Consortium 2021).

### Lung cancer target prediction

Lung cancer disease targets were mined from DrugBank, DisGeNET, GEO, and OMIM. DrugBank (Wishart et al. 2018) contains information about drugs and their respective drugs. DisGeNET (Piñero et al. 2020) curates disease associated genes and variants. GEO (Barrett et al. 2013) (Gene Expression Omnibus, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) is a public database where researchers can submit and share high-throughput genomic data, such as microarray and sequencing datasets. OMIM (Amberger et al. 2015) (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, http://omim.org) is a knowledge base that provides expert-curated information on human genes, phenotypes, and their interrelationships. Gene targets were standardized using UniProt and duplicates were removed.

### GO and KEGG analysis

R ClusterProfiler 4.0 (Wu et al. 2021) was used for GO enrichment analysis and KEGG analysis of the lung cancer-related targets of G. lucidum components.

### PPI network

The harvested G. lucidum compound targets and lung cancer-related targets were input into a program written in Python programming language 3.10 to find the intersected genes. The PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (https://www.string-db.org/) (Szklarczyk et al. 2021).

### Compound-target-pathway network construction

The results of STRING analysis – PPI network, drug-target mapping, gene-pathway mapping were imported into Cytoscape 3.9.1 software (Shannon et al. 2003) to construct compound-target-pathway graph network.

### Artificial intelligence-based molecular docking

In-silico methods were applied to test binding affinity of identified compounds and their respective targets. Molecular docking was performed using DiffDock, a deep learning model using generative diffusion to predict the binding modes of ligands and protein (Corso et al. 2022). DiffDock uses a scoring function that compares the binding energies of multiple conformations, allowing for the identification of subtle structural differences between ligands. Compound structural data files were downloaded from PubChem. Protein Data Bank (PDB) format files were downloaded from RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) (Berman et al. 2000). Visualization of docking results was performed using PyMOL version 2.5.5. Protein-ligand interaction profile was conducted using PLIP (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index) (Adasme et al. 2021).

### Cell experiment reagents

G. lucidum extract containing 30% polysaccharides was purchased from G-clone (CS0016, Beijing, China). Lucidumol A (≥ 98% purity) was purchased from Acima (L7624, Beijing, China) and ganoderic acid A (≥ 98% purity) from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co. Ltd. (B20742, Shanghai, China).

To prepare the 50 mg/mL stock solution, 50 mg of G. lucidum extract powder was dissolved in 1 mL of sterilized, double-distilled water, and subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. Lucidumol A and ganoderic acid A were individually dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to create stock solutions at concentrations of 50 mmol/L and 100 mmol/L, respectively. These stock solutions were stored at 4 °C and subsequently diluted to the desired experimental concentrations using the medium before use.

### Cell culture

The human lung carcinoma cell line H1299 was provided by Professor Lai Fangfang’s Research Lab at the Institute of Materia Medica, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and Peking Union Medical College (Beijing, China). These cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, MA, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The culture was maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator (Model # 3543, Thermo Scientific, USA).

### Cell viability assay

H1299 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates (Costar Corning, USA). After 24 h, the cells were divided into different groups for treatment with either G. lucidum extract, lucidumol A, or ganoderic acid A. The first group was treated with G. lucidum extract at concentrations of 0, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1000, 1600, 3200, and 5000 μg/mL for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. The second group received lucidumol A at concentrations of 0, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 μmol/L for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. The third group was treated with ganoderic acid A at concentrations of 0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 μmol/L for the same time intervals.

Following the treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Meilunbio, Beijing, China) was added to each well, and the incubation continued for 2 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, absorbance was measured using μQuant Microplate Spectrophotometer (BioTek, USA) at 450 nm. The cell viability rate (%) was calculated as follows:Cell viability%=A450treated−A450blankA450control−A450blank× 100%

### Wound healing assay

The H1299 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates. After 24 h, the cell monolayer was carefully scratched using a sterile pipette tip, and the media were aspirated. The cells were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, P1010, Sorlabio, Beijing, China) before being replenished with medium containing varying concentrations of G. lucidum extract (0, 200, 400, or 5000 μg/mL). Cell movements were captured at 0, 20, and 30 h of incubation using a phase-contrast inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). The rate of cell migration was subsequently analyzed using open-source ImageJ software.

### Antibodies

β-actin was purchased from ZZSGB-BIO (Beijing, China). p-STAT3 was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA), STAT3, and C-Myc were acquired from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

### Western blot assay

The H1299 cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Following treatment with G. lucidum extract (at concentrations of 0, 200, 400, and 800 μg/mL) for 72 h, total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (C1053, Applygen, Beijing, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors (P1265, Applygen, Beijing, China) and phosphatase inhibitors (P1260, Applygen, Beijing, China). Similarly, H1299 cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Following treatment with lucidumol A (at concentrations of 0, 6.25, 25 μmol/L) for 24 h, total proteins were extracted. Then, protein concentrations were determined using BCA reagents (P1511, Applygen, Beijing, China). Subsequently, proteins were loaded onto 10% or 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). After blocking with 5% milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the previously described primary antibodies. Following this, membranes were probed with secondary antibodies, either HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG or HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG (C1309, C1308, Applygen, Beijing, China), for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized using Super ECL Plus Chemiluminescence Solution (P1050, Applygen, Beijing, China).

### Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphs were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0. All results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Two experimental groups were compared by Student’s t-test. Multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with Student’s t-test, depending on the experiments. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

## Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the multifaceted therapeutic potential of G. lucidum compounds in lung cancer treatment. These bioactive compounds demonstrate a polypharmacological approach, targeting a broad spectrum of molecular pathways that are central to cancer progression, immune evasion, and treatment resistance. This multitarget activity is a key feature of modern cancer therapies, as it offers a more integrated and comprehensive strategy compared to conventional single-target treatments. Identified chemical compounds, including beta-sitosterol, peroxyergosterol, ganodesterone, ganoderic acid A, lucidumol A, and ganoderiol F, have been found to act on multiple targets in biological networks, along with other active components. The identified phytochemicals were primarily categorized into triterpenes and sterols. G. lucidum triterpenoids (GLTs) are derivatives of the lanostane structure and are chemically classified into two primary groups based on their functional groups: Ganoderma alcohols, bearing a hydroxyl group on the lanostane skeleton, and ganoderic acids, which possess a carboxyl group on the side chain (Xia et al. 2014).

### Chemical characteristics and polypharmacological potential

Triterpenes and sterols are characteristic compounds found in G. lucidum. Triterpenes, which are C30 terpenoids, are primarily derived from oxidosqualene through a cyclization reaction catalyzed by oxidosqualene cyclase. The primary bioactive triterpenes in G. lucidum include ganoderma alcohols and ganoderic acids. Sterols, on the other hand, are crucial structural lipids that play an essential role in maintaining the integrity of eukaryotic cell membranes. These ring-shaped lipids are vital components of most eukaryotic cell membranes.

One of the most compelling aspects of these compounds is their ability to affect critical pathways involved in cell survival, apoptosis regulation, and immune modulation. For instance, beta-sitosterol targets critical targets, such as PIK3CA, TP53, EGFR, STAT3, VEGFA, and PTGS2 across multiple pathways. These compounds show promising therapeutic effects against lung cancer, demonstrated in both laboratory and animal studies. Beta-sitosterol, for example, inhibits the growth of human lung cancer cells by interfering with cholesterol absorption in the small intestine (Sayeed and Ameen 2015). Another compound, peroxyergosterol, which is uncommon among sterols, possesses diverse properties including antioxidant, antibacterial, immunosuppressive, and antitumor activities (Kuo et al. 2003; Akihisa et al. 2005; Takei et al. 2005). G. lucidum sterols (GLS) also inhibit the p38 MAPK pathway by preventing its phosphorylation, indicating potential anti-inflammatory properties (Xu et al. 2021).

Research has shown that triterpenoids isolated from G. lucidum—for example, ganolucidic acid E, lucidumol A, ganodermanontriol, and ganoderic acid DM—can inhibit proliferation and trigger apoptotic cell death in human cell lines, including Caco-2 and HeLa (Ruan et al. 2015). G. lucidum triterpenoids show targeted anti-cancer activity. Ganoderiol F promotes apoptosis in breast cancer via Foxo3 upregulation and Bcl-2/c-Myc downregulation (Li et al. 2019). Ganoderic acid A sensitizes liver cells to cisplatin by suppressing JAK/STAT3 signaling (Yao et al. 2012). Importantly, multiple triterpenoid alcohols, particularly ganoderiol F, are cytotoxic to Lewis lung carcinoma cells (Gao et al. 2006), underscoring their potential as lung cancer therapeutics.

### Network targets and core oncogenic pathways

The potential therapeutic targets identified in this study are fundamentally involved in the metabolism and progression of lung tumor cells. A primary example is the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) family. As key lipid kinases in the canonical PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway, PI3Ks regulate critical cellular processes including metabolism, proliferation, and survival (Liu et al. 2009; Thorpe et al. 2015). Upon activation, PI3K initiates signaling cascades that drive oncogenic functions such as uncontrolled growth and cell survival (Hernandez-Aya and Gonzalez-Angulo 2011). This pathway is among the most frequently dysregulated in cancer, directly impacting tumorigenic hallmarks like proliferation and genomic stability (Hanahan and Weinberg 2011), which underpins its attractiveness as a drug target. Notably, PI3K and its effector Akt serve as a major signaling axis downstream of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), a receptor of paramount importance in lung cancer biology (Wu et al. 2016).

In the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, several key nodes—EGFR, TP53, PIK3CA, mTOR, STAT3, MYC, and IL0—emerged as hub nodes due to their high degree, betweenness, and closeness centrality values. EGFR mutations are prevalent in 10% to 15% of Caucasian patients and up to 50% of East-Asian patients, particularly in those with lung adenocarcinoma, the most common form among nonsmokers (D’Angelo et al. 2011; Harrison et al. 2020). Mutations in the tumor suppressor TP53 are even more pervasive, occurring in most small-cell lung cancers and over half of non-small cell lung cancers (Govindan and Weber 2014). While the wild-type protein maintains genomic integrity, its mutated form promotes oncogenic survival (Govindan and Weber 2014). Furthermore, PIK3CA, which encodes the catalytic subunit of PI3K, is among the most frequently mutated genes following TP53, underscoring its significant role in lung carcinogenesis (Vallejo-Díaz et al. 2019).

Frequently activated in lung cancer, mTOR is a cytoplasmic serine-threonine kinase belonging to the PI3K-related kinase family that plays a central oncogenic role (Ekman et al. 2012). This kinase governs essential cellular functions, from core processes like proliferation, autophagy, and apoptosis to the regulation of critical metabolic pathways for amino acids, glucose, nucleotides, lipids, and fatty acids (Mossmann et al. 2018; Zou et al. 2020). Moreover, the mTOR signaling pathway can be activated by various carcinogenic stimuli (Corradetti and Guan 2006; Shimobayashi and Hall 2014). As a central component of the oncogenic PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway, mTOR is commonly hyperactivated in lung cancer (Panwar et al. 2023). This driving role makes mTOR, along with PI3K, a compelling target, leading to the ongoing development of respective inhibitors for NSCLC treatment (Tan 2020).

### STAT3-centered crosstalk and tumor microenvironment modulation

Dysregulation of multiple signaling pathways in lung cancer converges on Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3). STATs are activated by a range of extracellular stimuli, including growth factors, such as EGF, cytokines, and hormones, through cell-surface receptors like EGFR that possess intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity (Silvennoinen et al. 1993; Yu et al. 2014). Elevated levels of phosphorylated STAT3 have been observed in lung cancer patients and are associated with poorer overall survival (Tong et al. 2017). In ischemia/reperfusion injury rat models, treatment with ganoderic acid A significantly reduced the phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3 (Zhang et al. 2020). In NSCLC, STAT3 is constitutively activated in over 50% of patients (Haura et al. 2005; Sánchez-Ceja et al. 2006), with increased phosphorylated STAT3 levels correlating with advanced disease stage, therapeutic resistance, and EGFR status, thereby serving as a negative prognostic indicator (Yin et al. 2011, 2012; Lee et al. 2014; Mohrherr et al. 2020). Through its roles in tumor-promoting inflammation and immune evasion, STAT3 critically shapes the lung cancer tumor microenvironment (Mohrherr et al. 2020). Constitutive STAT3 activation in NSCLC has been linked to mutations in receptor tyrosine kinases, particularly EGFR (Kim et al. 2012; Lee et al. 2014).

Interleukin-10 (IL-10), a potent anti-inflammatory cytokine, has been associated with poor prognosis in patients with NSCLC (Zeni et al. 2007; Hsu et al. 2016). IL-10 activates the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway and upregulates EGFR expression, which subsequently enhances IL-10 production via PI3K signaling. This positive feedback loop contributes to sustained oncogenic signaling and promotes tumor progression (Parakh et al. 2021).

G. lucidum compounds not only target cancer cells but also modulate key aspects of the tumor microenvironment (TME), which plays a critical role in tumor progression, metastasis, and immune evasion. These compounds influence immune-related molecules like IL10 and CXCL8, which are involved in immune suppression and inflammation (Asokan and Bandapalli 2021). By reducing IL10 and modulating CXCL8, G. lucidum compounds may enhance immune responses and limit the inflammatory environment that often supports tumor growth. Furthermore, they target MMP9 and PTGS2, which are associated with inflammation, angiogenesis, and tumor cell invasion (Zeng et al. 2022), thereby potentially reducing tumor metastasis and impairing the ability of the tumor to thrive in the TME. The compounds also inhibit VEGFA, a key regulator of angiogenesis, which could suppress the tumor’s blood supply and further hinder its growth (Claesson-Welsh and Welsh 2013). Additionally, by targeting HIF1A, G. lucidum compounds disrupt the tumor’s adaptation to hypoxic conditions, impairing its survival under low-oxygen environments (Cimmino et al. 2019). The modulation of MPO, an enzyme involved in immune suppression, suggests that these compounds may help reprogram the TME to be more conducive to immune activity (Arnhold and Flemmig 2010). Collectively, these findings highlight G. lucidum‘s potential not only to directly target cancer cells but also to alter the TME in ways that enhance immune responses, reduce inflammation, and limit angiogenesis, making the TME less supportive of tumor growth and metastasis.

### Pathway enrichment and tumor heterogeneity

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified several pathways directly associated with lung cancer, including NSCLC (hsa05223) and SCLC (hsa05222). Among these, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway was prominently enriched, with a large number of genes represented in the network. This pathway is a key regulator of fundamental cellular processes such as growth, proliferation, differentiation, survival, and invasion. It plays a central role in the initiation and progression of NSCLC (Pérez-Ramírez et al. 2015) and is also implicated in the regulation of apoptosis in NSCLC cell lines (Tuya et al. 2017). Clinically, PIK3CA mutations were identified in 3.7% of a large cohort of 1144 consecutive NSCLC patients (Scheffler et al. 2015). Moreover, activation of the AKT pathway has been observed in 51% of NSCLC tumors, underscoring its high prevalence and biological significance in primary NSCLC (Balsara et al. 2004).

Lung cancer patients exhibit diverse genetic profiles. For instance, lung squamous cell carcinoma shows varying exonic mutations and differentially expressed genes (Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network 2012). Molecular analysis of lung adenocarcinomas indicates an average of 8.9 mutations per megabase (Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network 2014). G. lucidum may modulate distinct pathways depending on specific gene mutations and expressions. Given this tumoral heterogeneity, a multi-target therapy approach is warranted.

The results highlight significant cross-talk between various molecular pathways targeted by G. lucidum compounds, a key feature of their polypharmacological action. Multiple compounds, such as beta-sitosterol, peroxyergosterol, and ganodesterone, engage with interconnected signaling networks, creating synergistic effects that enhance their therapeutic potential. For instance, the modulation of PIK3CA, BCL2, STAT3, and VEGFA by these compounds exemplifies how targeting critical survival, apoptosis, and angiogenesis pathways can simultaneously influence various aspects of tumor biology. This cross-talk between pathways involved in cell growth, inflammation, immune modulation, and metastasis suggests that G. lucidum compounds could not only inhibit cancer cell proliferation but also influence the TME in a way that enhances immune response and limits tumor progression. The compounds’ effects on MMP9, PTGS2, and CXCL8 further demonstrate how the modulation of key targets can alter both the tumor cells and their surrounding environment, leading to a reduction in inflammation and metastasis. The interplay between these diverse pathways underscores the potential of G. lucidum to act on multiple fronts simultaneously, making it a promising candidate for combination therapies that could overcome the adaptive resistance mechanisms commonly seen in cancer treatment.

### Experimental validation and mechanistic consistency

The findings from the cell experiment underscore the potential of G. lucidum extract, ganoderic acid A, and lucidumol A as promising therapeutic agents against non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). G. lucidum extract demonstrated significant dose-dependent suppression of H1299 cell viability and migration, as evidenced by our CCK-8 assay and wound healing assay results. Specifically, concentrations of 200 μg/mL, 400 μg/mL, and 5000 μg/mL effectively reduced cell migration rates, and the extract exhibited an IC50 of 1669 μg/mL, indicating its potent anti-proliferative effects.

Lucidumol A, another component of G. lucidum, also showed promising anti-proliferative activity against H1299 cells in a time- and dose-dependent manner. Treatment over 48, 72, and 96 h demonstrated dose-dependent inhibition of cell viability. These results underscore lucidumol A’s potential as a targeted therapy for NSCLC.

Moreover, ganoderic acid A exhibited notable effects on H1299 cell viability after 96 h of treatment, particularly at concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 μmol/L. This compound significantly inhibited cell proliferation, complementing the findings of G. lucidum extract and lucidumol A.

Beyond their effects on cell viability and migration, G. lucidum extract and lucidumol A were shown to modulate the STAT3 signaling pathway, a central regulator of cancer progression. Treatment of H1299 cells with G. lucidum extract at 800 μg/mL resulted in a marked downregulation of phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) and C-MYC protein expression. Consistently, lucidumol A treatment at concentrations of 6.25 μg/mL and 25 μg/mL produced dose-dependent reductions in p-STAT3 and C-MYC protein levels. Collectively, these results indicate that G. lucidum extract, lucidumol A, and ganoderic acid A exert anti-cancer effects, at least in part, through inhibition of STAT3 signaling, a pathway known to support tumor cell survival and proliferation. The capacity of these natural compounds to simultaneously target multiple pathways and regulate key oncogenic proteins highlights their potential value as complementary therapeutic agents in the treatment of NSCLC.

### Limitations and future perspectives

This study integrates AI-assisted molecular docking, systems pharmacology, and in-vitro experimental validation to elucidate the multi-target anticancer mechanisms of G. lucidum; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. DiffDock was used as an AI-based docking tool to support network-level target identification, and comparative docking with alternative platforms was beyond the scope of the present work. Future studies incorporating multi-tool docking may further strengthen binding robustness. Biological validation was limited to experiments up to 96 h and extended time-course analyses would be valuable to assess sustained efficacy and adaptive cellular responses. Although transcriptomic profiling was not performed, pathway-level interpretations based on network targets were provided; integration with gene-expression data represents an important future direction. In addition, while structure-activity relationship analysis and direct comparisons with standard lung cancer chemotherapeutics were not conducted, the identified bioactive compounds and overlapping target pathways provide a rational basis for subsequent optimization and combinatorial studies. Together, these considerations define the current boundaries of the study while highlighting opportunities for future mechanistic and translational investigations.

## Conclusions

This study combined bioinformatics approaches with AI-driven analyses to investigate the therapeutic potential of bioactive compounds derived from G. lucidum in the treatment of lung cancer. A total of 86 compounds were identified, predominantly triterpenes and sterols, with ganoderic acids and ganoderma alcohols emerging as major bioactive constituents. These compounds were found to act on multiple molecular targets, including PI3K, EGFR, TP53, and STAT3, which play critical roles in lung tumor metabolism and progression. Protein-protein interaction network analysis further revealed key hub nodes—such as EGFR, TP53, PIK3CA, mTOR, STAT3, MYC, and IL10—characterized by high centrality and underscoring their central involvement in lung cancer pathogenesis and their potential as therapeutic targets. Experimental validation through cell viability and migration assays demonstrated the anticancer efficacy of G. lucidum extract, lucidumol A, and ganoderic acid A in NSCLC models. G. lucidum extract exhibited pronounced dose-dependent inhibition of cell proliferation and migration, as supported by IC50 values and wound-healing assay results. Lucidumol A showed strong anti-proliferative activity across multiple treatment durations, while ganoderic acid A significantly reduced cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner. Collectively, these findings provide compelling evidence for the therapeutic potential of G. lucidum extracts in lung cancer treatment. By elucidating underlying mechanisms of action and identifying critical molecular targets, this work contributes to the development of novel therapeutic strategies. Moreover, the integrative AI- and bioinformatics-based framework presented here offers a generalizable strategy for systematically screening bioactive compounds from other fungi, facilitating the discovery of promising drug candidates across a wide range of diseases.
