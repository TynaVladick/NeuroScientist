# Obstetric emergency readiness in rural emergency departments: a cross-sectional survey of birthing and non-birthing hospitals in Montana, USA

- **Authors:** Annie Glover, Carly Holman, Megan Nelson, Emma Piskolich
- **Journal:** BMJ Public Health
- **Year:** 2026
- **PMID:** 42851954
- **PMCID:** PMC13646923
- **DOI:** 10.1136/bmjph-2025-004491
- **Keywords:** Emergencies; Tertiary Prevention; Secondary Prevention; Maternal health; Pregnancy

## Abstract

Background: Rural emergency departments (EDs) increasingly serve as frontline providers of obstetric care due to widespread closures of labour and delivery (L&D) units. Building on a prior study that assessed hospital-level obstetric readiness using the WHO Emergency Obstetric Care (EmOC) Indicators, this study evaluates ED-specific preparedness for obstetric emergencies in a rural US state, with implications for similar settings globally.

Methods: A cross-sectional survey was conducted in Autumn 2024 involving ED medical and nursing staff from both birthing and non-birthing hospitals. The survey assessed hospital characteristics, staffing and nine domains related to emergency obstetric care. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise responses from 78 individuals across 45 EDs. Selected comparisons between birthing and non-birthing facilities were evaluated using Fisher’s exact tests.

Results: Most participating hospitals (87%) were in rural areas. Half of the EDs were in non-birthing facilities, many of which had previously had L&D units that closed. Key findings included inconsistent pregnancy screening and triage protocols, limited staff training and inadequate ED discharge and transport procedures. EDs frequently managed early pregnancy complications, postpartum emergencies and, in some cases, active labour and delivery.

Conclusions: Rural EDs function as critical safety nets but demonstrate important gaps in emergency preparedness, workforce readiness and transport systems. This assessment framework provides a practical approach for identifying facility-level vulnerabilities and informing targeted quality improvement, simulation training, referral-network strengthening and policy interventions. Readiness assessments such as this may help health systems improve preparedness for high-acuity, low frequency emergencies and promote equitable access to emergency care in rural and underserved communities.

## Full text

### WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

- Rural and remote communities worldwide face disproportionate barriers to maternal healthcare, including closures of labour and delivery (L&D) units and limited access to obstetric services. Emergency departments (EDs) often become the primary point of care for obstetric emergencies. These settings often lack the resources, training and infrastructure needed to deliver equitable and safe maternal care.

### WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

- This study reveals significant gaps in obstetric emergency preparedness across rural EDs, using a rural US state as an exemplifying case study. These gaps include inconsistent screening and triage protocols, limited staff education and inadequate transport and discharge procedures. It also highlights ED staff concerns about legal liability and the impact of shifting public policy related to reproductive health on their ability to provide care.

### HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

- This study demonstrates a practical framework for assessing obstetric emergency readiness in rural EDs. Findings can be used to identify facility-specific preparedness gaps, prioritise quality improvement activities, strengthen referral and transport systems and inform policies that support EDs in their role as obstetric safety nets.

## Introduction

After years of declining maternal mortality, global progress toward maternal health improvements has stagnated over the last decade.1 The US maternal health crisis is especially pronounced, with one of the highest maternal mortality rates among high-income nations. In this context, rural closures of labour and delivery (L&D) units and hospitals leave obstetric care to emergency departments (EDs) as the remaining provider, particularly in rural areas.2–5 Ensuring that rural EDs can manage obstetric emergencies safely is not just a clinical necessity—it’s a public health imperative rooted in addressing structural inequities in maternal care access.

While this study is situated in the USA, the challenges it addresses—geographic isolation, declining obstetric infrastructure and EDs serving as de facto maternity care providers—are shared by rural and remote communities globally.67 Previous research has applied global frameworks such as the WHO’s Emergency Obstetric Care indicators to assess structural readiness in rural US hospitals, underscoring the transferability of these challenges across contexts.89 While previous assessments have focused primarily on structural capacity, less is known about the operational processes that support obstetric emergency readiness in rural EDs, including screening, triage, transport, workforce preparedness and quality improvement engagement. The assessment presented here followed up Glover et al9 to improve care processes which may inform efforts to strengthen emergency maternal care in rural and low-resource settings globally.9

US Government responses at the federal level to the maternal health crisis include Perinatal Quality Collaboratives (PQCs), funded by the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which support evidence-based quality improvement (QI) in perinatal care. In parallel, the Alliance for Innovation on Maternal Health (AIM) programme—funded by the Health Services and Resources Administration (HRSA) with tools created by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG)—promotes standardised Patient Safety Bundles and resource kits. These initiatives primarily focus on inpatient L&D settings. However, in many rural communities, EDs are the first—and often only—point of contact for pregnant people experiencing complications or active labour. ACOG has called for stronger ED engagement in emergency obstetric care QI, providing obstetric triage guidelines, training, and recommendations for engagement with L&D units.1011 OB emergency preparedness ensures that patients receive timely and appropriate interventions and stabilisation prior to transfer to a higher level of obstetric care. Critical access hospitals (CAHs), a designation given to eligible rural hospitals in the USA by the Centres for Medicare and Medicaid (CMS), maintain no more than 25 inpatient beds but must provide 24/7 emergency care, putting them into a first and only responder position for many rural obstetric patients.12

The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labour Act (EMTALA), enacted in 1986 by the US Congress, mandates that all hospitals that participate in the federal Medicare programme, which finances healthcare for Americans age 65+, provide a medical screening examination to anyone seeking treatment, stabilise patients with emergency medical conditions and, if necessary, transfer them to higher level facilities regardless of insurance status or ability to pay.13 Using this threat of substantial financial loss, EMTALA prevents ‘patient dumping’ where hospitals refuse treatment due to lack of financial means to pay and reinforces the role of EDs in managing obstetric emergencies. As safety-net facilities that must provide a basic level of universal care, these hospitals are under increasing strain to provide emergency services to rural patients who face disproportionately high social vulnerability.14 From a public health perspective, these responsibilities position rural EDs as critical components of emergency obstetric preparedness systems, particularly in communities where local obstetric services have closed.

In recent decades, rural communities have faced a three-pronged threat to obstetric care access: there are fewer L&D units, EDs and rural hospitals generally now than there were thirty years ago.415 Hung et al identified financial constraints, workforce shortages, liability challenges, and lower patient volumes as key factors driving the decline in rural obstetric services.15 These closures force long distances for treatment and delivery and place rural communities far from urgent medical interventions during emergencies. People who live in maternity care deserts often have poorer health before pregnancy, receive less prenatal care, and experience higher rates of preterm birth.1617 Since 2010, over 130 US rural hospitals have closed.18 These closures often lead to increased travel distances for emergency care, resulting in poorer outcomes and lower care quality due to delays in timely intervention.

This study assessed ED readiness for obstetric emergencies within one of the most rural states in the USA. In rural settings, obstetric emergencies represent high-acuity, low-frequency events that depend on preparedness, coordination and clearly defined processes rather than routine clinical volume alone. As rural EDs increasingly serve as obstetric safety nets, understanding their readiness to recognise, stabilise, manage and transfer patients is essential for improving maternal health outcomes and strengthening health system preparedness.

## Methods

This cross-sectional survey was conducted as part of the Montana PQC initiative. The study assessed the provision of obstetric services in EDs across Montana, a geographically vast and sparsely populated US state. The survey was administered from 4 September 2024 to 15 October 2024.

Participants were identified through a contact list of hospitals with EDs provided by the state hospital association. The list included all birthing facilities in the state and most non-birthing facilities. For facilities without available email contact information, the study team made repeated attempts to identify an appropriate contact by telephone. A REDCap survey was distributed by email to ED medical and nursing staff, including ED directors, ED educators, directors of nursing, nursing staff and physicians. Survey invitations were followed by two rounds of telephone outreach to encourage participation. Survey respondents were eligible to enter a drawing for a US$100 gift card; individuals who wished to enter the drawing provided contact information on a separate form that was not linked to their survey responses to maintain anonymity.

The survey instrument incorporated elements from the AIM Obstetric Emergency Readiness Resource Kit, AIM Patient Safety Bundles and ACOG resources on obstetric emergencies in non-obstetric settings. Survey responses were collected using predefined categorical response options, except for selected open-ended numeric items used to assess the frequency of obstetric emergencies. Some questions also included an opportunity for open-ended comments. These responses were reviewed to provide contextual understanding of survey findings but were not subjected to formal qualitative analysis, content analysis or coding procedures and were not used to generate response categories reported in this manuscript. The survey underwent pilot testing by members of the study team and clinical advisors and received external review by one physician and four nurses. Feedback from clinical review and pilot testing was incorporated into revisions prior to distribution to improve clarity, contextual relevance and clinical accuracy. The full survey instrument is provided in online supplemental file 1. Surveys were emailed to all hospitals, and a research assistant followed up the email invitation with two rounds of phone calls.

The assessment measured hospital characteristics, staffing and nine domains related to emergency obstetric care: (1) Pregnancy Screening; (2) Triage; (3) Care Practices and Protocols; (4) ED Discharge; (5) Education; (6) Legal and Liability Issues; (7) Transport; (8) QI and L&D Engagement; and (9) Concerns and Priorities.

Participants also reported their facility’s trauma designation (Levels I–V), a commonly used classification of hospital emergency and trauma care capacity in the US trauma levels reflect available staffing, specialty services, equipment and transfer capabilities. Level I and II centres provide the highest levels of specialty and surgical care, Level III and IV centres provide stabilisation and selected specialty services and Level V facilities focus primarily on emergency evaluation, stabilisation and transfer.19 Because trauma designation is associated with hospital resources and emergency care capacity, it was collected as a descriptive facility characteristic.

For facility-level analyses, a single response was retained per facility. When multiple individuals from the same facility completed the survey, the response from the individual with the greatest organisational responsibility for ED policies and procedures was selected using a predefined hierarchy of roles. Leadership positions with administrative oversight were prioritised over program-specific or frontline roles. Additional responses from the same facility were excluded from facility-level analyses but retained for respondent-level analyses examining staff perspectives.

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and proportions, were used to summarise survey responses. Exact binomial 95% CIs were calculated for selected readiness indicators to provide information regarding the precision of key estimates. Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare selected readiness indicators between birthing and non-birthing facilities because of small cell sizes and the modest sample size. Inferential comparisons were limited to a small number of clinically prioritised outcomes, including pregnancy screening, triage protocols and maternal transport protocols (online supplemental table S1). ‘Don’t know’ responses were retained in descriptive tables but excluded from Fisher’s exact tests to allow construction of mutually exclusive 2×2 tables. Analyses were conducted using Stata 19.20

### Ethics approval

This study was reviewed and approved by the University of Montana Institutional Review Board (Protocol #IRB-FY2024-165). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants prior to their inclusion in the study. Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study.

## Results

Of 60 hospitals in the state, contact information was obtained for 56 facilities, including all 25 birthing hospitals and 31 of 35 non-birthing hospitals. Four non-birthing facilities could not be reached despite repeated telephone attempts. A total of 45 facilities participated in the assessment, representing 75% of invited facilities. Participation included 22 of 25 birthing hospitals (88%) and 23 of 31 non-birthing hospitals (74%), and one non-birthing Indian Health Service (IHS) hospital was represented in the sample. Seventy-eight individuals completed the survey. Facility-level analyses were conducted using one response per facility, resulting in a final facility-level analytic sample of 45 hospitals.

A description of the characteristics of the hospitals who responded to the survey is detailed in table 1. Most (87%) of hospitals that participated in this assessment are in rural (eg, rural, small-town core or micropolitan core) communities. Facilities from each of the five trauma levels were represented. Half of the responding EDs were in non-birthing facilities.

*[Table] Hospital characteristics*

Table 2 provides a summary of the frequency with which responding hospitals are managing obstetric emergencies in their ED. Most EDs (37/44, 84%) reported seeing at least one patient per month with an early pregnancy complication such as abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy. EDs in non-birthing facilities are managing active deliveries; 5 (22%) reported that 1–5 emergency deliveries presented to their ED in an average month. While most of these patients would be transferred to a facility with L&D if possible, 7 (30%) EDs in non-birthing hospitals reported that they managed a delivery in their ED in the last year. EDs also treat patients with postpartum emergencies. While EDs in a facility with L&D are more likely to treat postpartum emergencies, over two-thirds (71%) of all EDs reported they see these patients in an average month.

### Domains 1 and 2: pregnancy screening and triage

To better understand ED screening and triage practices, facilities described the extent to which they have established protocols (table 3). Just over half (53%) of facilities report that they conduct standardised screening for current and recent pregnancy in the ED. ED pregnancy screening is more common in birthing facilities, with 14 out of 22 (63%) implementing it compared with 43% of EDs in non-birthing facilities.

*[Table] Pregnancy screening and triage*

Of the 24 facilities that conduct pregnancy screening in the ED, 15 (63%) report that this is done routinely to a great or a very great extent. Facilities were more likely to report having written protocols for OB patients over 20 weeks’ gestation, with 73% stating they have this compared with just 56% reporting a protocol for patients less than 20 weeks’ gestation with an OB emergency. Overall, EDs in birthing facilities were more likely to have these written protocols than EDs in non-birthing facilities. Selected comparisons between birthing and non-birthing facilities are presented in online supplemental table S1. Birthing facilities were significantly more likely to report written triage protocols for patients less than 20 weeks’ gestation (p=0.004) and greater than 20 weeks’ gestation (p=0.034), whereas differences in standardised pregnancy screening were not statistically significant (p=0.364).

### Domains 3 and 4: care practices and ED discharge

Respondents reported a range of protocols, policies, and procedures in place in the ED for obstetric emergency care, with percentages presented as birthing versus non-birthing facilities. Massive transfusion protocols were reported by 94.7% of birthing facilities compared with 38.9% of non-birthing facilities, while sepsis assessment tools (68.4% vs 66.7%), telemetry monitoring (68.4% vs 66.7%), and magnesium sulfate administration (52.6% vs 55.6%) were reported at similar rates across facility types. Most birthing facilities (79.0%) had protocols for suicide risk assessment, compared with non-birthing facilities (38.9%). In contrast, fewer facilities reported protocols for other psychosocial issues, such as intimate partner violence (36.8% vs 22.2%), substance use disorder (SUD) (31.6% vs 22.2%), perinatal mental health (21.1% vs 0%), and pain protocols for patients with SUD (10.5% vs 0%). Only 47.4% of birthing facilities and 27.8% of non-birthing facilities reported having a hypertension treatment algorithm, and preeclampsia and eclampsia algorithms were reported by 31.6% and 33.3%, respectively. Checklists for management of hypertension in pregnancy and postpartum were less common overall (26.3% vs 16.7%). Not many facilities had an ED Obstetric Rapid Response Team, 31.6% of birthing facilities, and 5.6% of non-birthing facilities. Few EDs report using a discharge checklist for pregnant and postpartum patients, with 14.3% of birthing facilities and 4.4% of non-birthing facilities doing so.

### Domain 5: ED staff education

The survey asked participants to report on their facility’s current obstetric care education practices in EDs, with only 28.6% of birthing facility EDs and 52.2% of non-birthing facility EDs reporting staff education in OB. More EDs (57.1% vs 69.6%) reported having simulation activities to maintain preparedness for effective recognition and response to obstetric emergencies.

### Domain 6: legal and liability issues related to EMTALA

Respondents were asked to provide their perspectives on the dynamic legal environment in which EDs operate, specifically related to upholding EMTALA. Given the individual perspectives offered, results to this question are reported for all respondents, with some facilities represented by multiple individuals in the sample. Most respondents (69%) do have at least some concerns (eg, slightly, somewhat, moderately, or extremely concerned) about the legal risk of upholding EMTALA amidst changing public policy restricting the type of life-saving care (ie, pregnancy loss and termination) EDs may provide in OB emergencies. Approximately a third of respondents, (30.8%) reported that they were “not at all concerned” about legal risks associated with upholding EMTALA in the context of emergency obstetric care and shifting state and federal reproductive healthcare policies, with similar proportions among birthing (33.3%) and non-birthing (26.9%) facilities.

### Domain 7: transport

The assessment aimed to evaluate the strength and standardisation of regional referral networks and transport procedures. Table 4, below, details the extent to which responding EDs have written transfer protocols or policies for obstetric patients and neonates. Overall, just a third (32%) of EDs report having written transport protocols for obstetric patients; fewer (18%) for neonates. In non-birthing facility EDs, just 26% reported having written transport protocols for obstetric patients, and just 13% for neonates. Differences in the presence of written maternal transport protocols between birthing and non-birthing facilities were not statistically significant (p=0.168; online supplemental table S1).

### Domain 8: quality improvement and labour and delivery engagement

EDs located in birthing hospitals were surveyed about their involvement implementing AIM Patient Safety Bundles since the PQC has focused its QI exclusively in L&D units to date. Of the 40 individuals responding from birthing facilities, 15% reported active engagement with the PQC, 45% reported no engagement, and 40% were unsure if ED staff had participated. ED staff were also asked about their collaboration with L&D teams within their facilities. Responses ranged from no interaction or cross-training to strong cross-departmental engagement, indicating a broad spectrum of collaboration across hospitals.

### Domain 9: concerns and priorities

The surveys also assessed the experience and perceptions of staff regarding their roles and responsibilities in the ED setting, which is summarised in table 5. Just 8% of respondents from birthing facility EDs and no respondents from non-birthing facility EDs responded that they felt well-prepared to manage obstetric emergencies. Likewise, less than 10% of all respondents reported that these cases are too infrequent to warrant additional attention. ED staff most often reported they need more support specifically in emergency labour/birth (77%), followed by eclampsia (56%), shoulder dystocia (53%) and postpartum complications (47%). Some respondents also reported needing more support for early pregnancy complications (41%) and perinatal mental health issues (39%). Most (58%) respondents working in non-birthing facility EDs responded ‘Honestly, everything’ when asked what part of the OB patient walking into the ED is the most daunting, scary, or unsettling to the team. Just 18% of respondents in birthing facility EDs expressed the same degree of general anxiety about these emergencies, with a plurality of 32% reporting that precipitous birth was the situation that was most daunting.

## Discussion

Examining health systems from the vantage point of rural communities reveals an especially stark reality. Rurality is associated with higher rates of social vulnerability, such as poverty, as well as higher rates of pregnancy complications and comorbid conditions. These health disparities are compounded by the inherent resource constraints and remoteness of rural communities. Overall, this assessment provided an illustrative example of broader trends in the perinatal care needs of rural women and experiences of rural hospitals. Importantly, these challenges are not unique to the US rural and remote communities face similar barriers to emergency obstetric care.67 The findings from this study may be applicable to other settings where emergency departments serve as de facto maternity care providers.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample represents a convenience sample of facilities that opted to participate in the assessment, and therefore findings may be subject to selection bias. We cannot make population-level estimates or inferences to the full population of hospitals. Second, data were self-reported and may not fully reflect actual clinical practice, implementation of protocols, or organisational readiness. Third, the survey instrument was developed for quality improvement purposes using existing obstetric emergency readiness resources and expert review, but it was not formally validated, which may affect measurement consistency across respondents. For facilities with multiple respondents, facility-level analyses relied on the most senior respondent, who was expected to have the greatest knowledge of departmental policies and procedures; however, this approach may not capture variation in perspectives among staff within the same facility. In addition, some survey items relied on subjective interpretation by respondents, particularly questions regarding the extent to which practices were routinely implemented. Because this study used a cross-sectional design, it cannot assess changes in readiness over time or determine whether identified gaps are associated with patient outcomes. Finally, the study was conducted in a single US state, and meaningful differences in healthcare financing, geography, demographics, and policy environments exist between states and countries. Rural communities often share more in common with other rural communities across national borders than they have in common with urban communities in their own regions and countries. As such, while the specific findings may not be directly generalisable to all settings, the structural and procedural gaps identified here may inform efforts to strengthen emergency obstetric care in rural and low-resource settings internationally.

Our assessment highlights the ongoing reliance on EDs as obstetric safety nets and demonstrates how readiness assessments can identify actionable vulnerabilities in emergency obstetric preparedness. Specifically, the results of this study highlight several ways facilities can improve obstetric emergency preparedness in their EDs:

- Pregnancy screening and triage: The variability in standardised pregnancy screening practices across facilities indicates a need for universal protocols. Implementing consistent screening processes can ensure that all pregnant and postpartum patients are identified early, allowing for timely interventions. This is particularly important in rural EDs where referral and transfer already introduce delays in care, emphasising the need to expedite recognition and triage.

- Care practices and protocols: The presence of comprehensive care protocols in some facilities, but not others, highlights a gap in preparedness. Standardised protocols can improve the consistency and therefore quality of care provided. Protocols must be adapted to the context, setting, and resources of rural EDs.

- ED discharge: The lack of discharge checklists for pregnant and postpartum patients is another gap. Discharge checklists can ensure patients receive appropriate follow-up care and reduce the risk of complications after leaving the ED, which is especially crucial in rural communities where follow-up care may be a significant travel burden for patients.

- ED staff education: Continuous education programmes, including in situ simulation, can enhance staff preparedness and confidence, particularly for lower-volume EDs who are not seeing regular obstetric patients.

- Legal and liability issues related to EMTALA: Clinical skills and preparedness must be developed within the relevant policy environment. Staff need to understand not only the clinical and technical aspects of providing care but also the legal framework governing their actions. Concerns about legal risks and compliance with EMTALA amidst changing US reproductive health laws underscore the importance of clear guidelines. While EMTALA is specific to the USA, the broader principle of legal clarity in emergency reproductive care is relevant to international policy environments. Countries with similar mandates or gaps in emergency care legislation may benefit from comparative research and policy guidance.

- Transport protocols: The need for standardised transport protocols is critical in rural EDs, where timely transfers to higher-level care facilities can be lifesaving. Strengthening regional referral networks and ensuring that all facilities have clear, written protocols can speed up transfers and improve trust between referring and receiving facilities.

- Quality improvement initiatives: Engaging EDs in QI initiatives can enhance care quality in the ED and expedite transfer to higher levels of care and L&D. These initiatives should be adapted for the ED setting where providers have less time and different objectives for patients. Strengthened collaboration between EDs and L&D units can be achieved through cross-training, joint emergency drills, and shared policies. Readiness assessments such as the one presented here can serve as a foundation for targeted implementation efforts by helping facilities identify specific gaps in protocols, training, transport systems, and interdepartmental coordination. Assessment findings can be used to prioritise simulation-based training, develop contextually appropriate quality improvement interventions, and support adoption of clinical decision-support tools or technology-enabled protocols designed to improve adherence to evidence-based obstetric emergency practices in low-volume rural settings.

- ED staff concerns and priorities: Ongoing engagement with ED staff on their training and resource needs will help to focus limited time and build confidence and expertise in handling obstetric emergencies.

The research community and policymakers also have roles to play in improving obstetric emergency preparedness. Future research should focus on evaluating and developing interventions that address the specific challenges rural EDs describe, such as the effectiveness of QI patient safety bundles adapted to the rural ED setting. Future research should also examine whether identified readiness gaps are associated with adverse events, near misses, transfer delays, or other patient safety outcomes. Additional work is needed to characterise transport times, transfer delays, and travel distances in rural settings and evaluate how these factors relate to the presence of formal transport protocols and referral networks. Assessing the impact of changing public policies on emergency obstetric care will also help to improve these policies and prevent harm. Exploring the unique contextual factors that influence healthcare delivery in rural EDs, such as geographic isolation, decreasing volume, resource limitations, and social vulnerabilities, will aid in developing tailored interventions. Research on effective models of interdisciplinary collaboration and QI implementation between EDs and L&D will also enhance care coordination.

Although this assessment was conducted in the USA, several of the domains evaluated reflect core public health responsibilities related to emergency preparedness and quality assurance. Pregnancy screening and triage (Domains 1–2), workforce education (Domain 5), transport systems (Domain 7) and quality improvement activities (Domain 8) are mechanisms through which health systems maintain readiness to respond to obstetric emergencies. These functions are particularly important for high-acuity, low-frequency events, where safe care depends on preparedness, coordination and ongoing quality improvement rather than routine clinical volume alone. For this reason, the assessment domains examined here are broadly relevant across rural and low-resource settings globally, even when the specific policies, governance structures and healthcare delivery systems differ.

Policymakers should consider the unique obstetric safety net positionality of rural EDs and develop supportive policies that enhance their capacity. This includes allocating funding for transport centres, emergency preparedness and simulation programmes that provide connections between regional referral networks. It also includes supporting stable reimbursement and financing to ensure that hospitals can keep their doors open and maintain consistent staffing and access to essential medicines and commodities.

As this study illustrates, rural facilities face a unique context, including limited equipment, staffing and isolation, that requires design and implementation modifications prior to transferring an evidence-informed strategy from a larger or more urban setting to a rural ED. By meeting these clinical, research and policy calls for action, health systems worldwide can better support rural hospitals in their efforts to improve maternal health outcomes and to ensure that all families, regardless of their location, have access to high-quality emergency services.
