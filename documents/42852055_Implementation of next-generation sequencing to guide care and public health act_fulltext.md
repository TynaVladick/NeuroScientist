# Implementation of next-generation sequencing to guide care and public health action in settings with high burden of rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis: 32 stakeholder consensus statements generated using Delphi principles

- **Authors:** Anzaan Dippenaar, Vincent Rennie, Rubeshan Perumal, Christoph Lange, Michael L Rich, Amanzhan Abubakirov, Ashna Ashesh, Emilyn Costa Conceicao, Helen Cox, Miguel de Diego Fuertes, Lynette Keneilwe Eyde, Gunar Günther, Melanie Grobbelaar, Tim H Heupink, Pauline Howell, Farzana Ismail, Nabila Ismail, Yousra Kherabi, Marisa Klopper, Yulene Kock, Mikanda K Kunda, Johannes Loubser, Sneha Maru, Brendon Mann, Stephanus Theron Malherbe, Erika Mohr-Holland, Claudia Mutaquiha, Felex Ndebele, Tendai Nkomo, Lawrence Oyewusi, Anja Reuter, Assel Stambekova, Felicia Bernita Hunter, Zarir Udwadia, Jennifer Furin, Tulio de Oliveira, Robin Mark Warren, Annelise Van Rie

## Abstract

Background: Comprehensive knowledge of resistance to the novel all-oral regimens for treatment of rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) is important, but challenging variant detection by next-generation sequencing (NGS) in combination with variant interpretation software can diagnose resistance early. We aimed to generate a consensus on strategic issues for the implementation of NGS for routine RR-TB care.

Methods: We employed a stakeholder consensus process informed by Delphi principles. First, stakeholders participated in an online survey to provide feedback on statements using a 3-point Likert scale and free text. Responses were then summarised and follow-up discussion fora with stakeholders were held to revise statements until a consensus was reached.

Findings: Of the 116 invited stakeholders, 58 participated, representing RR-TB research (n=28), clinical care (n=11), diagnosis (n=5), RR-TB survivors and advocacy communities (n=5) from 14 countries in five of the six WHO regions. A consensus was rapidly reached for most statements with limited editorial changes needed. The debate mostly focused on statements covering performing sequencing at centralised facilities, acting on variants without statistical association with resistance in the WHO catalogue of mutations, strengthening WHO-endorsed regimens and the need for informed consent for the use of NGS for care and surveillance.

Interpretation: The consensus-building process grounded in real-world experience from a diverse group of stakeholders generated 32 consensus statements covering five strategic domains. The blueprint generated is not a definite prescriptive framework but a document that can support policymakers when developing country-specific policies for NGS-guided precision medicine and RR-TB care.

## Full text

### WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

- This study fills a critical gap by creating a policy blueprint for NGS implementation tailored to countries with a high burden of RR-TB. Using South Africa’s Tuberculosis Recovery Plan as a structural policy framework, the study employed an iterative, multi-stakeholder survey and consensus process to derive 32 consensus statements. Through engagement with a wide range of diverse global stakeholders, the resulting blueprint balances scientific evidence with real-world feasibility.

### HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

- As the use of bedaquiline-containing regimens expands globally and the burden of bedaquiline-resistant tuberculosis increases, NGS will likely become a critical tool in the fight against RR-TB. Policymakers in high-burden settings will be able to use the consensus statements as a starting point to develop country-specific implementation strategies to implement NGS at scale to improve RR-TB care, prevention and surveillance.

## Introduction

The global burden of rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) remains high and poor RR-TB treatment success rates (68%) persist despite the roll-out of rapid molecular diagnostics and the introduction of short all-oral RR-TB regimens.1 Another worrying observation is the increasing burden of bedaquiline-resistant tuberculosis among people with RR-TB, which is already 6% in South Africa and 14% in Mozambique.2 In Moldova, 40% of individuals failing RR-TB treatment were resistant to bedaquiline, 38% to linezolid and 75% to fluoroquinolones.3 The rapid rise and transmission of expanded tuberculosis, defined as resistance to rifampicin and any one of bedaquiline, linezolid, clofazimine, delamanid or pretomanid,4 highlights the need for mitigating strategies and the urgency of novel diagnostics.5

The endorsement of Xpert MTB/XDR by the WHO in 2022 facilitated rapid detection of resistance to isoniazid, fluoroquinolones, ethionamide and second-line injectables but not bedaquiline, delamanid, pretomanid, linezolid or clofazimine. Currently, only next-generation sequencing (NGS) and phenotypic drug susceptibility tests (pDST) can detect resistance to these drugs.67 While most low and middle-income countries have in-country sequencing capacity,8 many operational and health-system barriers hamper NGS integration into routine RR-TB care, including sample logistics, supply chain challenges, technical complexity, as well as infrastructural and human resource capacity for data analysis and interpretation.9 The WHO already incorporated NGS into its global policy frameworks by recommending NGS for drug resistance surveillance, endorsing targeted NGS as a diagnostic tool, and publishing the WHO Catalogue of mutations in Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb).610 In 2018, a technical guide focused on sequencing, bioinformatics and NGS data workflows.11 In 2023, an implementation manual contained a roadmap for NGS implementation in drug resistance surveillance systems.10 The 2024 WHO operational handbook (module 3: diagnosis) includes implementation guidance for new diagnostics with a focus on defining their intended use, assay placement, developing a costed implementation plan and building infrastructure (instruments, facilities and human resources).12 Guidance is also provided for NGS integration in diagnostic RR-TB cascades and treatment modifications based on NGS results.

We aimed to complement the existing WHO guidance by developing consensus statements to assist policy makers in overcoming key implementation challenges when developing country-level frameworks for NGS-guided patient management in high RR-TB burden settings.

## Methods

### Survey instrument development

The conceptual framework for the initial online survey was the five strategic pillars of the South African Tuberculosis Recovery Plan 3.0 (2024–2025) adapted to expanded resistance: (1) communicate and advocate, (2) find and link, (3) treat and retain, (4) prevent and prepare and (5) monitor and assess13 (figure 1). Based on experiences and themes identified in the literature, a team of clinicians and laboratory scientists developed 30 initial statements (online supplemental materials).

*[Figure] Five strategic pillars and targets for addressing expanded resistance (defined as resistance to rifampicin and any one of bedaquiline, linezolid, clofazimine, delamanid or pretomanid),4adapted from South Africa’s National TB Recovery Plan’s strategic pillars and targets for addressing bedaquiline-resistant tuberculosis.13*

### Stakeholders

Purposive sampling was employed to ensure diverse representation of stakeholders including laboratory, clinical and public health researchers, clinical experts, laboratory staff involved in routine drug susceptibility testing, people with lived experience of tuberculosis and patient advocates. In round 1, stakeholders were recruited through three research networks active in South Africa: the Tuberculosis Omics Research consortium, the Sequencing Mycobacteria and Algorithm guided Rifampicin resistant Tuberculosis Treatment trial team and the GenPath Africa Precision Action for RR-TB study team. In round 2, members of the global Building Experience Treating Tuberculosis with Expanded Resistance (BETTER) project were invited.4

### Data collection and analysis

We implemented a modified Delphi-informed consensus approach. During round 1, stakeholders were asked to respond to each statement using a three-point Likert scale (Agree, Disagree and Unsure), and if they ‘Disagree’, elaborate on their choice or suggest modifications. The Likert scale responses were summarised to determine the level of agreement for each statement. A statement was classified as having reached a consensus when ≥80% of stakeholders agreed with the statement. Open-ended responses were reviewed by two investigators to identify reasons for disagreement and suggestions for improvement. Based on these findings, an alternative statement was drafted. Next, stakeholders were invited to participate in an online meeting to discuss the findings and contribute to rewording statements for which a consensus had not yet been reached. During the meeting, the original and alternative statements were presented and stakeholders were invited to voice their concerns and suggestions for improvement. An iterative approach was taken, with voting using Mentimeter (https://www.mentimeter.com) for interactive audience engagement until ≥80% of stakeholders present agreed. All online meetings were audio-recorded (with stakeholder consent) and notes were taken by the study team to verify the conclusions made.

During round 2, stakeholders were asked to respond to the statements resulting from round 1. At the end of round 2, another online meeting was organised to reach the final set of consensus statements.

### Patient and public involvement

Patients were involved, but the public was not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

### Ethics

The study was approved by the University of Antwerp Ethics Committee (ID 8177). All participants gave informed consent and were offered co-authorship, acknowledgement or to stay anonymous.

## Results

### Survey responses

A total of 116 stakeholders (62 for round 1 and 54 for round 2) were invited. Nine gave informed consent but did not provide any answers, and one declared a conflict of interest. In total, 59 (50%) individuals (35 in round 1 and 24 in round 2) completed the survey. Nearly half (28) were primarily involved in research, including laboratory research (14), clinical research (11) and public health research (3). Others worked in clinical management (13), within the tuberculosis programme (8), in a routine diagnostic laboratory (5), or were patient advocates or people with lived experience of RR-TB (5). Stakeholders represented a broad geographic distribution, spanning 14 countries across five of the six WHO regions (online supplemental materials).

### Stakeholder survey feedback

In round 1, high agreement (≥80%) was achieved for 25 of the 30 (83%) initial statements, 4 (13%) reached moderate (65–79%) agreement and only 1 (3%) had low agreement (34%) (online supplemental materials). At the end of round 1, one of the 30 statements was split into two for enhanced clarity, resulting in 31 statements presented to stakeholders in round 2 (online supplemental table 2). In round 2, high agreement (≥80%) was achieved for 24 of 31 (77%) statements, 5 (16%) had moderate (65–79%) agreement, and 2 (6%) had low (<65%) agreement (online supplemental materials).

### Stakeholder discussion fora

During feedback meetings, stakeholders debated extensively on whether NGS placement should be centralised (statement 2.6), the need for informed consent for NGS for care (statement 3.2) and/or surveillance applications (statement 5.1), the clinical interpretation of novel variants and variants of uncertain clinical significance (statements 2.8, 2.9, 3.3, 3.5), and the option to strengthen the BPaL (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid) regimen for robust treatment of fluoroquinolone-resistant RR-TB (statement 4.1).

Initially, the statement on NGS placement strongly favoured a centralised approach by stating that ‘performing NGS should be centralised at specialised sequencing centres’. Stakeholders in favour of centralisation discussed the human and laboratory infrastructure requirements, the need for flexible batch sizes when performing sequencing in real time and the challenges of expiring sequencing reagents due to long and unreliable supply chains, customs delays and short shelf life. The main disadvantage raised was the possible increase in turn-around time associated with a centralised approach. Based on feedback after round 1, the statement was changed to ‘Performing NGS should be centralised (at national or provincial level) and performed at specialised, accredited sequencing centres to ensure quality and efficiency, while balancing costs and turn-around time’. Discussions in round 2 highlighted that the cost to patients and equity in access cannot be ignored, especially given the large differences between countries in sample transport logistics. To capture these viewpoints, the statement was revised to emphasise quality and patient access, and the need to balance cost and turn-around time.

Another topic of intense discussion was the need for informed consent when performing NGS for diagnostic purposes. The initial statements were that ‘patients should be informed that NGS will be performed on their sample to help decide the optimal treatment regimen for their TB. Communication must be simple and result in shared decision making’ and ‘Patients should have the option to opt-out of the use of NGS results for treatment regimen decision making’. Some stakeholders strongly believed that consent for NGS is required. Several felt that obtaining consent was too challenging as part of routine care. Others believed it is not productive to create exceptionalism for NGS as patients are currently not informed about which tests are performed (smear microscopy, culture, Xpert MTB/RIF, line probe assay, pDST etc). Some opposed the possibility of opt-out because this may disadvantage patients when their decision is made based on misinformation, such as the potential confusion about human or Mtb DNA sequencing. An opt-out option was also considered logistically challenging when NGS is integrated as a reflex test in the standard diagnostic algorithm. After round 1, the opt-out part of the statement was deleted and the statement was changed to ‘It is not necessary to inform the patient which type of test will be performed on their sample. This decision lies with the National TB programme which has the best interest of all patients’. The revised statement received poor agreement in round two and the discussion focused on the patients’ right-to-know principle. Most agreed that patients should be made aware that NGS is performed on their clinical sample but also acknowledged the real-world challenges in communicating this when NGS is integrated in a diagnostic algorithm. The final consensus statement achieved a balance between the different views by stating that patients should be made aware that drug resistance tests will be performed on their sample but that the decision which test(s) to perform lies with the National TB programme. It was also emphasised that communication must occur in simple and accessible language.

In contrast to NGS for care, informing the patient on the use of NGS for surveillance led to less discussion. Most stakeholders leaned toward a requirement for informed consent in this context and believed that anonymised contextual patient data should be made available for answering emerging research questions. Data sharing should follow a predefined process that ensures responsible access.

Another topic of discussion related to the interpretation of variants. When a variant detected is listed in the WHO catalogue as associated with resistance, most stakeholders agreed that a patient does not need to be informed whether this knowledge arises from NGS or another test. Challenges however arise when novel variants or variants classified as ‘uncertain significance’ in the WHO catalogue are detected by NGS. For the latter, stakeholders agreed that the observed frequency of a variant in resistant isolates, as reported in the WHO catalogue, can offer supportive evidence for clinical decision-making. There was also support to infer resistance using in silico methods, such as protein structure modelling, for which there is already some evidence for drugs such as rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and bedaquiline.14–17 Since the use of computational protein structure modelling to predict the impact of a variant on the protein function is a novel field, there was concern regarding its use in absence of clinical validation studies. A consensus emerged that classification based on protein structure modelling should be reviewed by experts before their use to guide treatment and that this should be coupled with pDST. The uncertainty in variant interpretation must be transparently communicated to the healthcare worker and should lead to shared healthcare worker-patient decision making on treatment selection.

A final topic of extensive discussion was how to strengthen the BPaL regimen for fluoroquinolone-resistant RR-TB. While many stakeholders did not express major concerns about the adequacy of the WHO recommendation for the BPaL regimen, evidence from the endTB-Q trial suggests that, in patients with fluoroquinolone-resistant RR-TB and extensive lung disease, treatment may need to be strengthened or individualised.18 Based on these findings, a consensus was reached that for fluoroquinolone-resistant strains susceptible to bedaquiline, clofazimine, pretomanid and linezolid, the regimen should be strengthened with clofazimine in mild non-severe pulmonary disease. In cases of severe or extensive fluoroquinolone-resistant RR-TB, individualised treatment guided by comprehensive DST data may be required.

### Visual summary

To support NGS implementation in line with the 32 consensus statements, we created a visual diagram (figure 2). Key elements are that NGS should be performed as a reflex test following a diagnosis of RR-TB or when not responding well to RR-TB treatment. Routine collection of two samples, one for a rapid nucleic acid test for detection of RR-TB and another for NGS if rifampicin resistance is detected, is an approach that can maximise NGS coverage.19 Targeted NGS (tNGS) can be performed directly on decontaminated sputum while whole genome sequencing (WGS) still requires a culture step. This may change in future when commercial assays for culture-free WGS become available for routine diagnostic use.20

*[Figure] Visual blueprint for leveraging NGS diagnostic assays to reduce the burden of drug-resistant tuberculosis and improve the care of persons suffering from drug-resistant tuberculosis*

The priority application of NGS should be precision medicine, where treatment regimens are tailored based on the comprehensive genomic drug resistance profile. Variant interpretation should be guided by the most recent version of the WHO catalogue of mutations, complemented by other methods when variants are listed as of ‘uncertain significance’ or when a novel variant is observed. The comprehensive genomic DST (gDST) is ideally reviewed by qualified laboratory staff before it is used for patient management.

Targeted NGS and WGS results also enable precision preventive therapy by selecting the optimal drugs based on the gDST profile of the index case. In settings where human resources for post-exposure management are limited, contact tracing could be prioritised to contacts of index cases with expanded resistance.

For precision public health, both tNGS or WGS can assist in detecting recent and ongoing transmission of Mtb strains with expanded resistance in near real-time by identifying clonal spread of specific drug resistance variants. In addition, WGS can empower precision public health by accurate monitoring of circulating Mtb strains which, when complemented with epidemiological investigations, can identify transmission chains and superspreading events.

## Discussion

NGS is an exciting and rapidly evolving field. Routine implementation of NGS technologies could improve RR-TB care by tailoring treatment based on the Mtb strain’s genetic profile and improve RR-TB control by precision public health actions. Reductions in cost, increased understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations and availability of end-to-end platforms for NGS-guided care have created opportunities for global NGS implementation. Through a participatory process with stakeholders, a blueprint containing 32 consensus statements covering five strategic policy pillars was developed to guide the implementation of NGS for RR-TB care and management.

While agreement was easily reached for most statements, stakeholders debated extensively on (a) the preference for centralised NGS, (b) the need for informed consent, (c) the clinical interpretation of variants not classified in the WHO catalogue as statistically (not) associated with drug resistance, and (d) the recommendation of treatment regimens that are not explicitly endorsed by the WHO.

Optimal placement of new diagnostic assays must consider a range of factors including demand, which is directly related to the RR-TB burden. Care should be taken in matching laboratory requirements with existing infrastructure and staff capacity.21 Centralising NGS at provincial or national reference laboratories can significantly reduce cost by increasing throughput and adapting sequencing instruments to batch sizes.22 Use of specialised sequencing centres could further improve efficiency by broadening the range of pathogens sequenced beyond Mtb.1223 Centralising NGS also optimises logistics, reducing the risk that reagents expire before their use.9 While reducing the cost per sample is important, cost must be balanced with turn-around time, cost to patients and equity in access. Centralisation of NGS may disadvantage patients in rural areas, especially when efficient sample transport services are lacking.24 The consensus statement therefore emphasised that decisions on NGS infrastructure placement should ensure NGS quality and balance cost, turn-around time and patient equity. It is important to consider that achieving these goals will also require capacity building of the healthcare workforce training and supply chain resilience.

Another topic that led to intense discussions was the need for informed consent. Although this issue is equally relevant for other diagnostic tests such as Xpert or phenotypic DST for which consent is not required. NGS tests however present new challenges given their association with big data, use of electronic databases and the uncertainties involved in NGS data interpretation.25 A consensus was reached that obtaining informed consent for NGS may be too challenging when NGS is performed as part of a routine diagnostic algorithm, especially in high burden settings where providers may lack the necessary time and training to correctly obtain patient consent for NGS. An opt-out option was rejected by most stakeholders due to the risk that this could result in reduced patient benefit. For example, health misinformation via social media can negatively affect individuals’ decisions, which can then lead to poor health outcomes.26 When performing consent, the communication to patients should be standardised with simplified language so that patients are not lost in communication. Most stakeholders believed a patient does not need to be informed on the type of assay that detected drug resistance, especially when there is certainty on the interpretation of the NGS result. Some stakeholders highlighted that the healthcare worker-patient relationship has changed, and the patient’s role has shifted from a passive role to one in which they are increasingly involved in decision-making related to their medical care. A final consensus was reached that, under the right-to-know principle,27 patients should be informed in accessible language that drug-resistance testing is performed on their sample but that the decision which test(s) to perform lies with the National Tuberculosis Programme. Communication materials about NGS should be developed and made freely available to ensure patients understand the purpose and process of NGS.

Informing the patient is challenging when variants are novel or classified as ‘uncertain significance’ in the WHO catalogue. Stakeholders agreed that the observed frequency of the variant in resistant isolates—as reported in the WHO database—can offer supportive evidence for clinical decision-making. There was also some support to use computational protein structure modelling to predict the impact of a variant on the protein function and thus infer resistance, for which there is already some evidence for isoniazid,16 pyrazinamide14 and bedaquiline.15 Since this is a novel field, there was concern about the use in absence of clinical validation studies. A consensus emerged that such findings should be reviewed by an expert and coupled with pDST. Any uncertainty in variant interpretation must be transparently communicated to the healthcare worker and should lead to shared healthcare worker-patient decision making. Clear, step-by-step clinical guidance algorithms should be developed to support clinicians when interpreting complex NGS results.

NGS for data-driven monitoring of the spread of hard-to-treat TB and to guide targeted public health interventions also raises new challenges. For example, the construction of granular transmission networks with case-to-case directionality poses ethical challenges such as risk to personal privacy or stigmatisation and blame for transmission.2829 Even though the ‘harm principle’ of public health ethics allows public health authorities to perform disease surveillance or contact tracing activities without the consent of individuals or groups involved, most stakeholders leaned toward a requirement for informed consent to use NGS for public health purposes. When obtaining consent, communication must be simple and the patient must be made aware that NGS tests analyse DNA of the Mtb strain, not the patient’s DNA. Anonymised contextual patient data should be available for answering emerging research questions, following a pre-defined process that ensures responsible data sharing and data security.

A few limitations should be noted. First, stakeholders were purposefully selected, based on their membership of different groups active in RR-TB care and management. The variability in NGS experience between regions resulted in an imbalance in representation of the different WHO regions. Second, while the iterative survey and discussion process drew on key principles of the consensus-building approach of the Delphi method, it was intentionally more flexible and dialogic to ensure inclusivity and transparency.30 Unlike the Delphi approach, which typically relies on multiple rounds of anonymous rating, we combined structured online surveys with facilitated group discussions to enable clarification of divergent viewpoints and co-development of revised statements. Finally, routine use of NGS is only recently becoming a reality in low- and middle-income countries. The statements thus represent expert consensus rather than evidence-based guidelines, should not be considered as definitive, and will need to be revised as new evidence emerges and experience of implementing NGS for RR-TB in these settings grows.

In conclusion, NGS technologies hold promise to improve RR-TB care and could help reduce transmission of drug-resistant Mtb strains. The uptake of NGS into routine diagnostic algorithms will facilitate the impact of NGS. We developed guiding consensus statements through a participatory process with a wide range of stakeholders from around the globe to provide a preliminary framework for feasible, practical and patient-centred NGS implementation. Future research should conduct operational research to evaluate the impact of routine NGS when implemented as a component of comprehensive RR-TB care.
