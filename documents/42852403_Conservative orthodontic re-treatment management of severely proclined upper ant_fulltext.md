# Conservative orthodontic re-treatment management of severely proclined upper anterior teeth with root resorption

## Abstract

This case report presents conservative orthodontic re-treatment management for an adolescent patient with severe maxillary incisor proclination and advanced external apical root resorption. This treatment was proposed to improve incisor inclination and overjet while minimizing further biological risk and preserving long-term tooth vitality. A 13-year-old female patient with a history of prior orthodontic treatment presented with excessive maxillary incisor flaring, increased overjet, and radiographic evidence of moderate-to-severe external apical root resorption. Comprehensive clinical and radiographic examinations revealed shortened roots with open apices, though periodontal support and pulpal vitality were preserved. Treatment planning focused on minimizing additional root resorption through conservative biomechanics. Space was gained by extracting the maxillary second premolars, allowing controlled retraction of the maxillary incisors using light orthodontic forces. Periodontal health and pulpal vitality were closely monitored throughout the treatment. Significant improvements in maxillary incisor inclination, a reduction of overjet, and enhanced facial harmony were noted. Periodontal probing depths remained within normal limits, no pathological tooth mobility was observed, and pulpal vitality tests remained positive. Although root shortening persisted radiographically, no progression of root resorption was detected during treatment. Orthodontic re-treatment in adolescents with severe external apical root resorption can be successfully performed using conservative biomechanics and careful force control, achieving functional and esthetic improvements while preserving periodontal health and tooth vitality.

## Introduction

Orthodontic tooth movement is a biologically mediated process that requires a delicate balance between applied mechanical forces and the adaptive capacity of the periodontal tissues.[1] When this balance is disrupted, adverse sequelae such as excessive proclination, periodontal breakdown, tooth mobility, and root resorption may occur, particularly in cases involving inadequately controlled orthodontic mechanics.[2] When available arch space is inadequate, orthodontic treatment often requires compensatory tooth movements, which may increase the risk of biological complications involving periodontal tissues and dental roots.[3] These complications pose a significant clinical challenge, especially in adolescent patients, where long-term tooth preservation is preferred, as it directly impacts the self-perception and well-being of the individual.[4]

Severe proclination of the anterior teeth has been associated with increased stress on the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, potentially resulting in attachment loss, increased tooth mobility, compromised vascularization, and reduced periodontal support.[5] Furthermore, orthodontically induced inflammatory root resorption remains one of the most unpredictable and irreversible complications of orthodontic treatment, with severity influenced by force magnitude, treatment duration, individual susceptibility, and root morphology.[67] Incisor flaring following previous orthodontic treatment represents a particularly complex clinical scenario, as it often reflects a history of space inadequacy, prolonged force application, or compromised biomechanical control. Excessive labial inclination may displace the tooth–root complex toward or beyond the alveolar envelope, reducing the margin for safe subsequent tooth movement and increasing susceptibility to periodontal stress.[5] In such cases, further orthodontic correction becomes mechanically challenging, as force systems must be carefully modulated to avoid exacerbating periodontal damage or advancing existing root resorption, while still achieving functional and esthetic objectives.[6] Adolescent patients may exhibit enhanced periodontal adaptability and vascularity compared to adults, which can partially mask the clinical signs of compromise despite significant radiographic root resorption. However, the long-term prognosis of teeth with reduced root length remains uncertain. Consequently, treatment planning in young patients with flared incisors and compromised roots necessitates a conservative, biologically driven approach that prioritizes stability and periodontal preservation over idealized orthodontic outcomes.[8]

This case report aims to highlight the periodontal implications of severe orthodontic proclination and root resorption in a teenage patient with previous orthodontic treatment, suggesting further treatment to overcome these complications. Emphasis is placed on clinical reasoning, interdisciplinary decision-making, and prognostic assessment, particularly in the context of preserved tooth stability despite advanced root resorption. The report underscores the importance of individualized treatment planning and realistic long-term expectations when managing compromised teeth in young patients.

## Case Report

A 13-year-old female patient presented for evaluation with the chief complaint of esthetic and functional concerns related to upper front dental flaring and an increased distance between the upper and lower front teeth following previous orthodontic treatment. At the initial visit, the patient presented with study models of her initial malocclusion [Figure 1]. The patient demonstrated a skeletal Class I relationship with a bilateral 50% Class II molar and canine relationship. The vertical skeletal pattern was within normal limits, suggesting a normodivergent growth pattern. The patient exhibited significant upper incisor flaring, which contributed to an excessive overjet of 6.8 mm and a reduced overbite of − 1.5 mm, consistent with an anterior open-bite tendency. Additionally, the left maxillary second premolar was noted to be severely intruded, with a radiographic examination showing localized buccal alveolar bone resorption. Following the removal of the existing orthodontic appliances, a baseline periodontal examination revealed fair oral hygiene with mild plaque accumulation. Probing depths ranged between 2 and 3 mm, with generally pink and healthy gingiva. Grade 2 mobility was observed on all maxillary incisors, and grade 1 on the maxillary canines. A radiographic assessment demonstrated normal alveolar bone levels that were appropriate for the patient’s age, except for teeth 11 and 21, which were severely proclined. Moderate to severe external apical root resorption was observed on the maxillary incisors, with an open apex located at the base of the palatal alveolar bone. No gingival recession was found. These findings indicated periodontal stability, allowing orthodontic re-treatment under close periodontal observation.

The patient had a relatively convex facial profile, indicating dentoalveolar protrusion. A panoramic radiograph examination revealed that the apical portions of the maxillary anterior roots were not clearly visualized due to the altered axial inclination of the incisors and limitations inherent in panoramic imaging. A cone-beam computed tomography evaluation demonstrated external apical root resorption affecting approximately one-third of the maxillary incisors’ root length, along with a severely damaged buccal plate on the upper left second premolar [Figure 2].

### Treatment objectives

The primary treatment objectives were to correct the severely proclined upper anterior teeth and excessive overjet, while preserving the periodontal tissue through a conservative orthodontic approach. A comprehensive periodontal evaluation was planned following the completion of orthodontic treatment to assess the periodontal tissue status and the teeth affected by orthodontic movement.

### Treatment alternative

Treatment alternative included the extraction of four premolars. However, due to the severe mobility and root resorption of the maxillary incisors, which could potentially lead to tooth loss, as well as the prolonged treatment duration associated with the previous treatment attempt, this more invasive treatment approach was not pursued. Myofunctional therapy was not indicated in this case, as no evidence of myofunctional dysfunction was identified.

### Treatment progress

The extraction of teeth 1.5 and 2.5 was performed as part of the orthodontic treatment plan due to the severely damaged buccal plate on the intruded tooth 2.5 and to provide space for upper anterior retraction. The orthodontic phase was initiated by bonding 0.022 slot passive self-ligating metal brackets, followed by the insertion of 0.014 Cu NiTi wires into both arches. As treatment progressed, the archwires were changed to 0.018 Cu NiTi, 0.016 × 0.025 Cu NiTi, and 0.021 × 0.025 BioFinisher®. Upper anterior retraction was carried out using a light elastomeric chain on 0.019 × 0.025 stainless steel archwires, accompanied by the use of bilateral light Class III triangular elastics to reciprocally protract the upper posterior teeth while preventing further lower anterior teeth proclination.

Periodontal considerations were prioritized throughout the treatment, with controlled force employed to prevent bone and apical defect exacerbation. As alignment progressed, the anterior tooth position and angulation gradually improved to reduce overjet and facilitate open-bite correction. Space management and incisor retraction were carried out incrementally, with frequent monitoring of the periodontal response. After 29 months of orthodontic treatment, the fixed appliances were removed. The patient was fitted with upper and lower removable retainers, in addition to an upper bonded 3–3 retainer, which concurrently acted as a splint [Figure 3]. Throughout the treatment, the patient demonstrated good cooperation, and no additional adverse periodontal changes were observed.

### Treatment results

Posttreatment occlusal relationships included a Class I canine relationship and a Class II molar relationship bilaterally. The flared upper anteriors were corrected, and good occlusion was achieved. Normal overbite and overjet were achieved with midline coincidence. Teeth 11, 12, 21, and 22 demonstrated external root resorption, with teeth 12 and 22 involving approximately one-third of the root length and teeth 11 and 21 involving approximately one-half of the root length. Despite the degree of radicular shortening, the clinical examination revealed no gingival recession, no increased probing depths, and no pathological tooth mobility. The apical root resorption was confined to the root structure and did not involve the surrounding periodontal tissues. The gingival examination demonstrated a healthy appearance with normal pink coloration and an absence of bleeding on probing. The radiographic assessment confirmed that the alveolar crestal bone height remained within normal limits and was not compromised by the resorptive process. The periodontal ligament space appeared intact, and the remaining root length provided an approximate 1:1 crown-to-root ratio. All four incisors showed no discoloration and responded positively to thermal vitality testing. The remaining dentition exhibited healthy periodontal tissues with no evidence of progressive crestal bone loss or clinical attachment loss [Figure 4].

*[Figure] Posttreatment radiographic images*

Posttreatment lateral cephalometric analysis and superimposition demonstrated significant dentoalveolar correction. The maxillary incisor position and inclination were substantially normalized, improving from 12.3 to 9 mm relative to APo, and from 153.8° to 123.1° relative to SN, respectively. The interincisal angle increased from 76.2° to 96°, thereby contributing to an overjet improvement from 6.8 to 1.8 mm and an overbite improvement from − 1.5 to 0 mm. The facial profile evaluation showed improved balance and harmony [Figure 5 and Table 1]. Furthermore, the facial appearance and occlusion remained stable at the 10-month follow-up appointment, confirming the success of the interdisciplinary and conservative orthodontic approach [Figure 6].

## Discussion

Orthodontic re-treatment of severely proclined maxillary incisors with advanced external root resorption presents a complex biological and mechanical challenge, especially in adolescent patients. Excessive labial inclination pushes the tooth–root complex toward the alveolar envelope, increasing periodontal stress and the risk of dehiscence, attachment loss, and further root resorption.[9] In this case, previous orthodontic treatment amid space limitations likely contributed to uncontrolled incisor proclination and subsequent root damage. Cephalometric evaluation showed significant improvement in upper incisor protrusion (decreased by 3.3 mm), proclination (decreased by 30.7°), interincisal angle (increased by 19.8°), overjet (decreased by 5 mm), and overbite (increased by 1.5 mm), resulting in the normalization of overjet and correction of the anterior open-bite tendency, with a positive improvement in facial balance and harmony. These changes demonstrate that controlled orthodontic mechanics can reposition compromised upper incisors within biological limits while meeting functional and esthetic goals.

Despite radiographic evidence of moderate to severe external apical root resorption with persistently open apices, periodontal evaluation both before and after treatment revealed a healthy periodontium, characterized by physiologic probing depths, an absence of bleeding on probing or gingival recession, preserved alveolar crestal bone height, and an intact periodontal ligament space. These findings suggest that the resorptive process was confined to the root structure and did not compromise periodontal support, supporting the decision to proceed with orthodontic re-treatment under strict periodontal supervision.[10] This supports the idea that orthodontic tooth movement in compromised conditions can be performed safely, provided periodontal inflammation is controlled, and movement remains within the biological envelope.[611] Several treatment options were evaluated. A non-extraction approach was considered unfavorable due to the risk of further incisor flaring and increased periodontal stress.[12] Extraction of severely resorbed incisors was seen as inappropriate in a young patient with preserved periodontal support and pulpal vitality.[13] Extraction of all four premolars was considered but rejected because the prolonged treatment duration and more aggressive space-closure mechanics required could have increased biomechanical stress on the already compromised maxillary incisors and heightened the risk of further root resorption.[1415] Therefore, the extraction of teeth 1.5 and 2.5 was chosen to create space for controlled incisor retraction while preventing further labial displacement beyond the alveolar envelope, enabling correction with a more conservative approach.

Beyond mechanical factors, such as force magnitude, direction, and treatment duration, external apical root resorption was recognized as a multifactorial process influenced by individual biological variability. Previous studies have identified several contributing factors, including genetic predisposition, inflammatory response variability, root morphology, and a history of previous orthodontic treatment or dental trauma.[16] However, in many clinical scenarios, the severity of resorption often reflects a complex interaction between mechanical loading and individual host response.[17] In the present case, the observed resorption was most plausibly associated with a history of previous orthodontic treatment combined with excessive incisor proclination beyond the alveolar envelope, rather than a clearly identifiable systemic or structural predisposing factor. At the completion of treatment, the primary objective shifts toward maintaining long-term tooth stability. Dental assessment demonstrated no pathological mobility, no discoloration, and positive responses to thermal vitality testing despite marked radicular shortening and open apices. Although extensive regeneration of the resorbed root structure is not expected, immature teeth with preserved pulpal vitality may achieve functional apical stabilization through continued hard-tissue deposition and apical closure.[18] In this case, preservation of the coronal periodontal attachment and alveolar crest likely contributed to long-term tooth stability despite the 1:1 crown-to-root ratio.[19] Under these conditions, endodontic intervention was not indicated because the prophylactic treatment of vital teeth with external apical root resorption is not supported by current evidence and may have unnecessarily eliminated the potential for biological adaptation.[13] Importantly, in cases where pulpal vitality is maintained and there are no signs of infection or inflammatory resorption, endodontic intervention is not indicated as a preventive approach toward external apical root resorption. Current evidence indicates that prophylactic endodontic treatment does not arrest or reverse external apical root resorption and may unnecessarily compromise the biological potential of the tooth.[20] Therefore, pulp vitality preservation is preferred, as it may facilitate continued apical adaptation and contribute to long-term functional stability.

A bonded maxillary canine-to-canine retainer using a flexible multistrand wire was selected to enhance anterior stabilization by improving load distribution, minimizing stress concentration, reducing the risk of further root resorption, and limiting tooth displacement.[21] Overall, this case highlights that severe external apical root resorption should not be regarded as an absolute contraindication to orthodontic re-treatment. With careful control of biomechanics, appropriate treatment selection, and an emphasis on maintaining periodontal health and pulpal vitality, teeth with compromised root structures can be repositioned safely, achieving functional and esthetic improvement with a favorable prognosis.

## Conclusion

Conservative orthodontic re-treatment in adolescents with severe maxillary incisor proclination and advanced external apical root resorption can achieve meaningful functional and esthetic improvement without compromising periodontal health or pulpal vitality.Controlled space creation and light-force biomechanics enabled incisor repositioning within biological limits, resulting in stable occlusion, and maintained periodontal support despite persistent root shortening.Severe external apical root resorption should not be considered an absolute contraindication to orthodontic re-treatment when individualized planning and continuous periodontal monitoring are applied.
