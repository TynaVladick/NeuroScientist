# When are large language models medical devices? Navigating the MDR and AI Act for healthcare applications

- **Authors:** Jessica D Workum, Sade J Krijgsman, Ildiko Vajda, R M Van Stokkum, Jan van de Brand, Diederik Gommers, Ari Ercole, Michel van Genderen
- **Journal:** BMJ Digital Health & AI
- **Year:** 2026

- **PMCID:** PMC13646968
- **DOI:** 10.1136/bmjdh-2026-000003
- **Keywords:** Artificial intelligence; Medical Informatics; Clinical Governance

## Abstract

The flexibility of generative artificial intelligence (AI), and specifically large language models (LLMs), holds promise to transform healthcare by offering new tools to improve patient care and enhance clinician efficiency. However, this flexibility introduces new challenges, as LLM applications might inadvertently be used in ways that do not align with the intended purpose of the manufacturer, raising patient safety concerns and liability issues. We have identified a regulatory gap whereby LLM applications that could be repurposed, malfunction or be inadvertently misused for medical purposes fall outside the scope of the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) because they lack a medical intended purpose, and outside meaningful AI Act safety obligations because they do not qualify as high-risk systems, yet still present patient safety risks without adequate regulatory guidance. In this manuscript, we aim to address this regulatory gap for LLM applications in healthcare by identifying these ambiguous grey-area applications and providing four recommendations—validation, mitigation, compliance and alignment—focused on prevention and risk mitigation rather than reclassification as medical devices. These recommendations are intended to advance the responsible implementation of LLM applications in healthcare while ensuring patient safety.

### WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

- This review identifies a regulatory gap for non-medical LLM applications that may still function as medical devices through repurposing, misuse or malfunction.

- This review poses that blanket reclassification of such applications as medical devices is neither sufficient nor proportionate, and instead proposes risk-based actionable governance strategies focused on validation, mitigation, compliance, alignment and clear stakeholder responsibilities.

### HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

- The manuscript offers concrete actionable guidance for healthcare organisations, policymakers and developers to navigate the regulatory landscape and mitigate inherent LLM risks, supporting responsible innovation and patient safety through prevention, proportionate governance strategies and clear stakeholder roles.

- This approach may serve as a model for other jurisdictions and stimulate further research and consensus-building on the governance of generative AI in clinical settings.

## Introduction

The integration of generative artificial intelligence (AI) into healthcare has the potential to transform medical practices by offering new tools to enhance efficiency, accuracy and patient outcomes. Specifically, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as transformative technologies capable of performing diverse tasks ranging from clinical documentation to decision support.12 By reducing administrative burden, LLMs can free clinicians to spend more time in direct patient interaction, potentially improving the patient experience and allowing more opportunity for patients to ask questions and engage in shared decision-making.

Unlike traditional AI models, which are typically designed for specific purposes, LLMs are inherently multipurpose systems. LLMs differ from traditional AI models in their flexibility, allowing adaptation to various contexts and tasks.3 This may result in use of LLM applications that does not align with the original intended purpose of the manufacturer.

Regulatory frameworks play a pivotal role in ensuring that these technologies are deployed responsibly in healthcare. In the European Union (EU), the Medical Device Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (MDR) and In vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation (EU) 2017/746 (IVDR) provide a robust framework for assessing the safety and performance of medical devices, including software with medical purposes.4 However, the rapid implementation of LLMs has introduced complexities that were not anticipated when these legislations were drafted, posing new regulatory challenges.56 All relevant legislative citations can be found in online supplemental section 1.

A key issue lies in determining whether an LLM application qualifies as a medical device under the MDR. According to Article 2 (1) of the MDR, software intended for medical purposes, such as aiding in diagnosis, prevention or treatment, falls within scope. The LLM application must then undergo rigorous testing before it can be used in clinical practice. If LLM applications are not explicitly intended for medical purposes, they do not qualify as a medical device and clinical testing is therefore not mandated by the MDR. However, some LLM applications could still be repurposed, malfunction or inadvertently be misused as a medical device (table 1).7 For example, an LLM-based chatbot deployed into healthcare for administrative purposes could be instructed by the clinician to suggest treatment options for a specific patient. As these applications have not undergone the scrutiny of the MDR, this raises patient safety concerns and may cause liability issues for all parties involved.8

This creates a dual regulatory challenge. On one hand, LLM applications that are not medical devices may nonetheless present patient safety risks when they malfunction or are misused for clinical purposes. On the other hand, unnecessarily categorising LLM applications as medical devices could unjustifiably hinder innovation and delay implementation. MDR compliance is a time-consuming and costly process, often requiring years of preparation, extensive technical documentation, clinical evaluation and conformity assessments by a Notified Body (NB). If LLM applications that do not genuinely serve a medical purpose are nonetheless deemed medical devices, developers and healthcare institutions may face disproportionate regulatory burdens, stifling the development and deployment of useful tools for administrative or low-risk functions. A practical answer lies in proportionate prevention and mitigation strategies rather than broadening the scope of the MDR.

The EU AI Act, while introducing a risk-based approach and general requirements for AI systems, does not sufficiently address this gap. Adopted in 2024, the AI Act establishes a comprehensive legal framework for regulating AI systems and complements existing regulations like the MDR by focusing on AI systems’ broader societal and ethical implications.9 It adopts a risk-based approach, tailoring regulatory requirements to the level of risk posed by AI systems. This approach categorises AI systems into minimal, limited, high and unacceptable risk, with stricter requirements imposed on high-risk AI systems and specific obligations for both providers (ie, developers or manufacturers) and deployers (ie, the healthcare institution implementing the LLM application). The AI Act states that if the use of an AI system is not in accordance with its intended purpose, it is considered a form of misuse. Specifically, reasonably foreseeable misuse of high-risk systems must be addressed by the provider. Although the AI Act regulates General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models and systems, these specific regulations only apply when the system has the capability to serve a variety of purposes, such as the GPT-model itself. However, many LLM applications deployed for specific tasks do not fall under these provisions. Furthermore, the AI Act does not explicitly account for the specific risks, challenges and requirements associated with LLMs in medical contexts or limited-risk applications, nor does it provide any potential solutions to the challenges posed in this manuscript.10–12 The most recent EU guidance on MDR-AI Act interplay (MDCG 2025–6) aims to minimise the dual regulatory burden of the AI Act and MDR and clarify definitions, but does not address these challenges either.13 Therefore, specific guidance on how to assess multipurpose LLMs, especially those that could be repurposed for or misused in clinical settings, is lacking. As a result, there is a risk of both under-regulation of high-risk applications and over-regulation of low-risk or administrative tools, leaving stakeholders without a clear pathway for compliance or innovation.

Several bodies of scholarly work have identified similar novel risks and regulatory challenges with the introduction of generative AI. Minssen et al provided an early broad overview of the legal and ethical challenges posed by LLMs in healthcare and called for regulatory guidance from bodies such as the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency.14 While they identify determining a system’s intended purpose as a central and unresolved challenge, they do not address governance solutions for non-qualified LLM applications already operating in clinical settings. Gilbert et al focused on LLM chatbots intended for clinical decision support (CDS).15 They argue that such systems legally qualify as medical devices under EU and US law, yet their fundamental technical characteristics, including the generation of plausible but unverifiable outputs, a near-infinite range of inputs and outputs that cannot be systematically tested and the absence of traceable sources, preclude approval as such under current frameworks. Smith and Price examined the regulatory challenge for specialised medical LLMs within the EU MDR.16 They identified that the framework’s forward-walking approach, which sequences risk assessment from a fixed intended use, is poorly suited to systems whose general-purpose origins introduce a variability of risks across a broad spectrum. They ultimately conclude that governance cannot rest with providers alone. Singh et al, in a taxonomy of 1016 FDA-authorised AI-enabled medical devices, found that not a single authorised device involves an LLM.17 This empirically illustrates that the entire regulatory apparatus for medical AI was built around traditional, task-specific systems; LLMs are entering healthcare through a pathway that sits entirely outside this framework. These works each address either LLMs designed or adapted for an explicit medical intended purpose, or AI systems already operating within the regulatory perimeter. A distinct and growing challenge receives far less attention: LLM applications deployed in healthcare without a medical intended purpose, which may inadvertently be repurposed, malfunction or misused as such.

Busch et al and Aboy et al analysed the compliance obligations of the EU AI Act for healthcare AI and regulated digital medical products, respectively, both confirming that general-purpose LLMs that do not formally qualify as medical devices fall outside the Act’s high-risk requirements; for such applications, only transparency obligations and voluntary codes of conduct apply, with no structured governance pathway.1012 Freyer et al propose a five-paradigm typology for the functional classification of AI systems deployed in intensive care. Their framework helps determine regulatory implications for these types of AI systems and they acknowledge the challenges of multipurpose broad AI. However, their framework does not provide guidance on what healthcare organisations should do to govern deployment, prevent misuse or mitigate patient safety risks associated with these applications. Ong et al make a compelling case that existing regulatory frameworks are structurally inadequate for generative AI and call for global legislative reform. Their proposed solution is, however, contingent on legislative processes that may take years to conclude, and in the interim, healthcare organisations managing patient safety risks from non-qualified LLM applications remain without structured guidance.

This manuscript provides the structured guidance that healthcare organisations currently lack, addressing a specific and growing governance gap: LLM applications that do not qualify as medical devices under the EU MDR, yet have the potential to function as such through repurposing, malfunctioning or misuse. To that end, we describe three categories of LLM applications in the context of the MDR: those that carry an explicit medical intended purpose and qualify as medical device software; those that clearly fall outside its scope and a grey area of applications that do not qualify as medical devices yet carry precisely these patient safety risks. We provide four actionable practical recommendations (validation, mitigation, compliance, alignment) designed to function as governance best practices for healthcare organisations and developers operating within the current regulatory framework, without requiring legislative reform. The broader aim is to give healthcare institutions, developers, legal professionals and policymakers a shared reference point for understanding where governance responsibilities lie and how to discharge them in the absence of comprehensive regulatory guidance. In doing so, we aim to protect patient safety for non-MDR LLM applications through proportionate governance measures, rather than through legislative change. We then situate this framework within an international regulatory context, drawing on approaches in the UK, Australia and the USA, before addressing the limitations of the current framework and identifying the empirical validation work needed to operationalise these recommendations in practice.

### Medical device

According to the MDR, software—including AI systems—qualifies as a medical device only when the manufacturer explicitly intends it to be used for medical purposes such as diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, treatment or alleviation of disease (MDR, Article 2 (1)).418 This is commonly described as CDS. Relevant medical purposes for AI systems are shown in figure 1.1314 In this manuscript, medical device software (MDSW) follows the MDR definition. Additionally, software is considered MDSW if it aids in clinical decision-making for individual patients and performs an action on data beyond simple functions like storage or communication.18 The key factor in determining whether software qualifies as MDSW is the manufacturer’s explicit definition of its ‘intended purpose’ as a medical purpose. The mere use of software within a medical context or the processing of medical information as input and output does not automatically make the software a medical device (MDR Recital 19). However, a manufacturer’s declaration of non-medical intended purpose does not preclude MDR qualification where objective evidence points to a clear medical purpose. Under MDR Article 2 (12), intended purpose is determined from all data supplied by the manufacturer, including the label, instructions for use and promotional or sales materials. Where these materials, or the product’s actual functional design, demonstrate that the software is intended to perform clinical reasoning (eg, by structurally generating diagnostic conclusions), it may qualify as MDSW irrespective of how the manufacturer has characterised its intended purpose.

*[Figure] Medical purposes relevant for artificial intelligence applications as defined by the Medical Device Regulation.*

When addressing the MDR, it is important to distinguish between qualification and classification. Qualification addresses whether the software falls within the definition of MDSW. Only once software qualifies is it classified into its associated risk category (I, IIa, IIb or III) based on its mode of action and potential harm. A more in-depth explanation can be found in online supplemental section 2.

An LLM application qualifies as MDSW, if its intended purpose is CDS for individual patients. It must then comply with all medical device requirements to ensure patient safety and clinical efficacy. While class I devices involve a self-certification process, higher classes require oversight by the NB, adding complexity and costs to compliance.19 Regulatory approval under the AI Act additionally involves addressing issues related to bias, explainability, transparency and human oversight.1315

For LLM applications, this process may present additional challenges; for instance, because LLM applications can produce differing outputs from identical inputs, clinical validation may become more complex. However, given that clinicians are prone to automation bias, meaning the generated results are no longer critically questioned, clinical validation is of utmost importance for LLM applications, as incorrect or misaligned information could lead to patient safety issues.820 While improving AI literacy among clinicians reduces automation bias, its overall impact remains limited.21 This stresses the importance of using validated tools in healthcare, regardless of medical device status.

Problems arise when an LLM application is not qualified as a medical device but has the potential to be used as one, as these applications have not undergone the scrutiny of the MDR. We therefore propose a framework to classify LLM applications into clear categories with regard to legislation and provide recommendations for stakeholders to guide responsible implementation of non-MDR LLM applications where legislation falls short.

### LLM applications clearly MDSW

Applications in this category are those explicitly intended for medical purposes (figure 1). These include clinical decision support systems (CDSS) that assist in diagnosis, treatment or monitoring. Examples include using an LLM application with access to the electronic health record (EHR) to analyse patient data, such as clinical notes, laboratory results and imaging, to provide diagnostic or treatment recommendations (online supplemental section 4.1). For such applications, their risk classification is decisive, as it determines the regulatory pathway. Most LLM-based CDSS would likely fall under class IIa or higher due to their potential impact on patient outcomes and their mode of action.18

Under the AI Act, LLM applications with MDR class IIa or higher are considered high-risk AI systems. These applications must comply with the stringent requirements of the MDR, while the AI Act imposes additional requirements related to transparency, human oversight, data governance and conformity assessment by the NB.12 The dual regulatory burden of MDR and AI Act compliance underscores the need for harmonised conformity assessments to avoid duplicative efforts.22

### LLM applications clearly not MDSW

This category encompasses LLM applications with no intended medical purpose or substantial risk of being inadvertently misused as medical devices. Examples include tools designed solely for administrative tasks, such as translating clinical documentation into a different language (online supplemental section 4.2), rewriting radiology reports into layman’s terms or drafting correspondence. Because they are not intended for CDS, they do not qualify as MDSW. However, to ensure patient safety, it remains important to perform clinical validation, verifying that hallucinations and errors do not introduce misinformation. It is recommended to have a governance system in place for evaluation.23

These applications are not subject to MDR but still fall under the EU AI Act, generally as limited-risk systems. Regardless of the risk classification, the AI Act emphasises principles such as transparency, accountability and the importance of AI literacy among users.

### LLM applications not MDSW but with the potential for misuse

This category represents a grey area where LLM applications do not qualify as medical devices under the MDR, but could (1) be repurposed, (2) malfunction or (3) be misused by the end user in ways that align with the MDR’s definition of a medical device. Table 1 shows specific examples to illustrate these three scenarios. These examples highlight the challenges for these LLM applications, as their unintended use could compromise patient safety. Legal implications for providers, deployers and even healthcare professionals may arise if an application inadvertently causes harm while being used as a de facto medical device without proper scrutiny.

#### Repurposing

Repurposing occurs when software is adapted beyond its original intended purpose to serve new functions, including medical purposes. For example, a hospital may initially deploy an LLM application to automatically extract International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision codes from clinician notes for billing purposes. Over time, the system is enhanced to generate SNOMED CT-encoded clinical summaries from unstructured EHR data and begins ranking differential diagnoses. When a healthcare organisation undertakes such repurposing, it effectively modifies the intended purpose and therefore assumes new regulatory roles: under the AI Act, it becomes the deployer and the provider, and under the MDR it risks being regarded as the manufacturer.24

#### Malfunctioning

To further illustrate malfunctioning of LLM applications, consider LLM-based summarisers. The software’s intended purpose is strictly to condense existing clinical notes into a shorter, more readable format. Where the ground source, such as a clinical transcript or the original clinical notes, already contains the clinician’s findings and conclusions, and the LLM only condenses this existing information, no clinical reasoning is performed by the software itself; the intended purpose remains administrative and falls outside the definition of medical intended purpose. Conversely, where an LLM uses clinical reasoning to arrive at a diagnosis not present in the source material, this constitutes a medical purpose and the software would qualify as MDSW. Under the MDR, qualification hinges on the intended purpose of the software, not on how downstream users might use the summarised text. Thus, summarisation of medical notes is an administrative action that does not constitute a medical purpose by itself. The fact that another clinician may later rely on the summary to inform a decision does not retroactively change the summariser’s purpose. Under the revised MDCG 2019–11, such a tool would only qualify as MDSW if the software contributes to achieve a specific medical purpose (eg, guiding therapy); otherwise, it remains outside MDR scope.18

However, administrative LLM-based summarisers may inadvertently employ clinical reasoning to formulate a diagnosis or hallucinate a treatment recommendation not present in the source material. As this would be considered malfunctioning of the software, it does not constitute them MDSW, as is described in MDCG 2019–11 section 3.1. However, such newly introduced risks could lead to serious patient harm. We call for strict measures to prevent these types of malfunctioning. Therefore, mitigating strategies should be employed, as outlined below.

#### Misuse

Misuse occurs when an end user applies software for a medical purpose for which it was not intended. For example, a clinician may use a generic LLM-based chatbot, such as Microsoft Copilot, marketed for administrative tasks, to draft a differential diagnosis for a specific patient. The chatbot itself does not thereby become a medical device, but the clinician and healthcare organisation assume responsibility for its inappropriate use. Misuse highlights the importance of institutional governance, procurement policies and user training, and under the AI Act, may give rise to deployer obligations even when the system is not qualified as a medical device. Healthcare professionals could also be held liable when misusing an LLM application as a medical device.

For these grey area applications, the AI Act’s risk-based approach becomes crucial. While certain requirements for high-risk AI systems offer guidance, the AI Act does not provide practical direction on how to specifically address illicit use as a medical device. Additionally, many grey area LLM applications would be considered limited risk. As such, high-risk requirements do not apply. Regulatory sandboxes, as outlined in the AI Act, provide valuable opportunities for adversarial stress testing of LLM applications in controlled environments to determine the potential risks of providing unintended medical advice. This approach enables the identification of vulnerabilities and the enhancement of safeguard robustness to mitigate the risk of illicit use as a medical device.

### Mitigating strategies for grey area applications

In the absence of formal EU guidance on grey area LLM applications, the following four recommendations (validation, mitigation, compliance and alignment) represent actionable governance best practices, shared between providers and deployers. Our recommendations draw on principles established in the EU AI Act for high-risk systems and, where applicable by analogy, the MDR, adapted proportionately for applications that fall outside these regulatory frameworks.

First, ensure robust validation of the output of LLMs, warranting the output is consistently accurate. Validation encompasses a technological component (ie, output accuracy compared with ground truth) and a clinical component (ie, clinical relevance of errors). Validation should assess accuracy, consistency and clinical utility using both quantitative methods (eg, error rates, consistency measures) and qualitative expert assessments on diverse test cases.23 This mirrors Article 15 of the AI Act, which requires high-risk AI systems to achieve appropriate levels of accuracy. It is imperative that developers be transparent about their methods for validation and their results, as validation of outputs is essential to ensure patient safety, as reflected in AI Act Article 13. However, the field currently lacks universally agreed on validation standards and guidelines, making it challenging to assess whether these applications reliably perform as intended or to compare their performance with others or across different settings. Initiatives such as the RIGH:T Consortium are working to address this gap by developing consensus-based validation frameworks for generative AI applications in healthcare.25 Additionally, validation should be repeated periodically, as changes in the underlying LLM or input data could impact the output. Therefore, as reflected by AI Act Article 17, a governance strategy should be in place.23

Second, developers should implement mitigating strategies and technological safeguards to prevent misuse of their LLM application as a medical device, underscored by AI Act Article 9, which requires providers to identify reasonably foreseeable misuse and take appropriate risk management measures. Practically, this can be accomplished through the creation of custom content filters or through prompt engineering techniques (online supplemental section 5). Determining what constitutes CDS requires assessment of both the query and the deployment context. For example, queries like ‘List the medications in this discharge letter’ or ‘Explain atrial fibrillation in patient-friendly terms’ would be permitted as non-CDS, whereas ‘Based on this 75 kg patient’s creatinine of 210 µmol/L, what dose of amoxicillin should I prescribe?’ does constitute patient-specific CDS and a response should be prevented. Ambiguous queries such as ‘What is the most common cause of elevated troponin in a middle aged male?’ could represent either educational study or preparation for treating a specific patient. Therefore, for LLM chatbots, we propose a three-tier barrier: if it is evident that the chatbot is being used for CDS, no output should be provided; if the usage is ambiguous, the output should be accompanied by a warning reminding the user of the intended use; for all other inquiries, the output should be displayed as usual (figure 3).

Third, to promote compliance, mandatory terms of use should be clearly defined for the end user, specifying usage only for non-medical purposes. Articles 13 and 26 and Recital 72 of the AI Act establish transparency and instructions-for-use requirements for high-risk AI systems, including information on intended purpose and precluded uses directed at the deployer. We recommend making these obligations enforceable: access to the software must be conditional on explicit acceptance of these terms of use, ensuring that users fully understand and agree to the restrictions, including the limitation of use for non-medical purposes. A reminder should be clearly visible during use. Additionally, AI literacy initiatives should be incorporated to help clinicians recognise the importance of appropriate use and explain the potential risks associated with misuse as a medical device (AI Act Articles 4 and 26).

Finally, developers should align with best practices for safety-critical healthcare applications. This includes establishing comprehensive technical documentation, implementing at least a lightweight quality management system, conducting safety evaluations appropriate to the application’s risk profile and maintaining postmarket surveillance. Inspiration can be drawn from the MDR Annex I and Sections 2 and 3 of Chapter III of the AI Act for high-risk systems. Specifically, developers should incorporate versioning and clear release documentation whenever either software or intended use changes, as well as incident logging and review of any adverse events or unexpected behaviours. Such processes are already mandatory in some jurisdictions, for example, in the UK. Such proactive measures demonstrate responsible development and reduce the likelihood of patient harm, liability disputes and potential regulatory scrutiny should use patterns evolve.

These mitigation strategies have direct implications for institutional governance. Healthcare organisations should establish procurement criteria requiring vendors to disclose intended purpose, validation evidence and safeguards. Liability allocation requires contractual clarity between providers and deployers. Institutions should: (1) inventory existing LLM applications and classify them using figure 2; (2) implement the four recommendations proportionate to risk for grey area applications and (3) monitor for drift from intended purpose.

Although this manuscript focuses on the EU regulatory framework, stakeholders often operate across jurisdictions. A brief overview of approaches in the UK, Australia and the USA helps put the regulatory challenges into context (summarised in table 2).

#### United Kingdom

After Brexit the UK’s device regulations are split between Northern Ireland (NI), which follows the EU’s MDR and IVDR, and Great Britain (GB), where the regulations are still based on the EU’s three Directives (embodied as the Medical Devices Regulations 2002). The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) is the UK’s Competent Authority for medical devices and is working to implement new regulations following a consultation in 2021. Draft regulations have since been formally notified to the World Trade Organization with implementation expected from 2027, although these remain subject to further comment. The full application of the EU AI Act in NI is currently under discussion. GB does not have an equivalent to the EU AI Act, but it does have comprehensive regulation of patient records, specifically Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 Regulation 17, with responsibilities placed on service providers and employees creating records.26 These requirements apply to any system used to create medical records whether a medical device or not.

The MHRA emphasises transparency (explainability and interpretability of AI outputs) and adaptivity (controls for retraining and updating models), recognising that many LLMs will evolve over time. In early 2025, National Health Service (NHS) in England issued a statement stating that LLM-based summarisation would be treated as high functionality and likely would qualify as Class I MDSW.2728 This has led to an immediate, although temporary, pause in scribe deployment in the UK.29 It must be noted that the NHS statement is primarily operational risk-management guidance for the NHS rather than a formal regulatory interpretation, though it has occasionally been conflated with the regulator’s view.

By contrast, the MHRA’s general software guidance emphasises that software used in healthcare does not automatically qualify as a medical device. Importantly, requiring class I status does not add independent premarket scrutiny. While class I brings obligations such as compliance with the Essential Requirements, technical documentation and postmarket surveillance, it does not introduce a third-party review. As such, the step from unclassified software to class I alone provides limited additional safety. On July 29, 2026, the MHRA released guidance on Ambient Voice Technology (AVT)-enabled products explicitly stating that if this software is solely intended to summarise a conversation in a healthcare setting and is not intended to be used for a medical purpose, this AVT product is not considered MDSW, thereby providing much needed clarity.30 The NHS has since updated their statement to align with the MHRA.

Of particular note, there is a legal responsibility on NHS organisations under the Health and Social Care Act 2012 to apply additional published standards to deployment of digital healthcare technology which, by definition, encompasses AI and LLM tools irrespective of whether they are classed as medical devices or not which is not determined by the NHS. At the time of writing, there are two relevant standards: DCB0129 (typically fulfilled by the manufacturer’s technical file developed as part of compliance assurance) and DCB0160. The latter is of particular interest because it places a legal responsibility on the deploying organisation to implement a clinical risk management system including hazard assessment and mitigation. This provides a convenient framework into which our four recommendations neatly fit and may be a useful framework for other locales.

#### Australia

The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) provides specific guidance on ‘digital scribes’.31 Transcribing or translating clinical conversations is not considered a medical device, but once software interprets conversations for the purpose of generating diagnostic or therapeutic suggestions not explicitly stated by the healthcare professional, it is. For example, if a technical subsystem or tool begins to interpret clinical conversations or provide diagnostic or therapeutic recommendations not explicitly stated by the healthcare professional, it would be reclassified as a medical device and subject to regulatory oversight. For example, a digital scribe that starts suggesting treatment options based on summarised notes would trigger TGA review and require compliance with MDRs. This nuanced approach shows that summarisation alone is not a medical purpose, but summarisation combined with interpretation for the purposes of providing CDS is.

#### United States

The FDA is the regulatory body for medical devices in the USA.32 They have defined CDSS as software that is intended to provide decision support for the diagnosis, treatment, prevention, cure or mitigation of diseases or other conditions.33 Administrative support is not considered CDS.34 Additionally, in case of CDSS, the 21st Century Cures Act provides exemptions of certain types of CDSS that are subsequently not regulated as medical devices.33 For example, if an LLM-powered chatbot deployed for administrative support, such as drafting discharge summaries, is limited to summarising or displaying medical information without offering clinical advice, it falls outside the scope of the FDA’s MDRs, as clarified by the 21st Century Cures Act. But if, for example, the chatbot starts to recommend medication dosages or diagnostic pathways, it would be subject to FDA regulation. LLM applications that merely display or summarise medical information without providing medical advice are likely not considered CDS and likely fall outside the definition of a medical device.

### Limitations

Our analysis has two principal limitations. First, our four governance best practices have not been empirically validated in real-world clinical settings. They are derived from regulatory principles, existing legislative frameworks and practical experience, but stakeholder consensus across clinicians, legal experts, regulators and patient representatives is needed to refine and operationalise them. Future research should pilot these recommendations in healthcare institutions of varying scale and resource capacity and generate the evidence base required for broader adoption.

Second, this manuscript addresses a naturally evolving regulatory area. LLM capabilities are advancing at a pace that consistently outpaces legislative processes, meaning new clinical use cases and deployment patterns will continuously generate grey area scenarios beyond those we have identified here. At the same time, the regulatory landscape itself is in flux: ongoing developments such as the EU Digital Omnibus on AI and the MDR/IVDR Simplification Proposal may redraw the boundaries of the current grey zone either with or without resolving it.3536 The grey area our manuscript describes may therefore look different in the coming years but because both technology and regulation will have shifted. This manuscript should accordingly be understood as a principled starting framework rather than a definitive regulatory map, and it is intentionally designed to leave room for guidance documents, institutional policies and regulatory bodies to fill and adapt as the field evolves. We also note that similar issues apply to the IVDR, which falls outside the scope of this analysis. Notwithstanding these inherent challenges, we believe the framework presented here makes a meaningful contribution to governance discourse by identifying the structural nature of the gap, providing actionable steps for prevention and mitigation and a conceptual basis on which future guidance can build.

## Conclusion

The rapid emergence of LLMs in healthcare presents unprecedented opportunities, yet creates regulatory gaps that are insufficiently addressed by the MDR and the AI Act. Balancing patient safety while simultaneously leveraging these technologies requires clear guidance. We have identified a regulatory grey area of LLM applications that do not qualify as medical devices under the EU MDR, yet have the potential to function as such through repurposing, malfunctioning or misuse, falling outside both MDR scope and meaningful AI Act safety obligations. We propose four practical and actionable best practice recommendations—validation, mitigation, compliance and alignment—to address this gap through proportionate governance measures and thus advance the responsible implementation of LLM applications into clinical practice.
