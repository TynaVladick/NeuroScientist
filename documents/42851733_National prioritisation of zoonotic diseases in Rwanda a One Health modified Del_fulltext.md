# National prioritisation of zoonotic diseases in Rwanda: a One Health modified Delphi consensus study

## Abstract

Introduction: The Quadripartite One Health Joint Plan of Action and the WHO’s Pandemic Agreement emphasise operationalising the One Health (OH) approach to strengthen global preparedness, prevention and response to emerging health threats. In line with these global commitments, Rwanda has developed a national One Health Strategic Plan to enhance multisectoral collaboration across the human, animal and environmental health sectors. However, effective implementation requires substantial investment in integrated surveillance, preparedness and response systems, highlighting the need for an evidence-based prioritisation framework to guide resource mobilisation and strategic allocation. This exercise aimed to prioritise zoonotic diseases of public health importance in Rwanda using criteria including disease burden, outbreak potential, morbidity and mortality, socioeconomic impact, availability of diagnostic tools, transboundary risk and the effectiveness of prevention and control measures.

Methods: A modified Delphi approach was employed, consisting of (1) a planning phase to develop prioritisation criteria and survey instruments, (2) two rounds of expert surveys and (3) a consensus-building workshop using Mentimeter for anonymous voting. All the activities were conducted in Kigali, Rwanda. 25 experts representing the Ministries of Health, Agriculture and Environment; national regulatory agencies; the Rwanda Agriculture and Animal Resources Development Board; Rwanda Wildlife Conservation, Gorilla Doctors; the University of Rwanda; the University of Global Health Equity and development partners including Igisekuruza Tree (iGiTREE), Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit (GIZ) and United States Agency for International Development (USAID) participated. Additional technical support was provided by the Pan-African One Health Institute.

Results: Rabies, zoonotic tuberculosis and toxoplasmosis emerged as the highest-priority zoonotic diseases within their respective categories. Participants identified limited transdisciplinary training opportunities, inadequate sustainable financing mechanisms and insufficient data integration across sectors as key barriers to effective OH implementation.

Conclusion: Strengthening integrated surveillance systems, improving multisectoral data sharing and investing in a competent OH workforce are critical for enhancing preparedness against priority zoonoses. Furthermore, regular prioritisation exercises will ensure that disease rankings remain current and responsive to evolving risks.

- A call for global operationalisation of One Health (OH) to anchor preparedness and control of antimicrobial resistance and emerging zoonoses.

- In Rwanda, a national OH strategy had been developed but was poorly implemented.

### WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

- Using participatory approaches, we rank zoonotic diseases in order of priority for a more tailored implementation of the national OH strategy.

- The study highlights additional challenges related to the implementation of the OH Strategy and proposes mitigation approaches for Rwanda.

### HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

- The study findings contribute to strategic planning, efficient resource allocation and cost-effective interventions for zoonotic disease prevention and control, as demonstrated during the recent Marburg outbreak.

## Introduction

Zoonotic pathogens infect, transmit between and cause diseases in humans and animals.1 These pathogens can persist in the environment and be transmitted via food, water and/or vectors.2–6 There is evidence that the prevalence of zoonoses is increasing in Africa,7–10 often due to population growth, urbanisation, habitat disruption and climate change, all of which change the human–animal contact structure.11–16

One Health (OH) is an interdisciplinary concept that acknowledges the connection between human, animal and environmental health, and controlling zoonotic diseases is a quintessential OH challenge.17–19 The OH concept has its origins in Africa, emerging from anthropological studies of the Dinka ethnic group in South Sudan, characterised by the codependency of humans, animals and the environment.2021

Today, this concept is the basis on which a quadripartite consortium of United Nations (UN) organisations was established. It includes the Food and Agriculture Organisation of the UN, the United Nations Environment Programme, WHO and the World Organisation for Animal Health. The quadripartite redefined OH as an “integrated, unifying approach that aims to sustainably balance and optimise the health of people, animals and ecosystems.”22 There is a global call for nations to invest in the operationalisation of OH through coordinated, multisectoral action.2223 However, the World Bank estimates that operationalising OH will cost countries in the Global South $1.9–3.4 billion annually to realise short-term savings of $184 million.24 Therefore, prioritisation exercises can help identify critical areas of the systems to maximise efficiency and targeted deployment of resources.25–27 Such a process can also (1) raise awareness, (2) catalyse collaborative partnerships, (3) strengthen surveillance and (4) support resource mobilisation, ultimately enhancing preparedness and response capacities and informing policy and practice for effective OH implementation.

### OH in the context of Rwanda

Rwanda has developed an OH governance framework adapted from the OH policy28 (figure 1A). It focuses on the development of leadership and improving technical coordination functions to guide and maximise the benefits of an integrated control approach. To leverage convening power, the framework is anchored at the prime minister’s office, while director generals and/or chief executive officers of the core implementing agencies of the concerned sectors catalyse the technical implementation.28 This expertise across OH is assembled through technical working groups (TWGs) as shown in figure 1, whose mandate is to generate transdisciplinary evidence to guide the implementation of the national policy and strategic planning for disease control. An example where TWGs are active is the national action plans (NAPs) for climate change, health security and antimicrobial resistance (AMR).2529–31 The ambition is to operationalise such TWGs for the control of zoonotic diseases,689 directly contributing to pandemic preparedness, prevention and response.2532

### OH prioritisation for Rwanda

To strengthen implementation of the national OH strategy and identify pathways for operationalisation of OH, a zoonotic disease prioritisation exercise was conducted in Kigali, Rwanda. The exercise also aimed to (a) facilitate a joint expert assessment of current challenges, (b) identify actionable pathways and (c) launch a OH research consortium for Rwanda.

## Methods

### Design and tools

This study employed a participatory expert (online supplemental table S1 and online supplemental figure S1) consensus approach using an adapted Delphi method:34 a structured, iterative process involving multiple rounds of questionnaires and controlled feedback to achieve expert consensus on the ranking of zoonotic diseases based on predefined criteria. In this study, questions about zoonotic diseases were presented slightly differently across the two survey rounds. Because real-time consensus voting was used, we refer to this approach as an adapted Delphi methodology.

The criteria included (a) local disease burden, (b) likelihood of causing an outbreak with high morbidity or mortality, (c) potential for socioeconomic disruption,89 (d) availability of robust diagnostic tools,6 (e) transboundary risk and (f) availability of effective preventive and control measures.1935–37

### Participants

For logistical reasons, 25 participants were invited to participate from the national reference and public regulatory bodies, the Ministry of Environment, the Ministry of Agriculture, the Ministry of Health, the Rwanda Agriculture and Animal Resources Development Board, Rwanda Wildlife Conservation Authority and Gorilla Doctors, the Rwanda Environment Management Authority and academic institutions (University of Rwanda and the University of Global Health Equity) (online supplemental table S1 and online supplemental figure S1). In addition, there were delegates representing development partners, software developers, technical and financial support agencies such as iGiTREE, GIZ and USAID. Additional cross-sector technical support and transdisciplinary knowledge were provided by the Pan-African OH Institute. Henceforth, these will be referred to as partners (online supplemental table S1).

The planning phase was led by the OH Directorate of the RBC, with support from the Digital OH Laboratory at Roslin Institute, to develop and generate the survey materials (see online supplemental file 1). For example, list all zoonotic diseases reported in Rwanda and in the neighbouring countries and collate disease characteristics into the prioritisation matrix. Furthermore, resource mobilisation for subsequent steps, as well as sending the survey materials to the relevant stakeholders. This process also aimed to raise awareness and strengthen intersectoral communication.

First round survey material, including the criteria and survey tool, was electronically mailed to partners (see online supplemental file 1). This was then followed by an in-person workshop.

The second round survey was done at a workshop inviting the above partners, ensuring broad and balanced representation of the relevant partners and institutions across the country. Following the first round, we used Mentimeter,38 which is an interactive digital tool that facilitates real-time anonymised surveys and voting during workshops. Furthermore, partners presented evidence on zoonotic diseases, including current surveillance data (figure 1B) and AMR as a use case for data integration needed for zoonotic disease surveillance. This was then followed by a real-time second-round survey and discussions on disease impact (see online supplemental file 1).

### Consensus voting

In the final session of the workshop, partners anonymously ranked the diseases using Mentimeter, which allowed real-time consensus of views. Here, we expected that the voting would be informed by expertise and the above processes.

To analyse and interpret the results, R statistical software was used to summarise disease risk rankings based on expert responses, which were categorised into three levels: ‘low’, ‘medium’ and ‘high’. It is noteworthy that while funders and software developers contributed their perspectives during discussions, they did not participate in the voting process.

### Consensus threshold

Another slight difference from the Delphi process is the incorporation of structured discussion and real-time anonymous voting. Here, consensus was determined through the convergence of partner rankings following facilitated discussion rather than a predefined numerical agreement threshold.

### Ethics

This study involved consultation with professional partners to achieve expert consensus on zoonotic disease prioritisation and did not involve the collection of personal health information, biological samples or research involving patients or members of the public. Participation was voluntary, responses were anonymised during voting, and data were analysed in aggregate. Based on the nature of the activity, the absence of human subjects research procedures and the use of expert stakeholders acting in their professional roles, the exercise was exempt from ethical review requirements under national research ethics guidelines.

## Results and discussion

### Prioritisation of zoonotic diseases in Rwanda

Within the changing global and local policy landscape (figure 1C), we aimed to develop a priority list of zoonotic diseases to support effective resource allocation in Rwanda. Therefore, the prioritisation exercise was based on (a) the local burden of disease, (b) the likelihood to cause an outbreak resulting in high morbidity/mortality, (c) socioeconomic disruption,89 (d) availability of robust diagnostic tools,6 (e) transboundary risk and (f) availability of effective preventive and control measures.1935–37 Figure 2A shows the ranking of zoonotic diseases based on their case fatality rates, using literature collated in the first round (see online supplemental file 1). This is contrasted with expert perspectives on the utility and financing of OH in controlling zoonotic diseases in Rwanda (figure 2B). The prioritisation of zoonotic diseases was based on their impact and potential risk to societal mobility, healthcare systems and economic disruption (figure 3A). This is contrasted with the consensus ranking of the same diseases using Mentimeter (figure 3B). Viruses such as Ebola, Marburg and chikungunya were identified as posing the greatest risk to societal mobility. For healthcare system impact, Marburg, Nipah and Ebola ranked highest, with similar trends observed for transboundary risks. However, considering all factors and contextual discussions, the consensus ranking identified rabies, influenza, Marburg and Ebola as the top-ranked viral zoonoses in Rwanda. Zoonotic/bovine tuberculosis, brucellosis and salmonellosis emerged as the top-ranked bacterial diseases (figure 3B). Zoonotic/Bovine tuberculosis was largely viewed as having a high impact on healthcare, while salmonellosis, though ranked third, was generally considered a medium-to low-risk disease across the prioritisation matrix (figure 3A). Among protozoal/parasitic diseases, toxoplasmosis and trypanosomiasis ranked highest (figures 3A and 4A), with Toxoplasmosis noted for its higher transboundary risk and potential impact on societal mobility (figure 3A). These prioritisation outcomes align with surveillance insights (online supplemental figure S2), which consider disease risk by location, host, surveillance context and cross-border potential (online supplemental figure S2). Notably, four of the five top zoonotic diseases have been reported in neighbouring countries.

### Differences between the first and second survey rounds

The first-round survey indicates that the majority of experts did not provide an overall risk rank for the zoonotic disease (figure 4A). The few who responded indicated that bacterial zoonoses are generally of low to medium risk; some of these indicated that brucellosis and anthrax had an overall high risk. This information gap was filled by the second round survey using Mentimeter (figure 4B). However, the limited response to this suggests a potential lack of broad awareness of the impact; indeed, when experts were asked the biggest challenge facing OH in their sector, ‘Limited capacity building’ emerged as the top concern (figures 4A and 5A). Follow-up discussions revealed that while training programmes exist, they often remain siloed within sectors, limiting opportunities for knowledge sharing and trust-building across sectors.

### Interpretation of prioritisation findings

#### Priority bacterial zoonotic diseases in Rwanda

Bacterial zoonoses are generally ranked as low to medium priority, with only brucellosis and anthrax classified as high risk. This is likely due to their potential to disrupt economic activity, restrict social mobility (anthrax quarantines) and pose diagnostic challenges.262739 Consensus identified zoonotic tuberculosis, brucellosis and salmonellosis as the top priority bacterial zoonotic diseases for surveillance and control strategy development. This order of priority is slightly different from the 2021 prioritisation and rankings for most of the neighbouring countries.262739 For example, most African countries rank anthrax caused by Bacillus anthracis as the top priority bacterial zoonotic disease, followed by brucellosis.4041 Whether this genuinely represents an alignment with the true population impact in those countries or biosecurity concerns for the funding agencies remains an open question. Nevertheless, the finding urges the need to strengthen surveillance and implementation research to sustainably monitor cross-border dynamics of these diseases. Implementation of the International Health Regulations (2005) in the country is indeed a core part of the national preparedness and prevention strategy.184142

#### Priority viral zoonotic diseases in Rwanda

The highest-ranked risks to societal mobility are viruses, including Ebola, Marburg and chikungunya, while for impact on healthcare, Marburg, Nipah and Ebola, the same was true for transboundary risk. However, taking into account all the factors and the contextualisation of discussion, the consensus shows that rabies, influenza, Marburg and Ebola were the top ranked viral zoonoses in Rwanda. This ranking generally agrees with both the first prioritisation exercise and the outcomes of prioritisation for most of the neighbouring countries.262739 This indicates that these diseases are posing a serious threat to human, animal and environmental safety and health in the region. Here the discussion revealed that although significant resources were allocated to investigating viral haemorrhagic fevers (VHFs), no confirmed human cases had been reported as of July 2024. The prioritisation exercise preceded the September 2024 Marburg virus disease outbreak and may have supported preparedness planning. However, no causal relationship between the exercise and the effectiveness of the outbreak response can be inferred from the present study.43–49

#### Priority protozoal and parasitic zoonotic diseases in Rwanda

Toxoplasmosis and trypanosomiasis were the top-ranked parasitic zoonoses. Parasitic/protozoal zoonotic diseases generally represent low economic disruptions, and it is noteworthy that the list for zoonotic metazoans was combined with protozoan zoonoses, and experts recommended a separation going forward to enhance inclusivity of these lists.

### Pathways to operationalisation

#### AMR control as a use case for OH operationalisation in Rwanda

The control of AMR provides some insights on how OH could be embedded across sectors.29 Rwanda’s AMR National Action Plan (NAP, 2020–2024) was established on an OH foundation,29 recognising the diverse pool of experts needed to support the NAP. When experts were asked if OH was critical to implementing the AMR NAP, 84% agreed, with caveats on its sustainability given limitations in funding (figure 5B). Experts cited the 2024 AMR surveillance Operational Plan, which was well-costed but for which only a small percentage of resources have actually been secured and allocated.29 The control of AMR might represent an excellent use case for the transition from the OH theorem to practice, but it too has challenges.50

### Challenges to OH implementation

#### Funding

Financing OH activities was the top-ranked challenge for ensuring sustainability, and here the effort needed (what we finance) and potential economic impact (figure 5B) are central to getting political buy-in. Ebola, Marburg and chikungunya were identified as the viral diseases with the highest potential for economic disruption (figure 5A). Brucellosis, zoonotic tuberculosis and salmonellosis were also seen to pose moderate economic risks, while parasitic/protozoal diseases were generally perceived to have low economic impact. The results in figure 5B highlight that significant effort is needed to sustain collaborative partnerships, engage policy makers and funders, train the next generation of OH practitioners, establish equitable pathogen access and benefit-sharing models and promote data integration and sharing. With recent cuts to global health funding,51 it is more important than ever for countries like Rwanda to increase domestic investment for OH operationalisation.

#### OH community of practice

The emergence, transmission and maintenance of these zoonotic diseases are shaped by interactions between the environment, pathogens and hosts, both human and animal, underscoring the need for an OH approach to their control.51 However, a lack of a well-defined community of practice has emerged as a significant limitation to the utility of OH in Rwanda. The absence of joint capacity-building initiatives, with current efforts heavily reliant on donor funding, is a challenge. Notably, Rwanda has historically not been included in major OH initiatives, including the initial phase of the Fleming Fund, which limits contextualisation of methods, capacity building and opportunities to benchmark. This is where local financing can help catalyse skills, method and tool development for OH operationalisation. Indeed, limited funding emerged as one of the biggest challenges to zoonotic disease control based on OH approaches, greatly limiting contextualised strategic disease control.50 The current partial transition to OH creates four parallel systems in human, animal and environmental health characterised by resource loss, duplication and inefficiency. Without alignment, the gap between funded initiatives and local relevance will continue to grow, undermining OH goals.

The study has several limitations. First, although the expert panel included representation from multiple sectors relevant to OH implementation, the apparent absence of female participants may have limited the diversity of perspectives captured during the consensus process. Future prioritisation exercises should aim for more balanced gender representation alongside sectoral and disciplinary diversity to ensure broader inclusivity in decision-making.

#### OH data integration frameworks

Decision-making in the strategic planning and implementation of OH strategy is mainly evidence-based; therefore, it depends on data integration to support the generation of reliable evidence to inform policy makers and guide implementers. However, this is a challenging and widely recognised bottleneck for transitioning from theory to practice.52

Rwanda has established an OH Electronic Disease Surveillance and Response (OH_eIDSR) hosted on the District Health Information System2 (DHIS2). Although DHIS2 is widely used, it is not currently developed to enable integration across sectors because there is no harmonisation of processes and records, inherent differences in ethical, legal and social boundaries across OH and records, the existence of siloed views and practices, and, fundamentally, a lack of trust across sectors. One potential solution for data sharing could be the digital OH framework as described by Redman-White and colleagues.50 It is worth noting that Rwanda aims to pilot its implementation.

### Policy implications

The OH framework is often presented to policy makers as a logical process guide that encourages collective contributions to the design, implementation and evaluation of interventions. Implicit in this presentation is the assumption that logic will drive decision-making. However, political decision-making is far more complex and often centres around the cost of interventions. We argue that instead of solely focusing on the costs of implementing OH, it should also be presented as the cost of not implementing it. This approach highlights the missed opportunities for interventions and underscores the potential negative consequences of failing to adopt an OH approach.

The risk of zoonotic diseases in Rwanda changes due to increasing population density, migration and increasing contact with wildlife. The focus should shift from cost-benefit of investment to the benefits arising from healthy ecosystems, fauna and human communities. The alternative would be endangered biodiversity and rampant disease outbreaks, which inherently undermine the tourism sector, a key source of national revenue, highlighting the broader value of investing in OH approaches.

## Conclusion and recommendations

Despite Rwanda’s pioneering initiative to institutionalise OH, prevention and control of zoonotic diseases remain inadequate due to ecological, socioeconomic and systematic challenges. Our prioritisation exercise has not only revealed the rank of diseases but also the major challenges limiting the implementation of the OH strategy, including the lack of cross-sector system integration. Without a prioritisation to guide investments, there has been poor integration and duplication of efforts, leading to ineffective interventions. With prioritisation, we recommend the following:

- An integrated capacity-building strategy to nurture a community of practice that supports and evaluates OH strategies.

- Cross-sector trust-building ventures and activities, for instance, the development of dedicated OH research hubs to track OH metrics as evidence to influence policy and funding.

- Develop an OH operationalisation investment strategy following the World Bank’s guidance, focusing on increased local funding to ensure sustainable operationalisation of OH beyond zoonotic diseases.

- To contextualise a digital OH framework fit for Rwanda’s current and future challenges as the foundation for public health decision-making.

- Encourage regional cooperation on broader livestock diseases to reduce the transboundary risk associated with zoonotic diseases
