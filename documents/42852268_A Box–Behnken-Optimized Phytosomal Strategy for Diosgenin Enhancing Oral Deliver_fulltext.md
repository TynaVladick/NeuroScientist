# A Box–Behnken-Optimized Phytosomal Strategy for Diosgenin: Enhancing Oral Delivery and Antipsoriatic Efficacy in a Preclinical Model

## Abstract

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by excessive keratinocyte proliferation and immune dysregulation, affecting patients’ quality of life. Diosgenin (DG) is a natural steroidal sapogenin with potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. These effects could help treat psoriatic symptoms; however, poor solubility and limited dissolution limit its therapeutic potential. The current study aimed to develop diosgenin-loaded phytosomes (DG-PS) using the Box–Behnken design and to evaluate their physicochemical characteristics and antipsoriatic efficacy. The DG-PS were prepared using the thin-film hydration technique. The DG-PS were optimized with respect to phosphatidylcholine concentration, temperature, and evaporator rotation speed. The optimal formulation of DG-PS-15 showed a particle size of 201.1 nm, a polydispersity index of 0.4027, and a zeta potential of −36.87 mV. Characterization of DG-PS-15 by differential scanning calorimetry, Fourier-transform infrared, and X-ray diffraction demonstrated successful complexation with phospholipids, enhanced amorphous characteristics, and stable molecular interactions. Meanwhile, DG-PS-15 exhibited higher solubility, sustained in vitro drug release, and an entrapment efficiency of 68.55%. In vivo activity testing in an imiquimod-induced psoriatic mouse model revealed that DG-PS-15 at a 50 mg/kg dose significantly reduced erythema, scaling, and skin thickness. Histopathological and biochemical analyses further supported the enhanced antipsoriatic efficacy and antioxidant activity of DG following phytosomal complexation. These findings suggest that phytosomal complexation may be a valuable strategy to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of DG, thereby improving treatment outcomes and patient well-being.

## Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis that affects around 2–3% of the world’s population and has a negative impact on general well-being. Clinically, it is characterized by erythematous, scaly plaques, primarily resulting from keratinocyte hyperproliferation, aberrant immune responses, and dysregulated cytokine signaling. Current therapeutic options, such as the use of corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and biologics, have disadvantages like adverse effects, high costs, drug resistance, and limited long-term efficacy. These difficulties have led to increasing demand for plant-derived compounds as they possess safer pharmacological profiles, multiple mechanisms of action, and sustained efficacy.

DG, a steroidal sapogenin predominantly found in Dioscorea species and fenugreek, has exhibited significant anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and antiproliferative properties in various preclinical models. Its mechanisms of action include downregulating NF-κB, COX-2, and cytokines that promote inflammation, which are critical mediators in the pathogenesis of psoriasis. DG’s limited solubility and permeability compromise systemic bioavailability, necessitating the development of an effective drug delivery system to overcome these pharmacokinetic barriers. ,

Phytosomes, a novel class of lipid-based vesicular carriers, have emerged as a promising approach to enhance the bioavailability of poorly soluble phytoconstituents. In phytosomes, the active plant compound forms a complex with phospholipids via hydrogen bonding, thereby improving stability, membrane affinity, and absorption characteristics. This delivery system has demonstrated enhanced therapeutic potential of phytocompounds both in oral and dermatological applications. ,

In this work, Box–Behnken design (BBD), a statistical response surface method used to optimize formulation factors, is employed to prepare diosgenin-loaded phytosomes (DG-PS). The optimized formulation was evaluated for particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), zeta potential (ZP), and % entrapment efficiency (EE). Furthermore, the antipsoriatic potential of the optimized phytosomal formulation was assessed through in vivo studies using the imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis model in Swiss albino mice. The purpose of this study is to improve therapeutic outcomes and contribute to public health care in the treatment of psoriasis by developing a novel, efficient DG delivery method that addresses PK issues. ,

## Materials and Methods

### Materials

DG was sourced from Combi Blocks, Inc., USA, and phosphatidylcholine was obtained from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Maharashtra, India. The cholesterol was purchased from Nice Chemicals Pvt. Ltd. Cochin, Kerala. For solvent purposes, chloroform was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific India Pvt. Ltd. Mumbai.

### Development and Optimization of DG Phytosomes (DGPs)

DGPs are prepared by using the traditional lipid hydration method. At first, a mix of DG (100 mg per batch), phosphatidylcholine (PC) (0.15–0.25 g), and cholesterol (8.5–14.1 mg) was dissolved in chloroform (10–30 mL) in a round-bottom flask, and then the molar ratio of PC to cholesterol stayed at 9:1. After that, the chloroform was evaporated and the remaining solvent was removed under vacuum at 55 °C for about 10 min. Cholesterol was included to improve the particle strength, prevent drug leakage, and stop particle clumping. − Finally, the dried lipid film was hydrated with distilled water to a final volume of 100 mL while stirring continuously for 1 h. The phytosomal formulation was optimized using Design-Expert 13 employing BBD. The PC concentration (X 1) (0.15–0.25% w/v), temperature (X 2) (40–60 °C), and rotary flask evaporator rotation speed (80–120 rpm) were identified as independent variables based on the preliminary risk assessment. The impacts of these factors were then evaluated on the critical dependent variables particle size (Y 1), PDI (Y 2), and ZP (Y 3). − Three independent factors at three levels were selected, as shown in Figure .

### DG-Loaded Phytosomal Complexes: Structural Validation

#### Characterization by Differential Scanning Calorimetry

Using a Shimadzu DSC-60 instrument, phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, DG, a physical mixture, and the optimized phytosomes were analyzed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) to determine their thermodynamic properties. In each metal dish, 2 mg of the test powder was placed. Under nitrogen, thermal analysis profiles are generated by heating the sample from 25 to 400 °C at 10 °C min–1. The peak transition onset temperatures of the samples were compared to verify the development of phytosomal complexes. −

#### FT-IR Spectral Analysis

The formation of the phytosomal complex was confirmed by comparing the FTIR spectra of the phytosomal complex with those of placebo samples. The FTIR spectrum was recorded using a PerkinElmer Spectrum-1 FTIR spectrophotometer. Before being compressed into KBr discs, the samples were ground to a fine powder using an agate mortar. Specimens were scanned between 4000 and 400 cm–1 with a spectral resolution of 4 cm–1. Multiple spectra were collected and compared. −

#### X-ray Diffraction Analysis

The crystallinity of samples was assessed using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 ADVANCE XRD, Germany) with Cu/K radiation (40 kV, 40 mA) in the 2θ scanning mode. Sample diffraction patterns were collected over the 10–90° range with a step size of 0.029649°. The physical mixture and the optimized phytosomes (DG-PS-15) were analyzed by XRD. −

### Characterization of DG-PS

#### Solubility and Partition Coefficient

Solubility of DG and DG-PS-15 in aqueous and organic solvents, as well as the n-octanol/water partition coefficient, were determined and studied. Phyto-phospholipid complexes have higher lipophilicity and hydrophilicity than active ingredients. −

#### In-Vitro Drug Diffusion Studies

The comparative drug release behavior of DG-PS-15 and the pure DG dispersion was investigated using the dialysis membrane technique. A cellophane membrane (300 kDa) was presoaked in distilled water for 24 h before being installed in Franz-type diffusion cells with a receptor-compartment capacity of 33.2 mL. The receptor compartment was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C and continuously agitated at 600 rpm throughout the trials. 1 mL samples have been collected from the receiver compartment in regular intervals and replaced with an equivalent volume of fresh receptor fluid. At 225 nm, the quantity of DG released was measured spectrophotometrically and reported. −

#### Stability Testing of DG-PS-15

The formulated phytosomes were subjected to a stability investigation. For 30 days, the DG-PS-15 were stored at 4 ± 2 °C in a refrigerator and at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), in a stability chamber with 65 ± 5% RH. The stability of the phytosomes was assessed by comparing the initial particle size, PDI, ZP, and EE with those obtained from samples collected after 30 days. −

#### Antipsoriatic Effects of DG-PS in Animals

The Animal Ethical Committee of the Central Animal Facility at the Arulmigu Kalasalingam College of Pharmacy in Srivilliputtur, Tamil Nadu, India, approved all study protocols in accordance with the regulations established by the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA, Government of India) (approval number: 509/02/C/CPCSEA/2018). The Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) authorized the protocol for this study with permission number AKCP/IAEC/02/18-19. For the duration of the study, Swiss albino mice of either sex, weighing 25–30 g, were housed in a pathogen-free environment. −

#### IMQ Induced a Psoriatic Mouse Model

Mice with IMQ-induced skin inflammation were used to evaluate the efficacy of DG-PS-15 in treating psoriasis. To assist research, the 36 animals were divided into six groups of six. Group 1 is normal control, administered normal saline (10 mL/kg, p.o.); group 2 was a placebo (negative disease control); group 3 is standard, administered methotrexate (1 mg/kg/bd.wt., p.o.); group 4 was administered DG-PS-15 (50 mg/kg/bd.wt., p.o.); group 5 was administered DG-PS-15 (25 mg/kg/bd.wt., p.o.); and group 6 was administered pure DG dispersion (DG + 0.5% sodium carboxy methyl cellulose solution containing 0.1% Tween 80) (100 mg/kg/bd.wt., p.o.). All animals had their backs shaved before receiving a topical application of 5% (IMQ) cream (Aldara; 3 M Pharmaceuticals) containing 62.5 mg of IMQ for seven consecutive days, followed by standard treatment. IMQ-induced psoriasis was applied topically for 7 days. From the eighth day, treatment has started (eighth day is taken as “0” day of treatment) and performed for 10 days. End points are collected on the 10th day of treatment. To diagnose psoriasis, skin redness, scaling, and ear and skin thickness were measured. − The severity of psoriasis-like skin inflammation was evaluated using the psoriasis area and severity index (PASI) scoring system. Three parameterserythema, scaling, and skin thicknesswere each scored on a 0–4 scale (0 = none, 1 = slight, 2 = moderate, 3 = marked, 4 = very marked). For each mouse, the average PASI score was calculated by adding the scores for redness, scaling, and thickness and dividing by 3, yielding a score range of 0–4. All assessments were performed in a blinded manner to minimize observational bias. A grading system for reddish staining was utilized to quantify the degree of erythema. With six animals per group, the experimental findings were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and presented as mean ± SEM, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. ,

#### Biochemical Analysis

After 10 days of treatment, mice were euthanised under phenobarbitone anesthesia and blood samples were obtained for biochemical analysis. The amount of superoxide dismutase (SOD) required to prevent a 50% decrease in nitroblue tetrazolium reduction was calculated. The degree of catalase (CAT) activity in the blood was determined by measuring the rate of hydrogen peroxide degradation at 240 nm over 5 min. In addition, we measured the levels of the SOD, CAT, GSH, and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) and compared them with those of the disease control group. −

#### Histological Examination

On the 10th day of the experiment, skin from each mouse group was collected, embedded in paraffin, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, and sectioned longitudinally at 10 μm. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining reveals histopathological abnormalities, including nuclear, cytoplasmic, and epidermal alterations (e.g., epidermal hyperplasia and keratinocyte proliferation), as well as extracellular matrix features. The photographs were taken using a bright-field microscope at 200× magnification, with a 50 μm scale bar. −

### DG-Loaded Phytosomal Complexes: Structural Validation

#### Characterization by Differential Scanning Calorimetry

In DSC studies, DG exhibited a broad endothermic peak, with a melting onset at 213.9 °C. Cholesterol displayed an endothermic peak at 151.0 °C. Phosphatidylcholine showed a broad endothermic peak at 340.8 °C. The physical mixture of the drug, cholesterol, and phosphatidylcholine exhibited major peaks at 142.8 and 330.2 °C, indicating no interactions among the components. The phytosomes exhibited an endothermic peak at 133.4 °C (Figure ), which is lower than that of the physical mixture. The disappearance of the classic DG melting peak and the emergence of a new peak indicate successful interaction between DG and the phospholipid, resulting in a novel complex with unique thermal properties and complete solubilization of DG within the PC matrix.

#### FT-IR Spectral Analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was used to verify the formation of the phytosomal complex and to identify potential interactions among DG and PC. The spectra of the individual components, their physical mixture, and the optimized phytosomal formulation (DG-PS-15) are illustrated in Figure . The spectrum of PC (A) showed characteristic peaks at 3449 cm–1 corresponding to O–H stretching, along with C–H stretching vibrations at 2922 cm–1 and 2852 cm–1. A strong absorption at 1735 cm–1 indicated the CO stretching of the ester group, while peaks at 1457 cm–1 and 1052 cm–1 were attributed to CH2 bending and PO stretching, respectively. Cholesterol (B) exhibited bands at 2849 cm–1 (C–H stretching), 1376 cm–1 (C–H deformation), and 1021 cm–1 (C–O stretching), confirming the presence of aliphatic chains and hydroxyl groups. The FTIR spectrum of DG (C) displayed major bands at 3449 cm–1 (O–H stretching), 2950 cm–1 and 2846 cm–1 (C–H stretching), 1455 cm–1 (CH2 bending), 1171 cm–1 (C–O stretching), and 962 cm–1 (indicative of the saponin ring structure). −

*[Figure] FT-IR spectra of phosphatidylcholine (PC) (A), diosgenin (DG) (B), cholesterol (C), DG-PC physical mixture (D), and DG-PS-15 (E) showing compatibility between them and formation of the phytosomal complex.*

In the physical mixture (D), the major peaks corresponding to the individual components were retained with minimal shifts, indicating no significant interaction between the elements in their unformulated state. However, the optimized phytosome formulation (DG-PS-15) (E) exhibited significant spectral shifts and intensity changes, indicating the formation of new intermolecular interactions. The broad O–H stretching band at 3449 cm–1 was reduced in intensity, suggesting hydrogen bonding between DG and phospholipids. Additionally, the shifts observed from 1052 cm–1 to 1020 cm–1 and from 976 cm–1 to 966 cm–1 supported the involvement of phosphate and hydroxyl functional groups in complex formation. Based on the FTIR data, the formation of the phytosomal complex is confirmed by a reduction in O–H peak intensity and by specific peak shifts, indicating strong intermolecular interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding) between DG and PC that are absent in the physical mixture.

#### Analysis of Entrapment Efficiency and Drug Loading

The EE of the phytosome formulations DG-PS-15 and DG-PS-16 was 68.55 ± 3.8% and 48.24 ± 1.83%, respectively. This indicates that the method used to prepare phytosomes is consistent and reliable. The DL is 21.4 ± 3.6% and 15 ± 5.2% for DG-PS-15 and DG-PS-16, respectively.

#### Calculation of Relative Yield

The percentage yield of the phytosome preparations was used to assess the practicality of the formulation process. The yield was expressed as the percentage of the recovered phytosomal complex with respect to the total weight of the starting material. The formulation DG-PS-15 exhibited a relatively high percentage yield of 89.4%, thereby confirming successful drug entrapment and negligible drug escape during the process of thin-film hydration and sonication. In contrast, the yield for formulation DG-PS-16 (82.7%) was slightly lower and may be due to reduced lipid–drug interaction or small losses during transfer and recovery steps. The higher yield observed in DG-PS-15 suggests better compatibility and stabilization within the formulation, confirming its suitability for further development.

#### Solubility and Partition Coefficient

Pure DG was poorly soluble in water; solubility in distilled water was 0.42 ± 0.03 mg/mL, whereas in ethanol, it was much higher (5.86 ± 0.09 mg/mL). Solubility has been significantly increased for DG-PS-15 (3.15 ± 0.07 mg/mL), which may be due to improved dispersion and amphiphilic properties resulting from phospholipid complexation. Enhancement of aqueous solubility suggests a superior bioavailability potential of the phytosomal system.

Additionally, the partition coefficient (log P) of DG in the n-octanol/water system was definitely found to be 3.97 ± 0.04, which means that the compound is highly lipophilic and has low partition in the aqueous phase. Upon development of the DG-PS-15, the log P of the preparation decreased to 2.89 ± 0.06, which is indicative of a more favorable hydrophilic–lipophilic balance. This change is a direct consequence of the improved compatibility with biological membranes and the increased drug transport capacity. The phytosome pathway, as the optimal strategy for improving the physicochemical properties of DG, is supported by the observed increases in both solubility and partition behavior.

#### Antipsoriatic Effects of DG-PS-15 in Animal Models

Mice with psoriasis-like cutaneous inflammation induced by IMQ were used to evaluate the antipsoriatic effects of DG-PS-15. After topical IMQ was applied, the mice were treated with DG-PS-15 by per-oral administration at 25 mg/kg (Group 5) and 50 mg/kg (Group 4). In parallel, a pure DG dispersion dose of 100 mg/kg (Group 6) was given, and methotrexate at 1 mg/kg (Group 3) was used as the standard control. Severity was assessed using the PASI score (PASI), which includes erythema, scaling, and skin thickening. During the first 7 days of IMQ application, all treated animals developed erythema, scaling, and thickened skin, and psoriatic lesions started appearing between days 3 and 4. The highest ear thickness, 1.40 mm, was observed in the negative control (Group 2) right at treatment day 0, and it decreased slightly to 1.35 mm by day 10. Meanwhile, the group treated with 50 mg/kg DG-PS-15 (Group 4) had a larger drop, from 1.41 mm on day 0 to 0.64 mm on day 10. Visible inflammation persisted for 2–4 days and then gradually subsided by day 10 (Table ). Similar improvements were observed in mice treated with 25 mg/kg DG-PS-15 (Group 5) and in the pure DG 100 mg/kg group (Group 6). However, the fastest symptom decrease was seen in the methotrexate-treated group (Group 3) and in the 50 mg/kg DG-PS-15 group (Group 4). The PASI score for Group 4 was about three times lower than that of the IMQ-only control group (Group 2), which suggests stronger performance. The normal control mice had no psoriatic-type changes at all. Overall, 50 mg/kg DG-PS-15 produced the most noticeable antipsoriatic impact, as can be seen in Figure . No side effects were seen, and there were no behavioral changes observed in the mice within the tested dose range of 25 to 100 mg/kg body weight, which is in line with the published literature.

#### Histopathology Evaluations

Examination of skin tissue samples collected on day 10 of treatment revealed distinct pathological changes in the treated groups. In the negative disease-control group, marked epidermal hyperplasia, acanthosis, parakeratosis, and nuclear retention were observed in the stratum corneum. These are reduced gradually with treatment using DG-PS. ## Conclusion

The present study successfully optimizes and formulates DG-PS, with DG-PS-15 identified as the best-optimized formulation. DG-PS-15 exhibited favorable physicochemical properties, including a suitable particle size, a uniform size distribution, and a stable surface charge, all of which are prerequisites for enhancing formulation stability and supporting efficient oral delivery of DG. Thorough characterization by DSC, FTIR, and XRD confirmed the successful formulation of stable phytosomal complexes. It was also concluded that these complexes exhibit increased amorphous content and intermolecular interactions. Phytosomal encapsulation significantly improved the solubility and partitioning properties of DG, thereby overcoming its inherent biopharmaceutical limitations. In vitro studies demonstrated sustained drug release, enhancing the formulation’s potential for long-acting therapeutic effects. Morphological observations using SEM and AFM confirmed the formation of monodisperse, spherical particles, while high EE and phytosome yield confirmed the reproducibility of the adopted hydration method. In vivo experiments using an IMQ-induced psoriasis mouse model confirmed the therapeutic efficacy of DG-PS, as evidenced by reductions in erythema, scaling, and epidermal thickening. Biochemical investigations demonstrated that DG-PS enhanced antioxidant defense by reducing oxidative stress-associated biomarkers upon treatment. The histopathological examination showed a considerable reduction of psoriatic features and recovery of skin integrity with treatment using DG-PS. Taken together, these results indicate that DG-PS-15 is a promising phytosomal diosgenin delivery system with improved solubility, favorable physicochemical characteristics, sustained-release behavior, and enhanced antipsoriatic efficacy in the treatment of psoriasis. Subsequent studies will examine molecular mechanisms, assess long-term safety, and enable clinical translation of these promising DG-PS formulations.
