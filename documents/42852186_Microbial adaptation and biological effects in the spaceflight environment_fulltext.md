# Microbial adaptation and biological effects in the spaceflight environment

## Abstract

With the advancement of space exploration, space microbiology has become an important research area. Microorganisms show remarkable adaptive plasticity under extreme space environments. Space‑derived strains not only pose potential biosafety risks but also serve as valuable biotechnological resources. This review focuses on microbial phenotypic alterations, including colony morphology, biofilm formation, secondary metabolism, pathogenicity, and antibiotic resistance. These changes are mainly driven by genomic instability, horizontal gene transfer, and cellular pathway reprogramming. Here we uniquely integrate phenotypic, mechanistic, and application-level perspectives to provide a unified framework for understanding microbial adaptation in spaceflight environments.

## Introduction

Since humans first dispatched living organisms into space in the mid-twentieth century, space microbiology has evolved into a cornerstone of space life sciences. Following the beginning of the twenty-first century, the establishment of the International Space Station (ISS) has provided a stable platform for long-term space microbiology research. Deep space exploration necessitates that spacecraft function as isolated ecosystems subjected to an extreme composite environment defined by microgravity, heightened radiation, and prolonged confinement (Bijlani et al. 2021). This unique environment not only threatens astronaut health and equipment integrity but also imposes constant selective pressure on the resident microbiota, thereby shaping their growth, metabolism, and adaptive responses.

As adaptable pioneers within this engineered ecosystem, microorganisms play a fundamental role, making the comprehension of their adaptation to spaceflight paramount. Historically, research in this field has evolved through several developmental epochs. Beginning in the 1960s, U.S. Apollo missions and Soviet biosatellite programs dispatched microbial samples into space, providing preliminary variation of their survivability under conditions of weightlessness, vacuum, and cosmic radiation (Bucker et al. 1974, 1975; Berry and Volz 1979). By the 1990s, investigations conducted aboard Apollo 16, Spacelab 1, and the Long Duration Exposure Facility offered a systematic evaluation of the survival and mutagenic potential of Bacillus subtilis spores under extreme spaceflight conditions (Horneck 1993). These studies demonstrated that, provided there is adequate protection, spores possess the capacity to survive for years in space, thereby providing key empirical corroboration for the “panspermia” hypothesis. Entering the twenty-first century, the sustained operation of the International Space Station (ISS) has underpinned extensive space-exposure and simulated microgravity (SMG) investigations involving bacteria and fungi, serving as an unprecedented, stable on-orbit platform for long-term space microbiology research. In recent years, the synergistic integration of multi-omics technologies has empowered researchers to elucidate the complex molecular response networks of microorganisms subjected to spaceflight environments. For instance, while microgravity induces broad regulatory shifts in the expression of genes related to signal transduction, stress response, membrane transport, and energy metabolism (Padgen et al. 2020; Su et al. 2021; Lozzi et al. 2025), radiation exposure significantly augments DNA repair pathways and antioxidant systems (Chan et al. 2007; Nielsen et al. 2021).

There are three key reasons to study space microbiology: first, it is essential for risk assessment and the mitigation of threats to crew health and spacecraft systems (Voorhies et al. 2019; Morrison et al. 2021; Simões and Antunes 2021; Minoretti et al. 2024); second, it provides a unique model system for probing foundational principles of adaption under extreme, multifactorial selection; and third, it unveils novel biotechnological potential, as the metabolic reprogramming induced by spaceflight may unlock unique biosynthetic capabilities for in-situ resource utilization and manufacturing (Koehle et al. 2023). Despite these advances and recognized implications, there remains a critical need for a comprehensive biosynthesis that links microbial phenotypic adaptations to their adaptive drivers and practical applications are needed.

This review aims to bridge this knowledge gap by synthesizing current understanding of how the spaceflight environment modulates microbial phenotypes and steers adaptive evolution. Initially, we delineate the observed multidimensional phenotypic changes. Subsequently, we interrogate the driving mechanisms, including genomic instability, horizontal gene transfer, and cellular pathway reprogramming. Finally, we explore the implications of these adaptations for both risk mitigation and biotechnology, highlighting how this knowledge can inform the design of synthetic biology solutions for future space exploration (Figure 1). By providing this integrated perspective, this work seeks to inform strategies for planetary protection, crew health, and the development of sustainable biological support systems for long-term interplanetary missions. ## The spaceflight environment and its selective pressures

### Microgravity

The microgravity environment (~10−3−10−6 g) (Herranz et al. 2013) primarily manifests as a persistent low-fluid-shear state. Implicit in this state is the perturbation of fundamental physical processes—such as fluid convection, sedimentation, and mass transport—which subsequently destabilize the nutrient and chemical gradients surrounding microbial cells. Such physical constraints exert profound downstream effects on microbial physiology and behaviour (Klaus et al. 1998; Hammond et al. 2000; Huang et al. 2018). The increase in biofilm formation is considered to be closely associated with restricted diffusion of nutrients and signaling molecules under low shear conditions. Furthermore, low shear reduces the detachment force on surface-attached cells, thereby facilitating cell adhesion and the accumulation of extracellular matrix (Sharma and Curtis 2022). This not only enhances the ability of microorganisms to adhere to equipment surfaces but may also lead to increased difficulty in cleaning and disinfection. In addition to experimental observations, computational modeling has been applied to understand microbial community behavior under microgravity. Computational modeling, such as the CAMDLES framework integrating computational fluid dynamics and discrete element method (CFD-DEM), has further revealed how colocalization of strains enhances metabolite exchange under microgravity (An and Lee 2022).

### Radiation

Space radiation from galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events can elicit direct DNA damage (Patel et al. 2025), or indirect oxidative stress, further exacerbating cellular injury. These types of radiation primarily affect organisms through two mechanisms. The first is the direct deposition of energy into macromolecules such as DNA and proteins, inducing structural lesions such as cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and 6–4 photoproducts between nucleic acid bases, thereby interfering with DNA replication and transcription (Hanawalt and Spivak 2008). The second is indirect damage through the generation of free radicals, which attack cellular components, including proteins, lipids, and cell walls, producing biological effects (Lushchak 2001). Oxidative stress is considered a core challenge for microorganisms in space, and bacteria mitigate such damage by upregulating the synthesis of sulfur-containing amino acids (e.g. methionine and cysteine) and modulating the metabolism of arginine and proline (Sharma and Curtis 2022). Lushchak (2001) systematically summarized the three-tiered protection mechanisms in bacteria: preventing ROS generation, scavenging ROS via superoxide dismutase and catalase, and repairing oxidative damage. Notably, the OxyR and SoxRS regulatory systems sense hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anions, respectively, and precisely control antioxidant gene expression through redox-sensitive proteins. These mechanisms provide an important theoretical basis for understanding the survival and adaptation of spaceflight strains.

### Confined environment and multifactorial interactions

The confined cabin environment, characterized by limited biological sources, elevated CO2 concentrations, and controlled humidity cycles, shapes an ecosystem with low diversity and high selective pressure, continuously affecting microbial community structure and function. Although the various modules of the ISS share similar controlled environmental conditions, their surface microbial community structures exhibit significant module-specific characteristics. Through analysis of 803 surface samples collected from nine US Orbital Segment modules, it was found that module usage pattern is the primary factor driving differences in microbial composition. For example, marked differences exist between the Japanese Experiment Module, primarily used for experimental operations, and the adjacent JLP module, used for personal hygiene (Salido et al. 2025).

The interaction of microgravity, radiation, and confinement makes a microbial ecology unique to space, driving both genotypic changes and multidimensional phenotypic adaptations affecting virulence, biofilm formation, metabolism, and antibiotic resistance.

## Effects of the spaceflight environment on microbial phenotypic characteristics

### Effects of spaceflight/SMG on fundamental microbial phenotypes

#### Colony morphology and biofilm formation

As the most intuitive phenotypic features, colony morphology and biofilm formation reflect microbial strategies for cell–cell interaction, surface attachment, and resilience against environmental stress. Research conducted both aboard the ISS and underground-based SMG has shown significant alterations in the growth rate, biofilm-forming capacity, and cellular morphology of various bacteria (Nickerson et al. 2000; Kim et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2018) (Table 1). A notable and frequently observed change is the enhancement of biofilm formation, primarily attributed to the constrained diffusion of nutrients and signaling molecules in the low-fluid-shear environment (Sharma and Curtis 2022). Simultaneously, the reduction in shear forces decreases the detachment pressure on surface-adhered cells, further promoting attachment and the accumulation of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). Under these conditions, colonies and biofilms often exhibit significantly increased vertical growth, tending to form taller, denser, or more complex three-dimensional structures. Such architectures are advantageous for intra-population resource cycling and stress protection, and their formation is closely linked to the remodeling of genes associated with quorum sensing, carbon metabolism, and surface adhesion (Kim et al. 2013; Flores et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023; Hauserman et al. 2024).

Escherichia coli, a canonical model microorganism in space microbiology research, displays significant phenotypic adaptations under SMG, including the formation of thicker biofilms (Lynch et al. 2006) and enhanced metabolic activity (Chavez et al. 2024). SMG also triggers membrane stress adaptation in E. coli REL606. Transcriptomic analysis of changes over 24 h under both glucose-limited and glucose-sufficient conditions revealed that SMG significantly upregulates the expression of genes related to stress response, biofilm formation, and metabolism. After long-term culture, specific mutations associated with cell membrane structure and division were observed to accumulate (Lozzi et al. 2025). In contrast, the response of Staphylococcus aureus appears more strain-specific. Some spaceflight isolates show alterations in colony color, pigment production, and growth rate (Siems et al. 2022). For instance, the accessory gene regulator (Agr) system of S. aureus UAMS-1 was significantly upregulated after spaceflight (Hauserman et al. 2024). The Agr system is typically activated at high cell density, prompting a switch from an adherent, biofilm lifestyle to a planktonic, dispersive, and pathogenic state (Boles and Horswill 2008; Paharik and Horswill 2016). Interestingly, SMG conditions may conversely inhibit signaling molecules of the Agr pathway, leading to downregulation of this quorum-sensing system (Green et al. 2023). SMG can reduce its auto-aggregation, alter cell surface properties, and ultimately weaken biofilm-forming capacity (Singh et al. 2021). This discrepancy between real and simulated environments highlights the limitations of ground-based analogs. Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14 formed unprecedented columnar and canopy-like biofilm structures during spaceflight, with increased biomass and viable cell density (Kim et al. 2013). However, long-term SMG experiments showed that biofilm formation by its wild-type strain was slower, while the capacity of certain surface attachment-deficient mutants (ΔflgK and ΔpelA) was enhanced instead (Chen et al. 2024). Under SMG, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia exhibits coordinated enhancement in both colony morphology and biofilm architecture. Colony morphology analysis revealed a significant increase in swarming motility, forming larger (approximately 38 mm in diameter), more diffuse, semi-transparent colonies on soft agar plates. Concurrently, biofilm-forming capacity was markedly enhanced, with the biofilm thickness increasing by over 65% and the structure becoming denser and more complex. Multi-omics analysis indicated that this phenotypic shift is closely related to the coordinated upregulation of genes associated with motility (e.g. pilP, flgE), pilus assembly (e.g. pilA), and biofilm regulation (e.g. chpB, rpoN) (Su et al. 2021). Moreover, exposure to SMG induced notable phenotypic changes in Bacillus subtilis. While individual cell morphology remained rod-like, SMG-cultivated strains (BSS) exhibited increased intercellular mucus-like substances and significantly enhanced biofilm formation compared to controls. Multi-omics analyses revealed upregulation of biofilm-associated genes (epsB, epsC, epsN, tasA, bslA) and corresponding proteins (Su et al. 2023). Collectively, these studies show a range of microbial adaptations to space environments, where coordinated re-regulation of stress response and motility genes dictates the unique morphological and functional shifts observed in distinct bacterial taxa.

Fungi also undergo significant changes in colony morphology under spaceflight or SMG conditions, including alterations in colony area, surface texture, sporulation yield, and pigment production. For example, Aspergillus fumigatus isolates ISSFT-021 and IF1SW-F4 from the space station displayed faster radial colony growth, corresponding to changes in the abundance of proteins related to the cell wall and antioxidant response (Knox et al. 2016; Blachowicz et al. 2019). The Candida albicans SC5314 strain, under SMG, formed highly irregular, wrinkled colony structures–a morphology associated with enhanced virulence (Nielsen et al. 2021). The Aspergillus niger strain JSC-093350089, isolated from the ISS, showed accelerated growth rate and faster melanin diffusion (Romsdahl et al. 2018). SMG did not inhibit the growth of the A. niger N402 wild type, but instead promoted increased colony area and biomass in the ΔfwnA pigment mutant. Furthermore, sporulation yields significantly increased under SMG for both the ΔfwnA and ΔracA mutants, suggesting that FwnA and RacA may mediate the microgravity response (Cortesão et al. 2022). On the other hand, the spaceflight environment also affects fungal interactions with materials. Under space microgravity, A. brasiliensis ATCC 16404 adopted a distinct metal-handling strategy. Its hyphae transitioned from intracellular copper accumulation to a loose, instead secreting oxalic acid at levels approximately six times higher than the ground control. This oxalic acid sequestered copper extracellularly, thereby promoting localized corrosion (Liu et al. 2024). A. carbonarius CCTCC AF 2011004 showed enhanced spore and hyphal growth, as well as increased adhesion to metal surfaces under SMG. The spore surface was enriched with metal ions such as iron, aluminium, and copper, correlating with uneven deposition of corrosion products (Jiang et al. 2022), indicating an enhanced metal corrosion capability in the microgravity environment. Furthermore, under SMG, the rock-derived, polyextremotolerant black fungus Knufia chersonesos exhibited earlier hyphal formation compared to normal gravity conditions. Both the wild-type strain and the MA5790 mutant showed a stronger tendency for cell aggregation under SMG, forming clumped colonial structures (Tesei et al. 2021).

It is noteworthy that even extremophilic archaea, such as halophiles, exhibit significant phenotypic responses to the spaceflight environment. Research on two haloarchaeal strains, Haloferax mediterranei DSM 1411T and Halococcus dombrowskii DSM 14522T, demonstrated that SMG markedly reduced cellular aggregation in H. dombrowskii. Furthermore, the colony color of both strains shifted from light red to reddish-brown, suggesting that the synthesis or accumulation of carotenoid pigments may be regulated by microgravity (Dornmayr-Pfaffenhuemer et al. 2011).

Additionally, multiple studies have pointed out that the colony morphology and biofilm formation of microorganisms in the spaceflight environment are highly dependent on the surface materials to which they attach, such as stainless steel, silicone, cellulose membranes, and lubricant-impregnated surfaces. Engineered surface modifications can significantly weaken or even reshape colony structure and coverage, providing a key strategy for controlling biological contamination on spacecraft (Hauserman et al. 2024). For example, a spaceflight experiment found that biofilms formed by P. aeruginosa on lubricant-impregnated surfaces were significantly reduced in both thickness and surface coverage compared to the ground control (Flores et al. 2023). This indicates that the effect of microgravity on biofilms is not an absolute promotion or inhibition but is also largely dependent on the physicochemical properties of the material surface.

#### Hyphal, sporulation, and ultrastructural morphology

The spaceflight environment induces alterations in microbial microscopic morphology, affecting hyphal density, branching patterns, and even ultrastructure integrity. For example, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that after culture under SMG, the biofilm thickness of both A. niger wild-type and ΔracA mutant strains increased significantly. While the wild-type biofilm thickened by approximately 22%, the ΔracA mutant biofilm thickened by up to 56%. Notably, the ΔracA mutant biofilm developed a distinct tri-layered differentiation, resulting in an overall thickness roughly three times that of its wild-type counterpart. Beyond structural thickness, SMG augments sporulation, a phenomenon most pronounced within the mutant genotypes, spore counts rose by 26% in the ΔfwnA mutant and 35% in the ΔracA mutant. Mechanistically, these phenotypic shifts in the ΔracA strain are ascribed to the deletion of the Rho GTPase RacA. This loss disrupts actin polymerization, leading to more hyphal branching and greater extracellular matrix secretion (Cortesão et al. 2022).

Under SMG, the hyphal morphology of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, specifically Rhizophagus irregularis, changed significantly. Hyphal elongation is stunted, with total lengths reaching only approximately 50% of those observed under terrestrial gravity, while growth path transitions toward disoriented, multidirectional patterns. Furthermore, the complexity of the hyphal network is reduced; the frequency of secondary hyphal branches is markedly decreased, and higher-order branches are seldom observed. Adding the strigolactone analog rac-GR24 confirms this finding. Under SMG, new branch induction remains much lower than under normal gravity. These observations suggest that microgravity reduces the ability of hyphae to respond to branching signals. Without directional cues, the hyphal tip gravitropic sensing mechanism may fail. This prevents normal gravitropic growth and efficient branching, ultimately reducing mycorrhizal colonization efficiency (Liu et al. 2018).

In SMG conditions, the filamentous forms of C. albicans SC5314 exhibited morphological alterations. The transition frequency from the yeast form to filamentous forms such as germ tubes, pseudohyphae, and true hyphae increased, resulting in an increased prevalence of filamentous forms. Concurrently, the budding pattern shifted from normal bipolar or axial budding to random, abnormal budding, with bud scars or daughter cells observed at medial sites on mother cells. Cells displaying abnormal budding were often found in clumps or aggregates containing morphogenically diverse cells. Gene expression analysis showed that the expression of HWP1, a gene associated with hyphal formation, was upregulated 2-fold, while the expression of YWP1, a gene related to the yeast form, was downregulated 25-fold. These morphological and molecular changes collectively indicate that the SMG environment induces a morphogenic switch in C. albicans that is consistent with enhanced pathogenicity, suggesting that this fungal pathogen may become more virulent during spaceflight (Altenburg et al. 2008).

The hyphal morphology and ultrastructure of the basidiomycete Polyporus brumalis changed when cultured under spaceflight conditions. Under light conditions, the fruiting body could form a nearly normal stipe and pileus. The pileus still contained hymenophore. However, the distribution of basidia and basidiospores within the hymenium was limited, being primarily concentrated at the bottom of the culture tube with reduced distribution on the side walls. Furthermore, the paraphyses were irregularly arranged and reduced in size, and thick-walled hyphae were absent from the ultrastructure. Under dark conditions, the mycelium could grow and cover the medium surface but failed to form fruiting body primordia. If primordia had formed before launch, their stipes developed in a twisted and swollen manner under microgravity, and the pileus was absent. This indicates that microgravity affects hyphal differentiation, spatial arrangement, and the complete development of the hymenium. However, light could partially guide its morphogenesis, and the mycelium retained the ability to form normal fruiting bodies after returning to Earth (Kasatkina et al. 1980).

For bacteria with hyphal states, such as actinomycetes, microgravity similarly leads to adjustments in hyphal morphology. Early SMG experiments observed reduced hyphal branching, restricted extension, and alteration in mycelial aggregation morphology in certain actinomycetes (Fang et al. 2000; Nickerson et al. 2004; Zhou et al. 2006). A 2015 study found that Streptomyces coelicolor A3(2) exhibited accelerated sporulation with increased accumulation of gray spore pigment under both SMG and during the Shenzhou-8 spaceflight mission, accompanied by a shortened life cycle on agar medium. In liquid culture, the spaceflight samples yielded thicker, more fragmented, and well-dispersed hyphae. Global transcriptional analysis revealed upregulation of genes involved in morphological differentiation, notably the whi genes (whiD, sigF, and whiE), during days 4–6 under simulated microgravity, correlating with the observed phenotypic changes (Huang et al. 2015).

### Effects of the spaceflight environment on special functional phenotypes of microorganisms

#### Secondary metabolite synthesis

Research indicates that microbial genomes contain immense potential for the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, a potential remains largely unexploited under conventional laboratory culture conditions. The primary reason is that numerous biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) remain “silent” under standard laboratory settings (Van Lanen and Shen 2006; Caicedo-Montoya et al. 2021; Dinglasan et al. 2025). As traditional screening paradigms become exhausted, discovering novel bioactive compounds grows increasingly challenging (Koehn and Carter 2005). Therefore, subjecting microorganisms to extreme physical or chemical stresses, such as deep-sea, polar, or even outer space environments, is a viable strategy to induce the production of new secondary metabolites and overcome current bottlenecks in resource exploration.

Studies over the past decade have shown that the spaceflight or SMG environment can significantly influence and even reshape microbial secondary metabolic pathways. Research on fungi, particularly within the genus Aspergillus, has been the most extensive. For example, the secondary metabolic profiles of A. nidulans strains LO1362 (ΔnkuA), LO8158 (ΔmcrA), and CW12001 (laeA overexpression) changed greatly under ISS flight conditions (Romsdahl et al. 2019). In wild-type strains, the production of prenyl xanthones decreased to about one-fifth of the original level. In contrast, the anthraquinone pigment asperthecin increased in the mutant strain LO1362 (ΔnkuA), which lacks a major DNA repair pathway, and in the laeA overexpression strain CW12001, reaching levels approximately 300% and 150% higher, respectively. This suggests its possible induction as a radioprotectant. Meanwhile, in strain LO8158, which lacks the secondary metabolism negative regulator mcrA, the production of emericellamide and terrequinone decreased, while the synthesis of nidulanin and sterigmatocystin increased. These changes collectively indicate that the spaceflight environment may reprogram metabolic flux by interfering with the metabolic regulatory network.

Beyond A. nidulans, A. fumigatus isolates ISSFT-021 and IF1SW-F4 from the ISS also exhibited distinct secondary metabolite profiles (Knox et al. 2016). For instance, in strain IF1SW-F4, the production of fumigaclavine A increased, while the production of its downstream product fumigaclavine C decreased, likely due to a frameshift mutation in the prenyltransferase gene fgaPT1. Furthermore, the production of fumagillin, which has anti-inflammatory activity, was reduced in both ISS isolates mentioned above, whereas the production of pyripyropene A, which has insecticidal and immunomodulatory potential, increased. The A. niger strain JSC-093350089, isolated from the ISS, showed a significant increase in the production of naphtho-γ-pyrone (Romsdahl et al. 2020). Specifically, the yield of pyranonigrin A was about 60 times higher than that of the ground control strain ATCC 1015. Research found that this yield increase might be related to INDELs in the promoter region of the developmental regulator gene flbA. The study further identified a pyranonigrin A biosynthetic gene cluster containing a novel gene, pyrE, and confirmed that this compound can effectively enhance fungal UV resistance. These findings collectively highlight that extreme environments such as space are unique sources for mining novel natural products and elucidating their biosynthetic mechanisms.

Compared to fungi, spaceflight research on bacterial secondary metabolism has focused more on model organisms and their hallmark products. Among these, actinomycetes, as an important source of antibiotics, represent the earliest and most systematically studied group. Early spaceflight experiments found that the production of the peptide antibiotic actinomycin D by Streptomyces plicatus WC56452 exhibited a phasic change, first increasing and then decreasing, showing clear time dependence (Lam et al. 2002; Benoit et al. 2006). This phenomenon suggests that long-term microgravity may attenuate the stress response capacity of actinomycetes, thereby affecting secondary metabolite synthesis. Subsequent research further revealed that under microgravity conditions, changes in the expression of secondary metabolism-related genes and in metabolite profiles in actinomycetes are not always consistent (Liang et al. 2007; Gao et al. 2011; Huang et al. 2015; Sharma and Curtis 2022). The yield of some products increases, while others decrease or remain unchanged. This discrepancy may be related to disturbances in developmental processes such as hyphal differentiation and sporulation.

Research on other bacteria has also revealed diverse response patterns. The production of the toxic secondary metabolite pyocyanin by P. aeruginosa PA14 was significantly reduced under long-term SMG (Chen et al. 2024). Conversely, after a 15-day spaceflight, the overall production of nikkomycins X and Z by Streptomyces ansochromogenus increased by approximately 13% to 18% (Luo et al. 1998). Beyond direct observation, mutagenic screening utilizing the spaceflight environment has also achieved breakthroughs. For example, to increase the yield of the immunosuppressant Kanglemycin C produced by Nocardia mediterranei var. kanglensis 1747-64, a research team first obtained five high-yield mutant strains via UV and microwave radiation. These mutants were then carried aboard the Shenzhou-3 spacecraft. After 162 h of spaceflight, mutant strain F-16 was selected from the low-radiation group, achieving a yield of 12.5 µg/mL. This represented an increase of about 200% compared to the original strain, indicating that the combined mutagenic effects of the spaceflight environment may be superior to single physical mutagenesis (Zhou et al. 2006).

The prokaryotic microorganism Microcystis aeruginosa PCC7806 can be rapidly induced to synthesize and excrete microcystins under SMG. By the second day of treatment, intracellular content increased by 34.2%, and extracellular release doubled, suggesting it rapidly responds to gravitational changes by enhancing secondary metabolism (Xiao et al. 2010). For macromycete, a study on Ganoderma lucidum strains mutagenized via a recoverable satellite payload showed that the polysaccharide and triterpenoid content were significantly higher than in traditional cultivated strains (Nie et al. 2025). Polysaccharide yield increased to 178.59%–265.38% of that in traditional strains, with enhanced anti-inflammatory activity, and triterpenoid content also showed a clear increase.

In summary, the regulation of microbial secondary metabolism by the spaceflight and SMG environment is highly strain-specific and metabolite-specific. A summary of the key changes in microbial secondary metabolite production under spaceflight conditions is provided in Table 2. Most current research is limited to known metabolites or well-characterized secondary metabolite-producing strains. However, a vast number of microbial communities within spacecraft remain uncharacterized, and their metabolic potential under microgravity likely exceeds current understanding. Therefore, future research urgently requires more systematic and comprehensive metabolomic studies, combined with in-depth genomic exploration. #### Pathogenicity and virulence

For long-term crewed space missions, microorganisms represent not only ubiquitous contaminants but also direct threats to astronaut health, with potential alterations in their pathogenicity being particularly concern. Research indicates that the spaceflight or SMG environment may profoundly modulate the pathogenic phenotypes of microorganisms, including their abilities to colonize, invade, and cause tissue damage. As previously discussed, the spaceflight environment can significantly alter microbial colony morphology and enhance biofilm formation. These changes facilitate persistent colonization on host surfaces and evasion of immune clearance, often associated with chronic infection and the development of antibiotic resistance (Gilbert et al. 2020; Acres et al. 2021).

A case in point is P. aeruginosa POA1, which exhibited stronger adhesion, increased quorum-sensing activity, and upregulation of virulence factors expression under SMG conditions (Crabbe et al. 2011). Notably, in the 2022 WHO fungal priority pathogens list, both A. fumigatus and C. albicans were listed as of “critical priority” (World Health Organization 2022). A. fumigatus is a major opportunistic pathogenic fungus. Studies found that while the in vitro growth and chemical stress tolerance of ISS A. fumigatus isolates ISSFT-021 and IF1SW-F4 were similar to the clinical strain CEA10, their lethality was higher than that of clinical strains CEA10 and AF293 in a zebrafish infection model using neutrophil-deficient hosts (Knox et al. 2016).

As a common commensal fungus in humans, C. albicans colonizes multiple mucosal surfaces and skin (Romo and Kumamoto 2020) and can cause severe infections—ranging from superficial to systemic—in immunocompromised individuals or during microbial dysbiosis (Pfaller and Diekema 2007; Nobile and Johnson 2015; Crucian et al. 2018). Multiple studies have shown that the spaceflight environment potentiates the pathogenicity of C. albicans. Experiments conducted on the ISS over multiple missions indicated that microgravity not only accelerated the growth of C. albicans [CMCC(F)98001] but also bolstered its resistance to amphotericin B–a phenotype that persisted even upon return to Earth (Wang et al. 2020). Further validation from the SJ-10 satellite experiment showed that the spaceflight strain SC5314 grew faster and exhibited enhanced oxidative stress resistance and cytotoxicity (Nielsen et al. 2021). Integrated proteomic and metabolomic analysis revealed significant upregulation of pathways related to ribosomes, DNA replication, base excision repair, and sulfur-containing amino acid metabolism. This indicates that the strain may drive its rapid growth and high virulence in the space environment by synergistically enhancing protein synthesis, DNA repair, and oxidative stress protection.

Beyond fungi, several bacterial pathogens have also shown altered virulence in the spaceflight or SMG environment. As early as 2007, the seminal work by Wilson et al. (2007) highlighted that spaceflight could enhance the virulence of Salmonella typhimurium and identified the central regulatory role of the RNA-binding protein Hfq in this process. In 2022, utilizing more complex ground-based low-shear models and a three-dimensional human intestinal co-culture system, it was further found that long-term SMG significantly enhanced the adhesion, invasion, and intracellular survival capabilities of Salmonella χ3339 and its Δhfq mutant. It also induced a stronger inflammatory host response (Barrila et al. 2022). Intriguingly, this effect was maintained in Hfq-deficient mutants, implying the existence of novel, Hfq-independent regulatory circuits.

However, the microbial response to spaceflight is diverse. For instance, the virulence of Yersinia pestis was attenuated under SMG, manifested as impaired type III secretion system function, reduced expression and secretion of the effector protein YopM, and decreased proliferation within macrophages (Rosenzweig and Chopra 2012). Furthermore, some virulence-enhancing phenotypes may be reversible rather than genomic. While the lethality of Serratia marcescens increased during infection of Drosophila under SMG, such hypervirulence vanished upon re-exposure to conventional terrestrial culture conditions (Gilbert et al. 2020).

#### Antibiotic resistance

As human spaceflight advances toward deep-space exploration, the impact of the spaceflight or SMG environment on microbial antibiotic resistance has become a major concern, crucial for ensuring astronaut health and supporting long-term missions. Multiple studies suggest that this environment may enhance microbial resistance through diverse mechanisms.

First, the spaceflight environment may directly enhance phenotypic resistance. For example, the antibiotic resistance rates of 20 multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter pittii isolates from the ISS were increased (Tierney et al. 2022). Significant resistance elevation was also observed in Bacillus cereus CGMCC 1.230 spaceflight isolates: strain LCT-BC25 displayed a 50% reduction in the amikacin inhibition zone (1.3 cm vs. 2.6 cm), and strain LCT-BC235 converted from a ceftazidime-sensitive to a resistant phenotype (Su et al. 2014). Similarly, E. coli ATCC 4157 cultured on the space station for 49 h was able to sustain growth in the presence of inhibitory concentrations of gentamicin (25–75 µg/mL). At high concentrations, it produced many outer membrane vesicles not seen in ground controls, and the bacterial cells in the culture appeared as a single aggregated mass (Figure 2) (Zea et al. 2017). This suggests that limited nutrient and drug diffusion under microgravity, possibly due to altered membrane structure and population state, may enhance resistance. Second, microgravity may influence phenotypic resistance by modulating global stress responses. The EcAMSat spaceflight experiment demonstrated that microgravity could modulate the antibiotic sensitivity of uropathogenic E. coli AMG1 by altering the status of the stress regulatory factor RpoS (Padgen et al. 2020). This suggests that targeting such stress pathways could be a novel strategy to combat antibiotic resistance in space.

More importantly, the microgravity environment may greatly promote the horizontal transfer of resistance genes. In the absence of antibiotic pressure, SMG conditions promoted the transfer of resistance genes blaOXA-500 and ISAbat1 from Acinetobacter pittii IIF1SW-P1 to four S. aureus strains (IF4SW, IIF6SW, IF7SW, and IIF8SW). The transfer efficiency increased nearly a hundredfold compared to ground conditions, rendering the recipient strains resistant to the antibiotic oxacillin (Urbaniak et al. 2021). This reveals the potential risk of rapid resistance spread in the spaceflight environment.

On the other hand, metagenomic studies also indicate that the spaceflight environment promotes the accumulation and dissemination of resistance genes. Metagenomic sequencing of over 800 surface samples from nine ISS modules identified 1065 distinct antibiotic resistance genes spanning 22 classes (Salido et al. 2025). Among these, Pantoea piersonii carried the most diverse array of antibiotic resistance genes, including unique genes conferring resistance to antimicrobial metals like copper and arsenic. This directly reflects the unique selection pressures possibly formed by space station materials.

Another study showed that the kombucha mutualistic community (KMC) exposed to simulated Martian conditions exhibited a significant increase in the diversity of antibiotic resistance genes. Tetracycline and vancomycin resistance genes not detected in the original sample also appeared (Sabatino et al. 2022). Furthermore, various antimicrobial resistance genes in the salivary and gut microbiomes of astronauts showed significant changes pre- and post-flight. This was accompanied by an increased abundance of mobile genetic elements post-flight, suggesting that the spaceflight environment may promote the horizontal transfer of resistance genes within the human microbiome (Liu et al. 2020; Morrison et al. 2021).

It should be noted that existing findings are not universal; several studies have observed no significant increase in antimicrobial resistance, or even an enhanced sensitivity to antibiotics, under microgravity conditions (Tirumalai et al. 2017; Su et al. 2023). Simultaneously, changes in the astronaut’s immune system and decreased stability of drugs in the spaceflight environment may also interfere with the accurate assessment of resistance changes (Simon et al. 2021). Therefore, future research needs to further standardize experimental designs. This includes implementing more comprehensive multiple controls and using internal standard drugs to correct for biases caused by non-biological factors, thereby obtaining more reliable data.

## Molecular mechanisms driving microbial adaptation in the spaceflight environment

Substantial evidence indicates that cosmic rays and high-linear energy transfer (LET) radiation directly cause severe clustered DNA damage, such as double-strand breaks. Concurrently, the microgravity environment disrupts the efficiency and fidelity of DNA repair by interfering with the cell cycle, metabolism, and oxidative stress responses. The synergistic action of these two factors significantly increases genomic instability and mutation rates, providing abundant genetic variation for microbial adaptation in the spaceflight environment.

Cosmic radiation profoundly impacts microbial physiology, structure, and community composition, exerting lethal effects, inducing mutations, or triggering adaptive responses. Fungi exhibit considerable adaptability under space radiation (Blachowicz et al. 2020; Cortesão et al. 2020). On one hand, fungi primarily repair DNA double-strand breaks through non-homologous end joining (Arentshorst et al. 2012; Boiteux and Jinks-Robertson 2013; Fritsche et al. 2024), which, although often leading to mutations, aids in survival maintenance. On the other hand, some fungi, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae RSY12, YWY200, exhibit an inducible, genome-wide microhomology-mediated recombination (MHMR) pathway under radiation stress. This mechanism facilitates genomic rearrangements and has been proposed as a potential driver of adaptive evolution under genotoxic conditions (Chan et al. 2007). Furthermore, melanin synthesis is considered an important mechanism for certain fungi to resist radiation. For example, radiotrophic fungi discovered at the Chernobyl accident site grow towards radiation sources and enhance energy acquisition and radiation resistance through melanin (Zhdanova et al. 2004; Dadachova and Casadevall 2008; Shunk et al. 2020). Beyond these classical repair pathways, microorganisms have evolved more complex tolerance mechanisms. These include novel mechanisms such as chromosome structure rearrangement and specific small RNA regulation to cope with high-dose radiation. Taking the radiation-resistant bacterium Deinococcus radiodurans as an example, its highly ordered chromosomal structure and multi-copy genome provide the foundation for efficient reconstruction of broken DNA (Rai and Dutta 2024; Qiu et al. 2025) (Figure 2).

In contrast to radiation damage, microgravity induces cellular responses to stressors such as oxidative stress and nutrient limitation from another perspective. Studies have shown that Stenotrophomonas maltophilia enhances its growth and biofilm-forming capacity under SMG by upregulating genes related to energy metabolism and intracellular trafficking and secretion (Su et al. 2021). Evidence indicates that microgravity often triggers global transcriptomic and proteomic changes in bacteria, with enhanced carbohydrate metabolism being particularly prominent, reflecting increased oxidative stress and shifts in carbon substrate utilization (Sharma and Curtis 2022). In this special environment, increased microbial genomic instability creates conditions for horizontal gene transfer (Figure 2). SMG can significantly promote the transfer of antibiotic resistance genes between bacteria (Urbaniak et al. 2021). Comparative genomics research on ISS isolates found that mobile genetic elements, particularly prophages and integrons, play a central role in the adaptation process. For example, phage-related genes are differentially enriched in space station strains. Their insertion or rearrangement can directly regulate gene expression, mediate chromosomal rearrangements, or confer new functions, revealing that phage-mediated horizontal gene transfer is one of the important molecular mechanisms enabling rapid microbial adaptation within the confined cabin (Irby and Broddrick 2024).

The long-term accumulation of the molecular events ultimately shapes the trajectory of microbial adaptive evolution at the macroscopic scale. Strains from the space station or simulated environments exhibit systematic differences from ground strains in genes related to DNA repair, stress response, and metabolic networks. Whole-genome comparisons revealed that these strains commonly upregulate mechanosensitive ion channel proteins, homologous recombination and other DNA repair systems, as well as genes potentially associated with pathogenicity, such as those involved in small molecule biosynthesis and ATP-dependent transport (Szydlowski et al. 2024). These findings collectively elucidate that the spaceflight environment, by applying specific selective pressures, drives the rapid adaptive changes of microorganisms in terms of genomic structure and function, thereby greatly enhancing their survival and adaptive capacity in extreme environments.

## Transformation and applications of spaceflight microorganisms

### Health maintenance and medical support

Long-duration spaceflight exerts multidimensional effects on astronauts’ physiological status and microbial ecology. Substantial research confirms that spaceflight-specific factors, including microgravity, cosmic radiation, and confined environments, collectively lead to shifts in the taxonomic composition and functional remodeling of the astronaut microbiome across the skin, nasopharynx, and gut. These alterations in microbial ecology interact with concurrent physiological changes, such as immune function fluctuations and inflammatory activation, potentially elevating infection risk and affecting recovery capacity, thereby posing a systemic challenge to health (Morrison et al. 2021; Averesch et al. 2023; Etlin et al. 2024; Minoretti et al. 2024). For instance, long-term spaceflight often leads to decreased gut microbiota diversity, specifically manifested as a reduction in beneficial bacteria (e.g. Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) and a relative increase in potentially pathogenic bacteria (e.g. E. coli, members of Clostridium). This dysbiosis may further compromise immune function and increase the risk of opportunistic infections (Ilyin 2005). To address these risks, integrating systematic microbial monitoring into the space medical support system has become a consensus. Applying in-orbit rapid molecular detection technologies to track the dynamic changes of environmental and human-associated microbes in real-time can enable the early identification of hazards such as abnormal increases in pathogen abundance, biofilm formation, or contamination of critical equipment. This provides a decision-making basis for implementing precise, proactive infection prevention and control strategies (Khodadad et al. 2021).

Building on these findings, probiotic intervention, rapid diagnostics, and in-situ biomanufacturing have become three focal points in recent translational research in space microbiology. First, probiotic screening and nutritional intervention. Probiotics function through various mechanisms, including enhancement of intestinal barrier function, immune modulation via interaction with immunocompetent cells, and production of antimicrobial substances such as bacteriocins (Corthésy et al. 2007). To adapt to space missions, research focuses on their survival and functionality under extreme conditions. SMG experiments indicate that some Lactobacillus and Bacillus strains not only tolerate these conditions well but even exhibit adaptive features such as accelerated growth and enhanced resistance (Bharindwal et al. 2023). Lyophilized Lactobacillus casei maintained good stability after 1 month of storage in the space station environment (Sakai et al. 2018). Among these, Bacillus subtilis HU58 showed the highest survival rate in long-term radiation and simulated gastrointestinal tract experiments, demonstrating greater potential as probiotic formulations for deep space exploration missions (Fajardo-Cavazos and Nicholson 2021). Second, establishing rapid point-of-care diagnostic or monitoring systems suitable for the spaceflight environment. To overcome the limitations of traditional microbial detection methods on space stations, portable and rapid diagnostic technologies have become essential. Systems like the RAZOR®EX developed by NASA and other institutions, based on real-time quantitative PCR technology, can complete targeted detection of samples from water or surfaces in approximately 1 h. This system has been successfully validated on the ISS, providing a reliable means for real-time infection control and environmental assessment (Khodadad et al. 2021). Third, using the spaceflight environment as a platform for in-situ drug manufacturing and novel target discovery. The limited shelf-life of pharmaceuticals during long-term missions (Du et al. 2011) has spurred the concept of in-situ biomanufacturing utilizing the spaceflight environment. On one hand, changes in microbial secondary metabolite production under spaceflight conditions provide clues for the on-site production of critical drugs through synthetic biology engineering. On the other hand, the remodeling of microbial gene expression induced by the space environment may also offer unique opportunities for discovering new vaccines or therapeutic targets.

### Food biomanufacturing and life support systems

Long-duration crewed deep space missions present severe challenges to food supply and nutritional diversity. Given the prohibitively high cost of continuous resupply from Earth, developing in-situ microbial-based systems for food and nutrient production has become a critical research and engineering direction (Menezes et al. 2015). Space fermentation research aims to actively guide and utilize the adaptive evolution of spaceflight microorganisms. By screening, engineering, or even creating novel microbial strains, the goal is to achieve cyclic production of food, pharmaceuticals, and even oxygen within a confined space habitat, ultimately constructing a self-sufficient “miniature Earth” life support system.

Beer brewing represents a classic microbial fermentation process. In an SMG experiment using larger yeast, it was found that due to continuous suspension, the nutrient utilization efficiency of the yeast improved, increasing the fermentation rate by 33%. However, changes in the physical environment also led to a significant reduction in the concentration of key flavor esters, closely related to the downregulation of the ATF2 gene, a key gene in the ester synthesis pathway. This provides a basis for flavor regulation in future space biomanufacturing (Mendoza et al. 2024). Miso fermentation represents a more complex solid-state fermentation system. The first miso fermentation experiment was successfully conducted on the ISS (Coblentz et al. 2025). Comparative multi-omics analysis revealed the appearance of a unique bacterium, Bacillus velezensis, in the microbial community of the space-produced miso. The mutation rate in the primary fermentation fungus, A. oryzae, was also significantly higher than in the ground control group, with variations particularly concentrated on chromosome eight. This study not only verified the feasibility and safety of space fermentation but also provided new insights for developing flavor-enhanced and nutritious foods adapted to the space environment.

These cases demonstrate that the spaceflight environment can shape unique fermentation processes. Currently, research in this field is still in its early stages overall. Researchers are actively exploring the use of microorganisms, such as microalgae (e.g. Arthrospira platensis) and yeast, to produce food, vitamins, and other nutritional biomass, with the aim of constructing closed-loop life support systems for future long-duration missions (Berliner et al. 2021; Averesch et al. 2023).

### Resource utilization and bioprotection

The unique advantage of microorganisms in space exploration lies in their potential as “in-situ cell factories” for local resource utilization. These self-replicating micro-systems can efficiently convert local resources. On one hand, they can transform atmospheric carbon dioxide and similar compounds into organic matter. On the other hand, through biomining techniques, they can extract metals or minerals from extraterrestrial regolith for in-orbit manufacturing and synthesis (Verbeelen et al. 2021; Averesch et al. 2023). Utilizing engineered microorganisms to produce biopolymers and other biomaterials within the habitat can significantly reduce the mass of supplies that must be carried from Earth. This not only lowers mission costs but also enhances the capability for autonomous response to unexpected needs.

Furthermore, radiation-shielding fungi, due to their unique melanin structure and radiation tolerance, are considered important candidates for achieving lightweight, renewable bioprotection. Early discovery of the black fungus Cladosporium sphaerospermum at Chernobyl, which exhibited accelerated growth under strong radiation, suggested that its melanin might absorb and convert radiation energy into metabolic energy (Dadachova et al. 2007). ISS experiments further confirmed that this fungus could grow stably and reduce local radiation dose by approximately 2%, demonstrating its potential for on-orbit radiation shielding (Averesch et al. 2022). To this end, researchers fabricated biocomposite materials by blending polylactic acid (PLA) with fungal melanin (PLA-FunMel), synthetic melanin (PLA-SynMel), animal melanin (PLA-AniMel), and compressed mycelium (PLA-CMy). These materials were placed outside the ISS for long-term exposure experiments. The results showed that materials blended with fungal melanin experienced about 88.6% less mass loss compared to pure PLA, indicating that fungal melanin can significantly mitigate material degradation caused by the space environment (Cordero et al. 2025). This type of research offers a new sustainable, low-mass bioprotection strategy for deep space missions.

## Conclusions and perspective

In summary, research in spaceflight microbiology reveals that the composite space environment, constituted by microgravity, cosmic radiation, and a confined ecosystem, exerts strong selective pressures on microorganisms, driving systemic, multi-level adaptation. At the phenotypic level, microorganisms exhibit significant plasticity, undergoing significant changes in morphological structure, biofilm formation, secondary metabolite synthesis, and even pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance. The root of these macro-scale changes lies in the synergistic action of radiation and microgravity, which increases mutation rates by inducing DNA damage and interfering with repair fidelity. Concurrently, environmental pressures promote horizontal gene transfer, accelerating the dissemination of adaptive genes. Multi-omics analyses indicate that this evolutionary process manifests as the systemic reprogramming of key genes in core pathways such as oxidative stress response, energy metabolism, membrane transport, and quorum sensing. These insights not only reveal the molecular basis of the response of life to extreme environments but also provide critical theoretical foundations for developing space biotechnologies such as probiotics, in-situ biomanufacturing, and resource recycling.

However, research on spaceflight microbial strains still faces significant challenges. First, current studies heavily rely on short-duration flight missions and imperfect ground-based simulations, making it difficult to reveal genuine long-term evolutionary trajectories. Second, experimental systems predominantly focus on single model organisms, resulting in a weak understanding of the ecological interactions and stability within complex microbial communities. Third, the randomness of space-induced mutagenesis and its potential biosafety risks pose fundamental challenges to the controlled application of such strains.

Looking ahead, this field requires optimization across research models, methodologies, and technological applications. First, a shift from short-term observation to long-term evolution experiments utilizing platforms like the space station is necessary, alongside the development of ground-based simulation systems capable of reproducing the coupled effects of multiple environmental factors. Second, deep integration of multi-omics data is needed, coupled with the introduction of artificial intelligence to predict adaptive outcomes and design synthetic microbial systems, enabling a transition from “describing phenomena” to “predicting and designing”. Third, many current concepts urgently require engineering and systematization, such as developing integrated biomanufacturing systems and optimizing in-situ resource utilization processes. Finally, biosafety assessment and management must be strengthened concurrently. This involves systematically studying the interaction patterns within complex microbial communities and establishing safety protocols for the use of engineered strains. These steps are essential to ensure the safe and sustainable development of space biotechnologies, laying a solid scientific and technical foundation for humanity’s future deep space exploration.
