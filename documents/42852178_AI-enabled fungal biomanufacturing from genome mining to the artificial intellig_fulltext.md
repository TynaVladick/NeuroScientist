## Abstract

Fungi are important chassis organisms for sustainable biomanufacturing owing to their strong secretion capacity, metabolic diversity, and adaptability to industrial processes. Driven by the rapid development of Artificial Intelligence (AI), computational approaches that enable machines to learn patterns from data and make predictions, together with omics technologies and synthetic biology, data- and model-driven strategies are increasingly reshaping fungal cell factory design and optimization. This review summarizes recent advances in AI-enabled fungal biomanufacturing, covering AI-assisted genome mining, enzyme and pathway analysis, genome editing, metabolic network modeling, and intelligent fermentation control. The review further highlights the emerging concept of the artificial intelligence virtual cell (AIVC), which integrates multi-omics data, mechanistic modeling, and machine learning-based approaches into a unified multiscale framework for simulating dynamic cellular behaviors. The technical foundations and potential applications of AIVC in fungal metabolic engineering, drug discovery, agriculture, and environmental biotechnology are reviewed. Finally, key challenges-data quality and standardization, cross-scale model integration and interpretability, and the gap between in silico prediction and experimental validation-are highlighted. This review provides a systematic perspective on the role of AI and virtual cell technologies in advancing next generation fungal biotechnology.

## Introduction

Fungal biotechnology, situated at the intersection of modern biotechnology and industrial engineering, enables the scalable and sustainable production of high-value chemicals, enzymes, biomaterials, and natural products. Compared with traditional chemical synthesis routes, data-driven biomanufacturing offers renewable and environmentally benign alternatives with high resource efficiency, and has therefore become an essential pillar supporting the transition toward a global bioeconomy (Mariam et al. 2025). Recent advances in omics technologies, gene editing, synthetic biology, and computational biology have further positioned microorganisms, particularly fungi, as versatile platforms for both fundamental research and industrial applications (Garg et al. 2025).

Among microbial hosts, fungi-especially filamentous fungi and yeasts-exhibit unique advantages for industrial biomanufacturing, owing to their robust protein secretion capacity and extensive metabolic diversity. Filamentous fungi can efficiently produce a wide range of organic acids (e.g., citric, lactic, and itaconic acids), as well as complex secondary metabolites and other natural products (Taherzadeh et al. 2022). The inherent plasticity of fungal secretion systems and metabolic pathways provides broad opportunities for heterologous protein expression, metabolic engineering, and sustainable biomanufacturing. In particular, well developed secretory pathways and complex extracellular enzyme systems make filamentous fungi highly attractive chassis organisms for industrial protein production (Baker 2018).

At the protein level, the performance of fungal cell factories is shaped not only by genetic and regulatory networks but also by the intrinsic physicochemical properties of their proteomes. Proteome wide analyses have revealed that fungal proteins display structured distributions of molecular weights and isoelectric points, with a pronounced bias toward acidic proteins and bimodal physicochemical organization. These global physicochemical constraints influence protein stability, intracellular localization, and secretion efficiency, thereby providing essential prior knowledge for protein engineering and AI-driven design of fungal cell factories (Mohanta et al. 2021). However, translating these intrinsic strengths into predictable, high-yield production remains challenging. Fungal genomes can be difficult to manipulate, regulatory networks are complex, and metabolic and secretory fluxes are hard to control. Moreover, process scale up often leads to morphological heterogeneity, reduced secretion efficiency, and metabolic instability (Liao et al. 2025).

To address these challenges, rapid advances in AI and computational biology have highlighted the transformative potential of integrating synthetic biology with AI-driven approaches (Kong et al. 2025). By leveraging large-scale multi-omics resources together with machine learning and deep learning methods (Goshisht 2024), AI is increasingly reshaping fungal cell factory design across multiple levels, including gene and regulatory circuit design, protein and enzyme engineering, metabolic flux optimization, and fermentation process control (Seshadri et al. 2025). The integration of machine learning with system-level metabolic modeling and multi-omics analysis is gradually shifting fungal biomanufacturing from experience-driven strategies toward data and model-driven paradigms (Oyetunde et al. 2018).

Beyond optimizing individual genes, enzymes, or pathways, an emerging direction is to integrate spatial, structural, and multi-omics information into unified frameworks, paving the way for virtual cell construction. These spatially resolved, systems-level representations provide a foundation for next-generation AI-guided fungal biomanufacturing by enabling the rational integration of molecular properties, cellular architecture, and bioprocess performance (Mccafferty et al. 2024). However, most AI applications in fungal biotechnology remain fragmented and task-specific.

In this review, we use the term artificial intelligence virtual cell (AIVC) to describe an integrated computational framework that extends beyond existing digital twin or hybrid GEM-machine learning (ML) approaches. Traditional digital twins in biotechnology, virtual representations of biological systems or processes that integrate real-time data and mechanistic models, typically focus on process-level simulations or bioreactor states, whereas genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs), computational models that describe cellular metabolism at the genome scale, mainly represent metabolic flux distributions under defined constraints (Ma et al. 2025). In contrast, AIVC aims to integrate multi-scale biological information—including molecular structures, regulatory networks, metabolic pathways, cellular architecture, and bioprocess environments—into a unified predictive system. Importantly, in filamentous fungi, the AIVC framework must explicitly account for eukaryotic-specific features, such as compartmentalized metabolism, complex secretion pathways, and morphology-dependent productivity (Cairns et al. 2021). These characteristics distinguish fungal AIVC models from more generalized microbial modeling frameworks.

In this context, this review systematically examines AI-empowered fungal biomanufacturing. We first summarize current task-specific AI tools, including genome mining, enzyme analysis, and pathway design. We then discuss how these approaches are progressively converging toward integrated virtual cell frameworks, which aim to connect molecular mechanisms, cellular dynamics, and bioprocess performance within a unified modeling paradigm (Meyer et al. 2021).

## AI mining and design of microbiome omics data

Fungi possess a vibrant capacity for the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and have served as the source of numerous clinically important drugs, including penicillin, cyclosporine, and lovastatin (Keller 2019; Qian et al. 2025). However, the biosynthetic systems of filamentous fungi are intrinsically complex. On the one hand, their genomes harbor a large number of silent or cryptic biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), which are genomic regions containing genes responsible for the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites that remain unexpressed under standard laboratory conditions, resulting in a substantial reservoir of unknown metabolic products (Keller et al. 2005). On the other hand, the rational engineering of known biosynthetic pathways is constrained by incomplete knowledge of regulatory networks, enzyme kinetics, and pathway flux distributions (Paddon and Keasling 2014). Consequently, the traditional “build-test-learn” cycle is often time-consuming and labor-intensive, representing a significant bottleneck in the development of fungal natural products. Recent advances in AI have introduced a robust set of tools to address these challenges (Paddon et al. 2013; Mullowney et al. 2023; Yuan et al. 2023). Several studies have begun to demonstrate measurable improvements in fungal systems using AI-assisted approaches. For example, machine learning-assisted metabolic modeling has been used to identify key regulatory nodes for organic acid production in Myceliophthora thermophila, leading to improved product yields through targeted metabolic rewiring (Wang et al. 2024). Similarly, deep learning-based sgRNA activity prediction has enhanced CRISPR editing efficiency in filamentous fungi by improving guide RNA selection (Chuai et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2023). Nevertheless, practical deployment of AI models still faces challenges. Model predictions may suffer from limited training datasets, uncertainty in biological parameters, and difficulties in reproducing results across strains or fermentation conditions. Addressing these limitations will require standardized datasets, transparent model evaluation, and closer integration between computational prediction and experimental validation (Cho et al. 2022).

### High applicability of AI frameworks with filamentous fungi biosynthesis

Supervised learning approaches can be employed to classify BGCs or perform regression based predictions using known features, such as inferring metabolic outputs from domain composition (Medema and Fischbach 2015). Deep learning models further demonstrate strong capabilities in automatically extracting informative features from raw or high-dimensional data, enabling breakthroughs in protein structure prediction, mass spectrometry analysis, and mycelial image recognition (Jumper et al. 2021). Additionally, natural language processing (NLP), a branch of AI that enables computers to analyze and extract information from human language, facilitates the extraction of unstructured biosynthetic knowledge from large-scale scientific literature and databases. By constructing knowledge graphs with explicit entity relationships and logical inference capabilities, NLP-based approaches connect genes, enzymes, reactions, and metabolites, thereby supporting the generation of pathway hypotheses and functional annotation (Al-Saleem et al. 2021). Generative AI models, algorithms designed to generate new data that resembles their training set, can learn the statistical distributions of molecular structures or protein sequences and generate novel variants with desired properties, facilitating the design of new bioactive compounds or the optimization of biosynthetic components (Tong et al. 2021). Reinforcement learning, through iterative interaction with simulated environments, can identify optimal decision-making strategies and has been applied to the design of dynamic regulatory circuits and the optimization of fermentation process parameters (Pandey et al. 2023). Collectively, these AI paradigms can be deployed across the major stages of filamentous fungal biosynthesis-discovery, functional interpretation, and production optimization.

### AI technologies widely adopted in filamentous fungal biosynthesis

Intelligent genome mining approaches are increasingly used to identify novel BGC and natural products. AI-driven genome mining has revealed the metabolic potential of fungi at unprecedented depth and breadth. The advent of AI technology has significantly broadened the scope of fungal genome mining, enabling a more comprehensive re-evaluation of their latent metabolic potential. Prior to this advancement, classical tools such as antiSMASH established an analytical framework based on homology comparison and rule-driven pattern recognition. Through continuous refinement and optimization, these tools have substantially enhanced the sensitivity and accuracy of BGC prediction, establishing them as a cornerstone in the field of genomic annotation (Blin et al. 2019; Karatzas et al. 2021). However, homology and rule-based pipelines are less effective for non-canonical clusters, motivating representation learning approaches that generalize beyond conserved motifs.

More recently, deep learning-based methods that encode DNA sequences into numerical representations and perform deep feature learning have enabled the identification of non-canonical or distantly related gene clusters lacking conserved domains, thereby substantially expanding the discovery space of fungal biosynthetic genes (Hannigan et al. 2019). The deep integration of genomic mining and mass spectrometry-based metabolomics has emerged as a pivotal paradigm in the discovery of natural products. By constructing association maps linking genomic information with molecular networks, researchers can efficiently and accurately identify structurally novel secondary metabolites from specific fungal strains (Wang et al. 2016). Furthermore, knowledge graph-based reverse biosynthesis tools can infer plausible biosynthetic pathways for target molecules based on curated biochemical rules, providing actionable hypotheses for activating silent gene clusters (Zheng et al. 2022b; Rikuto Kotoge et al. 2025). Linking clusters to molecular features is only the first step; mechanistic interpretation of enzyme functions and assembly logic is required for rational pathway engineering.

Elucidating the functional logic of fungal BGC is a prerequisite for rational engineering of these pathways. In this context, AI serves as a “decoder” of biosynthetic pathways, enabling systematic functional inference at both the protein and pathway levels. At the protein structure level, the emergence of AlphaFold has revolutionized structure prediction, allowing accurate modeling of fungal enzymes with previously unknown conformations (Jumper et al. 2021). Structural information can then be combined with molecular docking and active site analysis to infer substrate specificity and catalytic mechanisms (Tunyasuvunakool et al. 2021). At the pathway level, the integration of biochemical rules with machine learning enables prediction of substrate specificity and assembly order in complex enzyme systems such as non-ribosomal peptide synthetases, providing critical insights into the biosynthetic logic of fungal secondary metabolites (Scholten et al. 2024). Deep learning models are also increasingly applied to predict enzymatic regio and stereoselectivity, including hydroxylation patterns mediated by cytochrome P450 enzymes, as well as drug-drug interaction profiles (Ai et al. 2023; Lu et al. 2025).

In intelligent fermentation and regulatory studies, AI-driven monitoring and control systems form the critical bridge between laboratory-scale research and industrial production. Deep learning-based image analysis systems can automatically identify and quantify mycelial morphology during filamentous fungal fermentation, providing intuitive indicators for physiological state assessment and process optimization (Ren et al. 2025). Time series prediction models can further integrate online sensor data to construct soft sensing models for key state variables, enabling trend forecasting and anomaly detection. On this basis, advanced process control strategies, such as the integration of machine learning with model predictive control, enable real-time optimization of operational parameters, including feeding strategies and pH regulation. Such approaches have already demonstrated improvements in the stability and yield of penicillin fermentation processes (Zheng et al. 2022a; Huang et al. 2024).

Despite this rapid progress, several challenges still constrain AI-driven filamentous fungal biosynthesis. High-quality, multidimensional, and consistently annotated datasets remain scarce, and data fragmentation, heterogeneous formats, and limited annotation depth severely constrain the training efficiency and generalizability of advanced AI models (Mullowney et al. 2023). In parallel, a pronounced spatiotemporal mismatch exists between the rapid iteration of computational models and the inherently slow pace of experimental validation, underscoring the need for efficient closed-loop systems that integrate intelligent design, automated synthesis, high-throughput characterization, and iterative data feedback (Roach 2025). More fundamentally, filamentous fungi exhibit complex genomes, multilayered regulatory architectures, morphological plasticity, and strong environmental responsiveness, resulting in phenotypes that are far more intricate than those of bacteria or yeast. Although AI has become indispensable across multiple layers of fungal biosynthesis, from genome mining and enzyme function prediction to fermentation optimization and regulatory control, most existing applications remain task-specific and fragmented, failing to capture the integrated, dynamic, and multiscale nature of fungal cells. Taken together, these limitations highlight an urgent need for a unifying computational paradigm capable of integrating heterogeneous biological data, mechanistic knowledge, and learning-based models into a coherent system-level representation. In this context, the concept of the AIVC emerges as a natural and necessary evolution of AI-assisted fungal biosynthesis research, providing a foundation for holistic, dynamic, and controllable cellular simulations that bridge molecular mechanisms with phenotypic outcomes and enable truly rational design and optimization of fungal cell factories.

## Constructing digital twins for next-generation fungal biotechnology

### The renaissance of virtual cells in the era of big data and AI

Building upon the rapid expansion of AI-enabled tools for fungal biosynthesis, researchers have increasingly recognized the need for a unified framework capable of capturing cellular behavior at the system level. The concept of the virtual cell emerged in the early 2000s as a computational approach to reconstruct cellular biochemical processes; however, limited data throughput and simplified, equation based frameworks prevented early models from capturing the complex spatiotemporal dynamics of living cells (Qian et al. 2025). With the rapid accumulation of high-throughput omics data and advances in single-cell and spatial omics, cellular imaging, and large-scale bioinformatics resources, including genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolic network databases, our understanding of intracellular structures, metabolic networks, and regulatory mechanisms has expanded to unprecedented depth and breadth (Lim et al. 2024). Single-cell and multimodal omics technologies now enable joint profiling of multiple molecular layers, such as RNA, proteins, chromatin accessibility, and metabolites, together with spatial organization and morphology at single-cell resolution and increasingly within intact tissues (Fu et al. 2025). Meanwhile, established genome-scale and advanced metabolic modeling strategies have continued to support the rational design and optimization of microbial cell factories (Han et al. 2023; Lu et al. 2024).

Despite these advances, traditional mathematical models remain largely rule-based and static: their parameters rely heavily on prior knowledge or experimental measurements, and they struggle to simultaneously capture regulation, metabolism, spatial organization, temporal dynamics, and environmental responses (Moyer et al. 2026). These limitations are particularly evident when modeling complex eukaryotic cells such as fungi, whose metabolism, secretion, morphological transitions, organelle interactions, and environmental adaptation are tightly coupled (Øyås and Stelling 2018). Recent breakthroughs in AI, especially deep learning, generative modeling, and multimodal data integration, are rapidly enabling the unification of heterogeneous, high-dimensional biological datasets into coherent and operational virtual cell representations (Bunne et al. 2024). In this context, the concept of the AIVC has emerged as a multiscale and multimodal computational framework, or digital twin, capable of dynamically simulating molecular, cellular, and tissue-level behaviors under diverse environmental conditions and perturbations (Wu and Xie 2025). This shift from purely rule-based modeling toward integrated data and model driven approaches has catalyzed the formalization and growing adoption of AIVC across systems biology and bioengineering (Wen 2025). In fungal biomanufacturing, AIVC holds particular promise: beyond industrial optimization, it provides new opportunities for understanding pathogenicity and antifungal resistance (Gao et al. 2025), while enhancing metabolic modeling, enabling rational pathway design, and accelerating autonomous strain evolution for sustainable biomanufacturing (Li et al. 2025).

### Technical framework of the AIVC

The construction of a trustworthy virtual cell depends on several core components and methodological pillars, among which the systematic integration of multi-omics and multidimensional biological data forms the foundation of the AIVC. By jointly incorporating genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, single-cell and spatial omics, cellular imaging, organelle structural information, and spatiotemporal dynamics, AIVC frameworks enable the reconstruction of a comprehensive cellular landscape that captures not only the static architecture of the cell but also its dynamic states and adaptive responses to environmental cues (Laubenbacher et al. 2024). This integrative capability is particularly critical for filamentous fungi, whose cellular organization is characterized by compartmentalized metabolism, polarized growth, and extensive organelle-level interactions.

A critical obstacle in AIVC construction is the quality and resolution of the underlying multi-omics data. AI model performance is highly sensitive to sequencing read types, depth, and technical biases. For instance, while short-read sequencing offers high-throughput transcriptomic profiling, long-read sequencing is increasingly necessary to resolve complex fungal genomic architectures and alternative splicing events. Furthermore, insufficient RNA-seq depth or sparse single-cell coverage can introduce high dropout rates, severely limiting the ability of deep learning models to accurately infer dynamic regulatory networks. Addressing these data quality constraints is an essential prerequisite for building reliable, predictive virtual cell models (Garg et al. 2025).

Moreover, advanced AI models constitute the computational core of the virtual cell, leveraging generative and representation learning capabilities to reconstruct spatially resolved cellular structures and functional interaction networks. Beyond static descriptions, a robust virtual cell must simulate cellular responses across diverse environmental conditions and perturbations, including genetic, chemical, and physical interventions, which may manifest as changes in metabolic flux distributions, morphological differentiation, stress responses, or survival strategies (Bhardwaj et al. 2025). ### Potential applications of AI-driven virtual cells in fungi

AIVC technologies are increasingly shaping fungal systems biology and computational modeling. By integrating multi-omics data-including transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and spatial omics with deep learning and multiscale simulation frameworks, AIVC enables in silico modeling of dynamic fungal cellular processes. Accumulating evidence suggests that AIVC holds substantial promise across five major application domains, spanning biomanufacturing, pathogenicity, drug discovery, agriculture, and environmental systems.

In biomanufacturing and metabolic engineering, AIVC frameworks facilitate the reconstruction of fungal metabolic networks and enable genotype to phenotype prediction under diverse environmental conditions. By leveraging graph neural networks and Transformer-based architectures, AIVC can simulate growth dynamics and metabolic responses to nutrient variation (Wang et al. 2024). Machine-learning-assisted genome-scale metabolic modeling has already demonstrated effectiveness in fungal systems (Han et al. 2023), and AIVC-based flux prediction provides critical support for rational strain design and fermentation optimization, given the strong sensitivity of metabolic flux distributions to environmental factors (Quiros et al. 2013).

Beyond industrial applications, virtual cell models have been increasingly applied to system-level analyses of fungal pathogenicity and host-fungus interactions. Predictive infection models, such as those developed for Candida albicans under human whole blood conditions, have revealed dynamic immune interactions and potential evasion mechanisms (Prausse et al. 2018), with subsequent studies further demonstrating their utility in predicting infection outcomes (Guthrie et al. 2025). In parallel, AIVC enables systems-level modeling of drug-target interactions, supporting the prediction of antifungal efficacy and resistance mechanisms (Li et al. 2025).

AIVC further contributes to antifungal drug discovery by enabling AI-driven virtual screening and resistance prediction. Selective antifungal compounds, including WXP-003, an inhibitor of fungal serine palmitoyl transferase that disrupts sphingolipid biosynthesis, have been identified through such approaches (Wang et al. 2021). Virtual cell-based models also support the prediction of resistance-associated mutations in key metabolic enzymes (Zhang et al. 2025), and their integration with molecular dynamics and systems pharmacology facilitates high-throughput screening and resistance evolution analysis (Madrigal-Aguilar et al. 2022).

In agricultural systems, AIVC models enable the simulation of fungal growth dynamics and mycotoxin biosynthesis in toxigenic genera such as Aspergillus and Fusarium, thereby supporting early risk assessment and targeted intervention strategies. AI-assisted biocontrol frameworks that combine virtual microbial modeling with nanoparticle based approaches have also been proposed to support the sustainable management of vascular fungal pathogens (Gomez-Lama Cabanas and Mercado-Blanco 2025).

Finally, AIVC has been applied to fungal-mediated environmental remediation by modeling pollutant degradation pathways and metabolic responses under complex environmental conditions (Renganathan and Gaysina 2025). Representative applications include simulations of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon degradation and heavy metal adsorption, coupled with environmental parameter optimization (Dinakarkumar et al. 2024). Integration with ecological network analysis further enables the rational design of cooperative fungal consortia for adaptive and efficient pollutant degradation (Efremenko et al. 2024).

## Advancing fungal cell factories through intelligent design and operation

While AIVC provides the computational blueprint for predicting fungal behavior, intelligent strain design and dynamic fermentation serve as the physical execution layer required to realize these predictions. The construction and optimization of high-performance cell factories directly determine whether bio-based products can compete with conventional petroleum-based products in terms of cost, efficiency, and sustainability. Advances in systems and synthetic biology have enabled the successful engineering of filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus niger (Iyyappan et al. 2018), Trichoderma reesei (Druzhinina and Kubicek 2017), and Myceliophthora thermophila (Li et al. 2020) for the production of biofuels, enzymes, pharmaceuticals, and other high-value products (Stephanopoulos 2007).

### AI-enabled genome editing and expression design in filamentous fungi

Advances in whole genome synthesis and precise genome editing tools have transitioned filamentous fungal engineering from low-throughput, trial, and error strategies to highly efficient, programmable approaches. CRISPR/Cas systems have dramatically lowered the technical barriers for multi-gene manipulations, enabling routine multi-locus knockouts, overexpression, and reconstruction of regulatory elements. Liu et al. (2017) established a high-efficiency CRISPR/Cas9 system in M. thermophila, capable of simultaneously editing 1–4 genomic loci with homologous recombination efficiencies of approximately 90%, 70%, 30%, and 22%, respectively. Using this system, they rationally engineered cellulase overproducing strains, significantly enhancing extracellular protein secretion and overall lignocellulose degrading enzyme activity, demonstrating the practical utility of CRISPR/Cas9 in industrial strain optimization. Similarly, Li et al. (2024b) constructed a CRISPR HDR system in A. niger, enabling the synchronous integration of a single gene at three genomic loci, thereby shortening editing cycles and improving integration efficiency.

Despite these advances, performance optimization remains necessary, particularly in enhancing the activity of single-guide RNA (sgRNA) and minimizing off-target effects (Chari et al. 2017). Recent studies have also explored the use of fungal fructosyltransferases for the production of prebiotics like fructooligosaccharides (FOS), which offer health benefits and could enhance fungal cell factory applications (Rawat et al. 2024). Early studies employed traditional machine learning approaches, such as support vector machines (SVMs), to predict CRISPR-Cas9 efficiency (Doench et al. 2016), yet were limited by data scale and feature representation. Recently, the integration of high-throughput screening with deep learning has significantly improved the accuracy of sgRNA activity prediction. For example, DeepCpf1 employs deep neural networks trained on large datasets derived from the Acidaminococcus sp. Cpf1 system (asCpf1) to model massive sgRNA sequences, enabling precise prediction of target gene knockout efficiency (Kim et al. 2018). This approach overcomes the limitations of conventional SVM models in handling large-scale data and complex sequence features, capturing nonlinear relationships and sequence patterns that improve sgRNA activity and specificity predictions. Overall, coupling deep learning with high-throughput experimental data provides a robust computational framework for sgRNA design and optimization of genome editing efficiency, laying a foundation for high-precision microbial engineering and next-generation genome engineering strategies.

Although efficient, precise, and scalable genome editing has been achieved, further enhancement of filamentous fungal cell factory performance relies on systematic optimization of gene sequence design, expression regulation, and metabolic resource allocation. In the context of heterologous protein production, single-layer genetic modifications are often insufficient to fully realize the engineering potential. Consequently, research emphasis has shifted from “whether editing is possible” to “how to design rationally”. Data-driven methods, such as machine learning, are increasingly employed to decipher the determinants of heterologous protein expression efficiency and to provide quantitative guidance for multi-level engineering strategies.

Machine learning models can accurately predict heterologous protein expression in A. niger (Peng and de Vries 2021). By learning from large-scale sequence and expression data, these models identify key features that affect transcription, translation, and secretion, enabling the rational design of high-efficiency expression systems. Transcription factors like MtfA regulate the biosynthesis of metabolites such as AICAR in Fusarium solani, offering insights for optimizing fungal expression systems (Chen et al. 2026). Wu et al. (2018) used machine learning to analyze codon usage preferences in A. niger and optimized the heterologous Lac26 gene, significantly improving translation efficiency. Furthermore, overexpressing a key transcription factor and deleting a competing pathway gene redistributed intracellular resources and increased β-galactosidase production. Beyond gene-level optimization, machine learning has been widely applied to systematically screen and optimize regulatory elements, including promoter and signal peptide combinations. Rationally designed promoter-signal peptide modules significantly enhance heterologous protein secretion and extracellular accumulation (Zheng et al. 2025). The integration of machine learning into the design-build-test-learn (DBTL) cycle enables iterative optimization of promoter-signal peptide combinations, not only improving expression efficiency but also stabilizing production across fermentation batches (Sui et al. 2020).

With the continuous accumulation of multi-omics data (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics) and advancements in deep learning for feature representation and nonlinear modeling, it is anticipated that key molecular mechanisms limiting heterologous protein expression can be elucidated more deeply, rate-limiting steps accurately identified, and multi-level coordination achieved across sequence design, regulatory networks, and metabolic resource allocation. This will enable the construction of robust, end-to-end platforms for expressing proteins from filamentous fungi in industrial applications.

### AI-driven metabolic network modeling and cell factory design

With advancements in genome editing and computational design, filamentous fungi engineering has progressed from local, pathway-specific optimization to system-wide, genome-scale design. This transition enables metabolic network reconstruction: modular design and precise flux control allow integration of heterologous pathways to surpass host metabolic limitations (Li et al. 2020), while synthetic pathways can be constructed to reprogram carbon and energy allocation (Annaluru et al. 2014), expanding the production potential of high-value chemicals (Gu et al. 2021), pharmaceutical precursors, and functional materials (Wang and Doudna 2023). High-throughput automation facilitates the rapid implementation of gene manipulations (Zhang et al. 2024). Nevertheless, cellular metabolism is intrinsically complex, involving multi-level regulation, pathway crosstalk, feedback loops, and competition for limited resources.

In this context, traditional trial-and-error metabolic engineering is inadequate to address high-dimensional design spaces and systemic complexity. Computational modeling has thus emerged as a critical bridge between gene manipulations and phenotypic outcomes. Genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs), computational frameworks that integrate gene-protein-reaction (GPR) relationships to represent cellular metabolism at the genome scale, describe metabolic network structure and flux distributions globally, enabling the prediction of metabolic bottlenecks, identification of engineering targets, and evaluation of design feasibility (Gu et al. 2019).

For industrial chassis strains, GEMs guide the selection of productivity-related genes and serve as scaffolds for multi-omics integration to elucidate the mechanisms underlying desired phenotypes (Mccloskey et al. 2013). Redox engineering in Myceliophthora thermophila has improved the production of organic acids like L-malic acid by optimizing NADH/NAD+ ratios, highlighting the importance of metabolic optimization in fungal cell factories (Zhang et al. 2026). In A. niger, GEMs such as iMA871, iHL1210, and iJB1325 have been developed (Brandl et al. 2018). Upton et al. (2017) extended these models to include dynamic flux balance analysis, capturing time-dependent metabolic features during citric acid fermentation and validating model predictions against experimental data. Multi-gene and multi-pathway engineering exponentially increases design complexity, making exhaustive search computationally intractable. Metaheuristic algorithms, including genetic and evolutionary algorithms, have been employed to efficiently explore these high-dimensional spaces. Upton et al. (2020) combined dynamic GEMs with a genetic algorithm and a high-efficiency fitness function to optimize organic acid production under batch fermentation conditions.

Myceliophthora thermophila is a thermophilic filamentous fungus with industrial potential due to its broad substrate spectrum and high temperature tolerance (Berka et al. 2011). Its complex metabolic network has historically limited rational engineering. Liu et al. (2022) constructed the first genome-scale metabolic model, iDL1450, comprising 1450 genes, 2592 reactions, and 1784 metabolites, which covers central metabolism and lignocellulose degradation pathways. Model validation demonstrated accurate predictions of carbon and nitrogen utilization, and integration with transcriptomics revealed temperature-dependent metabolic reprogramming. Although iDL1450 is a valuable tool for studying Myceliophthora thermophila metabolism, traditional GEMs rely on stoichiometric constraints and often overlook enzyme capacity, dynamic metabolic changes, and complex physiological feedback, limiting their utility in industrial strain design. To overcome these limitations, Liu et al. (2022) refined iDL1450 to generate the next-generation genome-scale model iYW1475. Using the ECMpy workflow to incorporate enzyme constraints, they developed an enzyme-constrained GEM (ecGEM) that enhances metabolic prediction accuracy, provides a physiologically relevant framework for analyzing metabolic regulation, and enables model-guided strategies for improving strain performance, thereby facilitating its application in lignocellulosic bioconversion and industrial biomanufacturing (Wang et al. 2024).

Using the genome-scale metabolic model, researchers have successfully applied M. thermophila to produce high-value chemicals. Computational analysis of the model identified three cytosolic enzymes involved in succinate formation: fumarate reductases (FR1 and FR2), isocitrate lyases (ICL1 and ICL2), and succinate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase (UGA)-and revealed that most byproduct succinate is primarily generated in mitochondria. Guided by these insights, researchers implemented metabolic engineering strategies to optimize carbon flux, including modulation of key enzyme activities, redirection of intermediate metabolites, and suppression of mitochondrial succinate formation. These interventions resulted in a high-yield malate-producing strain with minimal succinate byproducts, thereby significantly enhancing product purity and industrial applicability (Gu et al. 2024).

The integration of AI, particularly machine learning and deep learning models, enables the processing of complex experimental, omics, and metabolic simulation data, facilitating efficient strain design and metabolic pathway optimization. AI excels at extracting patterns from large datasets, performing global optimization, predicting optimal gene-editing strategies, and rapidly identifying rate-limiting steps, thereby providing effective solutions for metabolic engineering. Yet even optimally engineered strains require bioprocess environments that maintain the desired cellular state; therefore, intelligent fermentation becomes the execution layer that connects strain design to industrial performance.

### AI-assisted intelligent fermentation optimization

Building upon the task-specific AI tools discussed above, an integrated framework is required to coordinate strain design, metabolic regulation, and bioprocess optimization. From the perspective of an AIVC, fermentation represents the macroscopic manifestation of cellular state dynamics under controlled environmental perturbations. Traditional microbial fermentation relies on trial-and-error or static design-of-experiment (DoE) approaches (Witek-Krowiak et al. 2014). With the rise of smart bioprocessing, control strategies are shifting from reactive to predictive, leveraging data-driven modeling for decision support (Huang et al. 2025). Machine learning models are increasingly used to predict titers and optimize processes by capturing nonlinear dynamics and identifying key variables that affect production variability (Li et al. 2024a).

Within an AIVC framework, intelligent fermentation can be conceptualized as a closed-loop state-action-feedback system, where sensor-derived observations continuously update the virtual cell state, guiding real-time control decisions. Intelligent fermentation integrates online sensors (e.g., Raman spectroscopy, DO/pH probes) and multi-dimensional growth monitoring (temperature, pH, substrate concentration) to dynamically adjust feeding, agitation, and induction, preventing metabolic overload and maximizing productivity. Sharma and Mishra (2022) employed artificial neural networks (ANN) in combination with response surface methodology (RSM) to optimize fermentation parameters, resulting in a 1.36-fold increase in L-asparaginase activity. The integration of real-time data acquisition, dynamic process control, and machine learning algorithms enables intelligent systems to provide precise and scalable strategies for heterologous protein production. High-throughput transcriptomics (RNA-seq) provides global insights into regulatory and metabolic pathways, transforming fermentation from a “black box” to a predictable, mechanism-based system (Xu et al. 2025b). From a virtual cell perspective, high-dimensional Raman and omics signals correspond to partial and noisy observations of latent cellular states, necessitating representation learning and probabilistic modeling to improve state observability (Esmonde-White et al. 2022). However, online analytical techniques such as Raman spectroscopy face challenges, including high dimensionality, collinearity, and signal interference (Annaluru et al. 2014). To address this, data-driven modeling is applied to extract key features from high-dimensional spectral data and build predictive models. Random forest (RF) excels in modeling complex, nonlinear relationships and evaluating feature importance (Zhu et al. 2022), while Bayesian ridge (BR) provides robust estimation under high-dimensional and collinear conditions (Xu et al. 2025a). As a case in point, Wang et al. (2026) developed a process-level digital twin that serves as an AIVC execution layer, integrating data-driven modeling, mechanistic analysis, and real-time sensing to dynamically control α-amylase production, resulting in significant yield improvements. This study demonstrates a modular and intelligent precision fermentation strategy, integrating predictive modeling, omics analysis, and sensor-based control to establish a new paradigm for automated and intelligent bioprocesses.

Collectively, AI-assisted intelligent fermentation constitutes the process-level execution layer of the AIVC. Looking forward, AI-driven filamentous fungal biomanufacturing is expected to revolutionize industrial biotechnology by enabling highly efficient, precise, and adaptive production systems. The integration of machine learning and deep learning with multi-omics datasets, GEMs, and high-throughput genome editing will allow the rational design of strains with optimized metabolic pathways, regulatory networks, and resource allocation. Future systems will likely feature autonomous, real-time fermentation control that dynamically adjusts nutrient supply, environmental conditions, and gene expression to maximize product yield and stability. Such AI-enabled platforms will accelerate the development of fungal cell factories for diverse applications, including bio-based chemicals, enzymes, pharmaceuticals, and sustainable fuels, while reducing experimental costs and timelines. ## Conclusions

Although AI technologies have shown significant promise, many components of the AIVC framework remain under development. Current models often rely on incomplete datasets and simplified biological assumptions, and their predictive performance may vary across species, experimental conditions, and industrial scales. Fungi, particularly filamentous fungi, offer exceptional potential for sustainable biomanufacturing, but their industrial applications are constrained by eukaryotic regulation, compartmentalized metabolism, and morphology-dependent productivity. This review highlights how AI is transforming fungal biotechnology, from BGC discovery and functional annotation to genome editing, metabolic network modeling, and intelligent fermentation. Recent advances show a shift from isolated, task-specific AI tools to predictive and controllable fungal cell factory design.

A key insight from the literature is that performance improvements rely on coordinating molecular design, cellular state, and bioprocess execution, rather than optimizing single genes or pathways. In filamentous fungi, secretion capacity, organelle organization, hyphal growth, and stress responses are tightly interconnected; therefore, optimization in one layer can be undone by bottlenecks in another. This interdependence drives hybrid strategies that integrate mechanistic models (e.g., GEMs) with machine learning, enabling accurate and actionable predictions.

Despite fast progress, challenges remain. First, the “black box” nature of many deep learning architectures limits mechanistic interpretability, making it difficult to troubleshoot in vivo when AI-guided predictions fail. Second, current computational models struggle to accurately simulate the complex metabolic burden and cellular stress responses induced by heterologous protein expression, often leading to overestimations of yield. Furthermore, data limitations remain fundamental, as fungal multi-omics and bioprocess datasets are sparse, heterogeneous, and lack standardized metadata. Finally, a severe scalability gap exists: AI models trained on highly controlled, lab-scale omics data frequently fail to predict fungal behavior in industrial-scale bioreactors, where physical factors such as shear stress and heterogeneous mass transfer dominate.

Looking forward, progress toward autonomous fungal biomanufacturing would likely depend on a coordinated framework that integrates standardized data, hybrid modeling, and real-time control. To transition this long-term vision from task-specific models toward holistic, controllable digital twins, the field must target three concrete milestones achievable within the near future: (1) establishing standardized community datasets with FAIR metadata for fungal multi-omics; (2) developing the first generation of benchmark fungal AIVCs that couple mechanistic models with machine learning; and (3) demonstrating fully closed-loop DBTL cycles equipped with defined online sensor arrays for real-time bioprocess control. By integrating heterogeneous biological data into a unified, dynamic simulation, the AIVC framework provides critical pathways to achieving the milestones.

Ultimately, moving from AI for individual tasks to AI for integrated fungal systems is expected to define the next phase of fungal biotechnology and its impact on the green industry. Future research should therefore prioritize unified computational frameworks that couple mechanistic models with machine learning, establish standardized and shareable datasets, and enable closed-loop DBTL cycles, thereby accelerating the development of robust, scalable, and sustainable fungal cell factories.
