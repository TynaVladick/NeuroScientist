## Abstract

Biomimetic and advanced membrane technologies are emerging as powerful platforms bridging biomedical and environmental applications, particularly in cancer therapy and water purification. However, current research remains fragmented, with limited integration of design principles across these domains. This review addresses this gap by presenting a unified analytical framework to systematically evaluate diverse membrane systems, including biomimetic nanoparticle membranes, exosome-based membranes, implantable devices, ultrathin functional membranes, graphene/2D membranes, and more. We comparatively assess their structural features, functional performance, industrial and clinical maturity, and scalability to highlight their complementary roles in targeted drug delivery, biosensing, and contaminant removal. Emphasis is placed on translational challenges, including manufacturing complexity, standardization, and regulatory constraints. Furthermore, emerging strategies such as AI-driven design, hybrid membrane engineering, and sensing-separation integration are critically examined as pathways to enhance performance and real-world applicability. This work provides a coherent foundation for cross-disciplinary innovation and supports the development of scalable, high-performance membrane systems for precision medicine and sustainable environmental technologies.

## Introduction

Membrane technologies have evolved from passive laboratory filters to dynamic, multifunctional interfaces capable of molecular recognition, selective transport, and signal responsiveness. , This transformation positions them at the forefront of cancer therapy and environmental health, where both fields demand molecular precision, whether targeting tumor cells or removing ecosystem pollutants. The same design principles enabling biocompatible, stimuli-responsive membranes for drug delivery also underpin robust, energy-efficient systems for water purification and pollution mitigation. −

Water quality is fundamentally connected to public health and remains a critical global concern. As highlighted by Ritchie, nearly two billion people still lack access to safe drinking water. Moreover, climate change is making the situation worse by increasing both scarcity and contamination risks. Membrane-based desalination and purification systems, such as reverse osmosis (RO), nanofiltration (NF), and ultrafiltration (UF), have become essential for providing safe water supplies. These technologies represent a promising approach to building sustainable health infrastructure, focusing on prevention and environmental care rather than just treating problems after they arise. However, significant problems remain to be overcome, such as fouling, high energy use, and chemical waste, which limit their scalability and sustainability. Interestingly, new biomimetic and nanostructured membranes, inspired by natural systems like aquaporins, are tackling these challenges with better selectivity, self-cleaning abilities, and reduced energy needs, achieving 2–100x higher permeability than conventional polymeric membranes, as reported by Lee et al. In line with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, the quest for clean water for everyone highlights how innovations in membrane technology are vital for promoting global health equity and protecting the environment.

Similarly, biomedical membranes are important components of precision medicine. Their capacity for managing mass transport and molecular interactions makes them crucial in cancer treatment. , According to Zafar et al., traditional cancer therapies often struggle with issues like systemic toxicity, poor targeting efficiency, and resistance development. However, membrane-based systems, including cell-membrane-coated nanoparticles, exosome-mimetic vesicles, and hybrid lipid-polymer assemblies, offer innovative solutions to these problems by emulating the body’s natural communication and defense strategies. , Likewise, exosome membranes can transport therapeutic agents across biological barriers while reducing unwanted side effects. These bioinspired designs achieve both therapeutic precision and biocompatibility, showcasing the future of cancer treatments. , Beyond oncology, membrane technologies support life-sustaining processes such as dialysis, plasma exchange, and pathogen filtration, underscoring their expanding role in contemporary medicine.

Recent progress in membrane research increasingly integrates materials science, nanotechnology, synthetic biology, and artificial intelligence (AI) into unified design frameworks. AI and machine learning (ML) enable predictive membrane design, optimization of operational parameters, and adaptive control strategies across clinical and environmental applications. These interdisciplinary advances are accelerating the emergence of smart membranes: systems capable of sensing environmental stimuli, responding dynamically, and self-regulating in real time. At the same time, the rapid development of advanced membrane technologies raises important safety, governance, and sustainability concerns. Many high-performance membrane systems rely on nanomaterials, surface modifiers, and synthetic polymers, whose long-term environmental impacts, lifecycle burdens, and disposal pathways remain insufficiently characterized.

Although biomedical and environmental membrane systems are often studied separately, their integration offers significant opportunities for innovation. Environmental engineering strategies, including antifouling design, sustainable manufacturing, and low-energy separations, may improve biomedical membrane platforms, while advances in biological recognition and targeted transport from oncology can inform next-generation environmental sensing and purification technologies. This Review therefore examines the emerging synergies between membrane technologies in cancer therapy and environmental health, emphasizing shared design principles, translational challenges, and future opportunities for improving global health and environmental sustainability.

#### Biomimetic Nanoparticle Membranes

Biomimetic nanoparticle membranes are gaining traction in translational nanomedicine because they leverage natural cell membrane properties to enhance therapeutic delivery. , Xu et al. position biomimetic nanoparticle membranes as an innovative strategy for improving anticancer nanovaccines by mimicking natural cell interfaces to improve immune targeting, antigen delivery, circulation stability, and therapeutic specificity. By masking nanoparticles with natural cell membranes, these systems mimic endogenous biological interactions, thereby increasing the effectiveness of targeting while reducing immune clearance and side effects. Importantly, the functional behavior of these nanoparticles is strongly influenced by the biological origin of the membrane coating. Membranes derived from red blood cells, cancer cells, or immune cells retain surface-associated proteins and signaling molecules, including CD47, adhesion receptors, and tumor-associated antigens that regulate immune recognition, cell adhesion, and tissue targeting. , Studies conducted by Shen et al., Zhang et al., Zhang et al., and Zhu et al. demonstrated that these membrane signals can reduce macrophage-mediated clearance, expand systemic circulation, and increase tumor accumulation compared to unmodified nanoparticles. Unlike traditional synthetic surface functionalization, biomimetic coatings provide biologically active interfaces capable of engaging endogenous guidance mechanisms and receptor-mediated targeting pathways. In addition, membranes derived from immune cells can guide these nanoparticles to areas that are hypoxic or inflamed, expanding their use in chemotherapy, phototherapy, and immunotherapy. ,,

Erythrocyte membrane-coated nanoparticles utilize “self” markers like CD47, enabling immune escape and extended circulation. , Zhang et al. and Bao et al. used nanoparticles coated with erythrocyte membranes in starvation therapy, where nanoparticles saturated with glucose oxidase deplete nutrients for the tumor, and in photodynamic therapy, where erythrocyte membranes improve oxygen delivery and singlet oxygen production, thereby increasing therapeutic efficacy. , On the other hand, biomimetic nanoparticles on cancer cell membranes (CCMs) use homotypic recognition mechanisms that promote preferential accumulation in tumor tissues. By mimicking the surface characteristics of cancer cells, these nanoparticles evade immune control while facilitating the targeted delivery of photosensitizers and chemotherapeutic agents. Studies also demonstrate that functionalized CCM nanoparticles can modulate the tumor microenvironment by depleting glutathione, thereby improving treatments that depend on the formation of reactive oxygen species. ,

Platelet-membrane-coated nanoparticles exploit the natural roles of platelets in vascular adhesion, immune modulation, and tumor interaction to enhance targeted therapeutic delivery. Through the incorporation of platelet-derived membranes onto nanocarrier surfaces, these systems retain adhesion molecules such as P-selectin, which mediate interactions with complementary receptors expressed on cancer cells, as well as immune-regulatory proteins, including CD47, CD55, and CD59, that suppress immune recognition and complement-mediated clearance. This combination of adhesive and immune-evading properties contributes to prolonged systemic circulation and enhanced tumor localization relative to conventional nanoparticles. Beyond passive targeting, platelet-membrane-coated nanoparticles have demonstrated multifunctional therapeutic potential. Ortiz-Otero et al., Luo et al., and Zhang et al. designed platelet-membrane-based systems for the codelivery of doxorubicin and TRAIL, achieving synergistic antitumor activity while simultaneously reducing metastatic progression. Hybrid platelet-lipid membrane systems further extend this functionality by enabling pH-responsive drug release within acidic tumor microenvironments, thereby improving localized therapeutic activation and minimizing off-target toxicity. ,

Moreover, membranes derived from lymphocytes, especially from T cells and natural killer cells (NK cells), use endogenous immune recognition mechanisms to enhance tumor targeting and therapeutic specificity. , These membranes preserve surface receptors involved in the recognition of malignant cells and immune surveillance, allowing biomimetic nanoparticles to move through the complex microenvironment of the tumor with greater selectivity than conventional nanocarriers. Nanoparticles coated with NK cell membranes have particularly high functional characteristics since they retain native cytotoxic ligands and activating receptors responsible for interacting with tumor cells and activating immunity. − Such a biologically active surface structure not only enhances direct antitumor interaction but also improves the local and long-term delivery of chemotherapeutic drugs. Consequently, nanoparticles coated with NK membranes demonstrate superior therapeutic efficacy compared to traditional liposomal preparations, which underscores the importance of membrane functionality of immune origin in the development of nanotherapeutics for the treatment of next-generation cancers.

In summary, biomimetic nanoparticle membranes represent a significant advance in nanomedicine, combining the biological functions of natural cell membranes with the versatility of developed nanomaterials. By helping to overcome immunity, providing targeted delivery, and enabling multifunctional therapeutic action, these platforms open up great prospects for improving the effectiveness and safety of cancer treatment, immunotherapy, and next-generation nanovaccine systems. Continued advances in membrane engineering and biomimetic design are expected to accelerate their transition from experimental research to clinical applications.

#### Exosome Membranes

Exosome membranes are emerging as important platforms in cancer diagnostics and targeted therapy because they combine intrinsic biocompatibility with efficient intercellular communication capabilities. − Their native membrane composition, including surface proteins, adhesion molecules, and lipid architectures, enables selective cellular targeting, immune modulation, and efficient intracellular delivery, supporting applications in chemotherapeutics, siRNA, gene editing, and immunoregulatory cargo transport. Xu et al. further argued that exosomal membranes enhance dendritic cell uptake and antigen cross-presentation, highlighting their immunotherapeutic potential. In addition, the nucleic acids, proteins, and metabolites encapsulated within exosomes reflect the physiological state of parent cells, making them valuable biomarkers for liquid biopsy and disease monitoring in precision oncology. Significant progress has been made in engineering exosomes to improve therapeutic efficacy, targeting specificity, and manufacturing scalability. Advances in isolation, purification, and cargo-loading strategies, including electroporation, membrane fusion, incubation-based loading, and genetically engineered donor cells, have expanded the use of exosomes for delivering small molecules, RNA therapeutics, and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) components while minimizing systemic toxicity and improving tumor penetration. , Hybrid systems combining exosomal membranes with synthetic nanoparticle cores further integrate the biological specificity of natural vesicles with the structural tunability and payload flexibility of engineered nanocarriers. These hybrid vesicles demonstrate improved structural stability, drug-loading capacity, and pharmacokinetic performance, supporting their potential for clinical translation.

#### Implantable Membrane-Based and Intraluminal Devices

Membrane-based implantable and intraluminal devices are crucial as innovative solutions for targeted and long-term cancer treatments. These innovative systems ensure accurate and long-term administration of chemotherapeutic drugs directly into tumor foci or surgical cavities, solving the main problems of traditional systemic delivery, such as rapid drug elimination, inadequate toxicity, and suboptimal therapeutic concentrations in solid tumors. Thanks to advances such as gel membrane tanks and stent-like coatings, these devices can provide a constant dose of drugs, ensuring that therapeutic levels are maintained for a long time with minimal systemic effects and adverse reactions. , This is especially important for patients with recurrent or metastatic cancer, where frequent local treatment is required, but invasive procedures are not possible.

Multifunctional implantable membranes have recently been developed that combine both therapeutic and diagnostic capabilities within a single device. For example, membranes made of polymers or hydrogels filled with nanoparticles or sensing elements can release drugs in a controlled manner caused by various stimuli, such as changes in temperature, pH, magnetic fields, or enzymatic activity. These smart membranes regulate their permeability based on local biochemical signals in the tumor microenvironment, which increases the accuracy of treatment and minimizes side effects. , Recently, Krajcer et al. developed photolithinated flakes of lyophilized hydrogel for the local delivery of Temozolomide (TMZ) during glioblastoma surgery. The system is based on methacrylate gelatin, chitosan, and hyaluronic acid and includes two TMZ derivatives, which are combined by photografting, which ensures controlled release. The swelling, decomposition, mechanics, release characteristics, biocompatibility, and antitumor properties of hydrogels prepared with different polymer ratios were evaluated. The adjustment of the matrix composition makes it possible to effectively regulate the physicochemical properties and kinetics of TMZ. All drugs inhibit the growth of human glioma cells while remaining nontoxic to normal cells. These materials demonstrate great potential as implantable postoperative therapies with a controlled release profile suitable for both residual tumor control and recurrence prevention.

The convergence of implantable drug delivery systems and biosensor technologies in innovative membrane designs is a new achievement. By integrating micro- or nanosensors into these membranes, it is possible to monitor the levels of drugs, oxygen, and metabolic markers in real-time. This technology paves the way for closed-loop feedback systems that can adjust the drug release regime, providing more personalized treatment approaches and ensuring that patients receive the correct dose at the correct time. These theranostic membrane implants bridge the gap between traditional drug delivery methods and intelligent biomedical devices, providing doctors with the tools they need to track treatment effectiveness and make adjustments without invasive procedures.

#### Ultrathin Functional Membranes for Biosensing

Ultrathin functional membranes enhance biosensory and diagnostic accuracy by offering nanometer-scale interfaces that perfectly recognize molecules and enable fast signal transmission. Integrated into surgical instruments, catheters, and medical devices, these membranes enable real-time detection of circulating tumor DNA, proteins, metabolites, and microvesicles, which are critical biomarkers that can determine decisions during surgery and monitor treatment effectiveness. , By using these membranes in clinical instruments, seamless connectivity with implantable drug delivery systems can be achieved, allowing simultaneous therapy and drug dosing with feedback.

These nanostructured membranes, enhanced with antibodies, aptamers, or molecularly imprinted polymers, are excellent at capturing high-affinity biomarkers. Meanwhile, the conductive and optical converter layers convert binding events into measurable electrical or photonic signals. Platforms using materials such as graphene, MXenes, or gold nanostructures have demonstrated impressive sensitivity by detecting cancer-related markers, such as carcinoembryonic antigen or KRAS mutant DNA, at incredibly low concentrations. , These advances bring biosensorics into the field of microfluidics and wearable devices, simplifying minimally invasive or continuous cancer monitoring.

#### Graphene and 2D Nanomaterial Membranes

Given their atomic-level thicknesses, exceptional mechanical strengths, and highly tunable chemical functionalities, graphene and other 2D nanomaterials have recently emerged as some of the most promising membrane platforms for advanced environmental separations. − Graphene, graphene oxide (GO), and rGO laminates are made up of densely packed nanosheets, which can be engineered to have precisely controlled interlayer spacings that enable rapid water permeation through nanoconfined channels with selective rejection of ions, heavy metals, organic micropollutants, and pathogens. , The combination of high permeability with strong contaminant rejection enables graphene-based laminates to overcome the traditional trade-off between permeability and selectivity that has restricted polymeric membrane systems. According to Ge et al., the inherent structural versatility of GO-based membranes provides additional opportunities for enhanced water purification and desalination performance. GO laminates may be engineered to tune hydrophilicity, electrostatic charge, and chemical stability by functionalization of oxygen-containing groups, control of oxidation levels, and incorporation of cross-linkers. These modifications substantially enhance selectivity for emerging contaminants, including pharmaceuticals, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), and endocrine disruptors, without exhibiting swelling or delamination under variable ionic strengths. ,, The rigid yet flexible architecture of 2D nanosheets facilitates ultrathin composite structures with high flux and energy-efficient separation at minimal applied pressure.

Together, graphene and 2D nanomaterial membranes represent a revolutionary breakthrough in the science of environmental separation. Their unique combination of ultrafast transportation, high selectivity, customizable functionality, and energy efficiency allows them to take a leading position in advanced water purification, desalination, and pollutant removal. Current developments in membrane fabrication, stability enhancement, and large-scale manufacturing are expected to further accelerate their integration into sustainable environmental treatment systems.

#### Nanomaterial-Enhanced Polymeric Membranes and Fouling Resistance

Nanomaterial-enhanced polymeric membranes have emerged as a major innovation in environmental separation technologies due to their potential to enhance permeability, selectivity, chemical durability, and resistance to fouling. ,, Basically, integrating functional nanomaterials, including TiO2, GO, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), zeolites, and MOF materials, into traditional polymeric matrices has allowed the design of novel multifunctional membranes that overcome many of the classic limitations to membrane performance, as reported in works by Hashmi et al. and Foorginezhad et al. These nanocomposite systems introduce novel surface chemistries, catalytic functionalities, and hierarchical pore structures that help improve contaminant rejection and prolong operational life.

Nanomaterial incorporation provides a major advantage in reducing membrane fouling, which is considered one of the most lingering problems in water and wastewater treatment. Organic matter, microorganisms, and inorganic scaling reduce flux, increase energy consumption, and increase the frequency of chemical cleaning cycles. Nanoparticles such as TiO2 and ZnO exhibit photocatalytic properties, which allow for the oxidative degradation of organic foulants under UV or visible-light irradiation. , Hydrophilic nanofillers, like GO and surface-functionalized silica, enhance surface wettability and decrease foulant attachment while inducing hydration layers that provide physical obstacles to biofouling. ,

Beyond antifouling action, nanomaterial-enhanced membranes exhibit enhanced selectivity and contaminant removal efficiencies. Uniform nanochannels formed by zeolite- and MOF-based fillers selectively transport water molecules while rejecting salts, heavy metals, and emerging contaminants. , Memisoglu et al. stated that CNTs and graphene derivatives enhance mechanical strength and promote fast permeation along nanoconfined pathways. A few composite membranes further embed antimicrobial agents to inactivate pathogens on contact, expanding their application scope in the production of potable water and decentralized treatment systems. ,

Thus, polymer membranes using nanomaterials represent a new class of separation technologies that combine highly efficient filtration with improved antifouling and antimicrobial properties. Further progress in the development of nanomaterials and the production of membranes is expected to expand their role in next-generation environmental and industrial separation systems.

#### NF and Removal of Emerging Contaminants

NF has become one of the most important membrane technologies for a wide spectrum of emerging contaminants, including pharmaceuticals, PFAS, pesticides, industrial solvents, and microbial pathogens. Recent innovations in NF membrane design and integrated treatment processes have drastically expanded the range of contaminants that can be effectively managed in both drinking water and wastewater systems today.

NF membranes operate through a combination of size exclusion, charge interactions, and controlled pore chemistry, allowing for high rejection of multivalent ions, organic micropollutants, and colloidal species, with a permeability higher than that of RO. , Advances in the development of thin-film composite structures and surface-functionalized polyamide layers have greatly improved flux, chemical resistance, and solute selectivity. Moreover, low-fouling hydrophilic coatings and zwitterionic functional groups are being developed to further improve resistance against natural organic matter, oil, and biological fouling.

The removal of highly persistent pollutants, most notably PFAS compounds, which have severe health effects and resist conventional treatment, has been an important area of advancement. Tailored surface charges, nanoconfined pore structures, and engineered polyamide chemistries have enabled improved PFAS rejection with reduced membrane degradation. − Similar improvements in membrane performance have been achieved for pharmaceuticals and personal care product residuescontaminants that increasingly enter water sources due to widespread consumption and poor biodegradability. ,

Coupling with complementary treatment technologies has proven to be particularly effective in NF applications. Electrochemical polishing, advanced oxidation processes, granular adsorbents, and catalytic reactors deployed upstream or downstream of NF units degrade, immobilize, or capture contaminants that would otherwise accumulate on membrane surfaces. , These hybrid configurations substantially reduce fouling, tighten effluent quality, and enhance regulatory compliance for complex wastewater streams.

In general, advances in membrane materials, surface treatment technology, and hybrid purification systems have significantly expanded the role of nanofiltration in solving emerging water quality problems. Combining a high degree of purification from pollutants, energy efficiency, and smooth integration with other purification processes, nanofiltration has become an important component of modern strategies for protecting public health, preserving aquatic ecosystems, and promoting sustainable water management.

#### Smart Membrane Sensors and Distributed Monitoring

The integration of membrane science with sensor technology has resulted in the development of smart, membrane-based sensing platforms for real-time environmental monitoring. These systems integrate functional membranes with chemiresistive, electrochemical, optical, or nanophotonic sensor components to continuously detect pollutants in air, water, soil, and industrial effluents. Their deployment within wearable devices, distributed monitoring nodes, and Internet of Things (IoT) networks is transforming environmental health management, enabling early contamination detection and rapid response to predictive intervention. −

The sensing membranes make use of selective surface chemistries, engineered nanopores, and responsive nanomaterials such as MXenes, graphene derivatives, metal nanoparticles, or quantum dots to develop high sensitivity toward chemical and biological analytes. These membrane platforms enable the detection of toxins, heavy metals, PFAS, pesticides, microplastics, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and pathogenic organisms at trace concentrations. Their integration with electrical, optical, or mechanical transduction mechanisms allows pollutant-binding events to be converted into quantifiable, real-time signals.

Collectively, intelligent membrane sensors represent a significant step forward in the field of environmental monitoring. With the development of nanomaterials, sensor integration, and the Internet of Things (IoT) infrastructure, these platforms are expected to play an increasingly important role in sustainable environmental protection, risk assessment, and public-health decision-making.

### Unified Cross-Domain Membrane Framework

#### Selective Transport Mechanisms

Selective transfer is the main principle behind the development of both biomedical and ecological membrane technologies that enable precise differentiation of targeted and nontargeted species through carefully designed biological or physicochemical interactions. Although these systems operate in different environments, they share one goal: migration management with a high degree of specificity and efficiency.

In biomedicine, selective transport is largely achieved through the recognition of biological methods. Biomimetic nanoparticle membranes use cell membrane proteins and signaling motifs to improve targeting and reduce immune clearance. For example, CD47 helps nanoparticles avoid phagocytosis, while membrane-bound adhesion molecules and receptors promote homotypic targeting and preferential accumulation in affected areas, especially in tumors. Similarly, exosome membranes use their inherent tropism and natural communication pathways to enhance cellular uptake and targeted cargo delivery, thereby enhancing both therapeutic and diagnostic performance.

When applied in the environment, selective transfer occurs mainly due to physicochemical mechanisms such as size exclusion, electrostatic repulsion, and a specially designed pore structure. For example, GO membranes use channels of nanocomponents with adjustable spacing between layers to regulate molecular transport while preventing pollutants such as PFAS, dissolved salts, and other pollutants from entering. The combined effects of spatial interference and charge-based interaction contribute to efficient water purification and desalination without compromising permeability. Similar principles underlie nanofiltration membranes, in which specially selected polyamide selective layers remove pharmaceuticals, organic micropollutants, and multivalent ions due to constant pore size and electrostatic repulsion.

Despite these general design principles, important differences remain between biological and synthetic systems. Exosome-based membranes provide highly adaptive targeting based on their natural origin, but their wider application is limited by donor variability, structural heterogeneity, and manufacturing constraints. In contrast, graphene-based nanofiltration membranes benefit from production standardization, greater batch-to-batch reproducibility, and easier expansion for environmental purification applications.

Generally, selective transfer links biomedical membrane science to environmental science within a common conceptual framework. Whether it is biological recognition or the development of separation mechanisms, continuous progress in this area improves the accuracy of target determination, separation efficiency, and functional characteristics. Future progress is likely to be achieved through hybrid designs that combine the specificities of biological systems with the controllability of synthetic membranes.

#### Antifouling and Stability Drivers

Antifouling properties and long-term stability are key priorities for the development of both biomedical and environmental membrane technologies. Despite the fact that they are used in completely different conditions, both areas strive to maintain functional characteristics while avoiding the accumulation of biological or chemical substances, as well as solid particles, which can reduce efficiency and durability.

In biomedicine, biomimetic membranes derived from platelets, NK cells, and other cellular sources are designed to function under difficult physiological conditions. These membrane-coated nanocarriers must withstand hemodynamic shear stress as well as prevent recognition and neutralization by the immune system. By preserving native membrane proteins and glycocalyx structures, they support biological functionality, increase circulation time, and enhance therapeutic efficacy under dynamic conditions in vivo. ,

A similar problem exists in environmental membranes, where fouling by organic substances, microorganisms, colloids, and inorganic deposits can significantly reduce permeability and increase operating costs. To address this problem, researchers have developed antifouling strategies that incorporate functional nanomaterials into membrane structures. For example, polymer membranes enriched with titanium dioxide (TiO2) have photocatalytic activity under ultraviolet irradiation to decompose organic pollutants directly on the membrane surface, which helps maintain filtration efficiency and reduce the need for purification. ,

The main point of contact between these two areas is protection from contamination because of humidification. Stable hydration layers on the membrane surface create an energy barrier that limits the adsorption of proteins, microbes, and organic pollutants, thereby reducing contamination and maintaining functionality. This principle has served as the basis for both biological coatings and synthetic surface modifications, emphasizing the value of biomimetic design in solving common fouling problems.

Despite these similarities, the stability requirements varied significantly. Biomedical membranes must maintain their integrity and functionality under physiological conditions characterized by biochemical variability, immune interactions, and mechanical stress. Membranes derived from exosomes, although highly biocompatible and target-specific, are often mechanically fragile and vulnerable to damage under relatively moderate stress. On the other hand, environmental membranes must withstand prolonged exposure to aggressive chemicals, pressure drops, and repetitive duty cycles while maintaining separation efficiency over time.

Knowledge exchange among these industries has accelerated the development of advanced antifouling materials. The experience of using natural membrane systems served as the basis for the creation of bioinspired and zwitterionic coatings that combine the formation of a hydration layer with improved chemical and mechanical stability. These materials have higher resistance to contamination while maintaining permeability and operational reliability, which makes them particularly attractive for nanofiltration and other advanced separation processes.

Collectively, the development of membranes to protect against fouling and make them resistant represents an important area of convergence between biomedical and environmental research. By combining hydration resistance, self-cleaning surface structures, and biomimetic design, both fields strive to create more durable and efficient membrane systems for complex real-world applications.

#### Stimuli-Responsive and Intelligent Membrane Systems

The development of membrane systems that respond to stimuli represents a significant area of convergence between biomedical and environmental technologies, reflecting a broader shift from passive separation materials to adaptive, intelligent platforms capable of perceiving and responding to dynamic conditions. These advanced membranes include sensitive materials, sensory functions, and feedback mechanisms that enable them to adapt to environmental changes in real time, thereby increasing productivity, efficiency, and operational control.

Recent advances in ultrathin membranes based on graphene and MXene have demonstrated significant potential for both sensing and separation. Due to their unique electrical, optical, and surface properties, these materials allow the detection of target analytes at very low concentrations while maintaining high stability and repeatability. Although performance varies depending on the membrane architecture, analyte characteristics, and detection mechanisms, such platforms are promising for highly sensitive monitoring and selective transfer. Their ability to simultaneously perform separation and sensing functions highlights their potential as multifunctional membrane systems for next-generation technological applications.

In biomedical settings, stimulus-sensitive membranes are increasingly integrated into closed-loop therapeutic platforms that regulate drug delivery in response to localized biological signals. Environmental factors such as pH changes, enzyme activity, redox conditions, temperature fluctuations, and disease-specific biomarkers can activate membrane reactions that control the timing, rate, and localization of therapeutic agent release. These adaptive systems enhance the accuracy of treatment by ensuring that therapeutic drugs are delivered only when and where they are needed, thereby increasing their effectiveness and minimizing adverse side effects.

Similar principles are applied in environmental engineering, where sensitive membrane systems provide continuous monitoring of pollutants, intelligent fouling detection, and adaptive control of water- and wastewater-treatment processes. By integrating sensor functions directly into the membrane structures, these systems can provide real-time information on pollutant concentrations, membrane conditions, and cleaning efficiency, allowing for rapid response to changing environmental conditions and reducing the risk of system failure. ## Multifaceted Challenges in Membrane Technologies

Advanced membrane systems for biomedical and environmental applications have made significant technological progress, but a number of interrelated challenges continue to limit their widespread adoption, commercial implementation, and long-term sustainability. One of the most important issues is scalability and reproducibility. Although membrane systems developed in the laboratory often demonstrate exceptional performance, the introduction of these technologies in industrial and clinical settings often leads to significant changes in nanoparticle synthesis, membrane functionalization, coating efficiency, and isolation processes. Such inconsistencies can negatively affect membrane permeability, selectivity, structural integrity, and therapeutic efficacy in biomedical applications, while reducing the efficiency of pollutant removal and the operational reliability of environmental systems. ,, Consequently, the lack of standardized production and quality control protocols remains a major obstacle to industrial production and implementation in clinical practice.

Safety and environmental impacts are also important factors in the development of membrane technologies. The increasing use of nanomaterials, synthetic polymers, and hybrid nanocomposites creates uncertainty about toxicity, biocompatibility, and long-term environmental resistance. Potential risks, such as the leaching of nanoparticles, toxic decomposition products, additive-related toxicity, and the accumulation of persistent synthetic residues, can negatively affect both human health and environmental systems. In biomedical applications, these effects can manifest as inflammatory reactions, immune activation, or organ-specific toxicity, whereas in ecological systems, they can contribute to bioaccumulation and ecosystem disruption. , These concerns highlight the urgent need for comprehensive toxicological assessment and the development of environmentally sustainable membrane production strategies.

Regulatory constraints further complicate the implementation of advanced membrane technologies. As a result, there remains uncertainty regarding the standardization of identification characteristics, performance evaluation, safety validation, quality control, and environmental risk assessments. The lack of agreed-upon international guidelines and regulatory standards continues to delay clinical approval processes and limit widespread environmental adoption. ,

Economic and energy constraints also remain significant barriers to implementation. Synthesis, surface functionalization, purification, and integration of highly efficient nanocomposite membranes often require complex and energy-intensive manufacturing processes. These factors increase production costs and limit the availability and environmental friendliness of membrane technologies, especially in large-scale water treatment plants and healthcare systems with limited resources. It is important to note that these issues are closely related. For example, inadequate quality control measures during production expansion may increase safety concerns and reduce reproducibility, while regulatory uncertainty may discourage investment in sustainable and energy-efficient manufacturing technologies.

Similarly, membranes made of graphene and other 2D nanomaterials have exceptional transport and separation properties, but their use remains limited due to structural defects, uncontrolled pore arrangement, swelling in aquatic environments, contamination, and problems associated with the manufacture of mechanically strong, large-area membranes. ,

Addressing these multifaceted challenges will be essential to accelerate the adoption of next-generation membrane technologies in the clinical, industrial, and environmental fields.

## Cross-Cutting Accelerators in Membrane Technologies

### AI, ML, and Digital Twins

In membrane science, AI and ML are very important, shifting the focus from traditional experimentation to a more predictive, data-driven approach. In new materials discovery, ML algorithms excel at uncovering intricate connections between chemical makeup, nanostructures, and surface functionalities. , This capability allows for the rapid identification of the best formulations tailored for specific separation or therapeutic objectives. Advanced ML models allow better prediction of membrane properties such as permeability, fouling resistance, and selectivity based on molecular characteristics. ML models (GNNs/RF classifiers) predict permeability/selectivity based on molecular data, reducing trial and error by 50–70%. , Digital twins transform the operation of membranes by simulating flows, pollution, and mechanical wear in real time using sensor data and AI analytics. They simulate the dynamics of pollution and flows at wastewater treatment plants, allowing operators to test scenarios without disrupting current processes. This reflects the results of studies by Lian et al. on wastewater modeling and Møller et al. on efficiency tracking, although the specifics depend on implementation. Pilot tests have shown a 20–30% reduction in energy consumption due to optimization of aeration and dosing, which is consistent with the stated reduction of nitrogen emissions by up to 30% and energy savings of almost 25%. Combining real-time data with predictive models, as in the work of Cao et al., reduces downtime by providing advance notification of pollution trends. According to Woo et al., AI-enhanced twins extend membrane life by 18 days, increase permeability by 0.66%, and reduce chemical/pumping costs. They provide a balance between parameters such as wastewater quality and energy consumption, ensuring compliance and environmental friendliness in full-scale MBR plants. In the field of biomedicine, AI is revolutionizing the application of exosomes and nanoparticles by predicting pharmacokinetics, delivery accuracy, and immune responses, while environmental filtration benefits from adaptive control of pollutants through AI. AI models predict the pharmacokinetics and stability of exosomes by analyzing large sets of electronic data, which makes it possible to optimize membrane-coated nanoparticles for targeted delivery at the preclinical stage. , These tools model in vivo behaviors such as immune interactions, accelerating the development of therapies similar to Papachristou et al.’s new ideas in the field of electromechanics. AI is expected to drive breakthrough developments in the field of direct osmosis membranes to combat microcontamination, dynamically adjusting parameters to extend the life of the membranes. The reviews emphasize the role of AI in predicting and controlling fouling, increasing reliability, as noted by Jafari et al. and Al-Kathiri et al.

The integration of edge AI and ML algorithms in such systems has further expanded the scope of distributed membrane-sensor systems. The data-driven models allow for on-site classification of pollutants, early forecasting of contamination events, detection of anomalies, and adaptive operation of water treatment facilities. In air quality monitoring, smart membranes embedded in personal trackers or devices built into building ventilation can detect particulate matter, VOCs, airborne pathogens, and industrial gases at high temporal resolution. The combination of AI and digital twin technologies significantly enhances operational intelligence and sustainability in membrane manufacturing, as noted in the studies by Turan et al. and Jawad et al.

### Hybrid Systems and Circular Design

Hybridization accelerates the development of membrane technologies by integrating membranes with additional methods such as adsorption, catalytic oxidation, and MBRs, which increase the selectivity and efficiency of water purification. These setups simultaneously destroy pollutants, prevent the formation of deposits, and restore energy thanks to a modular design that surpasses autonomous membranes in their characteristics. For example, MBRs in combination with adsorption affect persistent pollutants while maintaining a high flow rate. In wastewater treatment, hybrids such as membrane catalytic systems break down organic substances and restore resources, which is consistent with the opinion of Jafari and coauthors on multifunctional treatment. They reduce operating costs and extend the life of the membranes through comprehensive pollution control.

In the field of biomedicine, hybrid membranes combine biological and synthetic elements to improve drug delivery, targeting, and responsiveness. A key example: nanoparticles coated with exosomes use the natural homing of vesicles for precise targeting using scalable synthetic scaffolds, as investigated by Chen et al. Lipid-polymer hybrids encapsulate therapeutic drugs for activated release under the influence of pH, temperature, or enzymes, thereby improving bioavailability. According to Jacob and coauthors, these platforms enable personalized medicine by combining biocompatibility with customizable mechanics, allowing laboratory prototypes to be created on an industrial scale. They reduce side effects and increase the effectiveness of cancer treatment and regenerative therapy.

Hybrid membrane designs prioritize circularity through life cycle assessments, recoverable nanomaterials, green solvents, and recyclable modules to minimize environmental footprints. Closed-loop systems allow for the extraction of metals and solvents from spent membranes, reducing waste and improving resource exchange during the production process. LCAs determine the choice of materials from the very beginning, ensuring a balance between high performance and low impact. These principles transform membranes into self-healing elements that perceive, separate, and adapt, as emphasized in the work of Jafari et al. Thus, hybridization promotes the prosperity of multifunctional systems in a closed economy without compromising efficiency.

For implantable systems, extended in vivo studies, often spanning at least one year, are necessary to evaluate the biodegradation behavior, chronic inflammation, and long-term biocompatibility before widespread clinical adoption can occur.

### Economic Evaluation

Economic feasibility remains a decisive factor for the large-scale adoption of next-generation membrane technologies. Conventional polymeric membranes continue to dominate industrial markets because they benefit from mature supply chains, established manufacturing, and reliable operational performance. Published cost estimates for specific polymeric membrane sheets fall in the double-digit USD/m2 range, while operating costs for RO-treated water are often reported in the subdollar per m3 range, depending on plant scale and energy conditions. ,

Graphene-based membranes are currently more expensive to manufacture than conventional polymeric membranes because of the complexity of nanomaterial synthesis and defect control. Scalable roll-to-roll fabrication is being developed to improve manufacturability and may reduce costs over time. Their potential operational advantages, especially higher permeability and lower pressure requirements, could improve long-term economic competitiveness in selected separation applications. ,

Biomimetic nanoparticles and exosome-derived systems remain among the most manufacturing-intensive platforms because of low-yield upstream production, heterogeneous product populations, and labor-intensive purification workflows, such as ultracentrifugation. Emerging microfluidic and standardized bioprocessing strategies are expected to improve reproducibility, scalability, and GMP compatibility, although their impact on absolute production costs remains process-specific. ,

Regardless of higher upfront costs, many novel membrane technologies may offer favorable economics through improved permeability, enhanced selectivity, longer operational lifetimes, and reduced fouling, all of which can lower the total cost of ownership over time.

Traditional separation and dialysis membranes have reached technological maturity and are currently focused on gradually increasing productivity, while advanced multifunctional membrane systems with bioinspiration and digital control are still under development or implementation. ### Standardized Characterization Pipelines for Biomimetic and Vesicle Membranes

Biomimetic and vesicle-derived membrane systems, such as exosomes, cell-membrane-coated nanoparticles, and hybrid vesicular constructs, are crucial for cancer diagnostics. , However, despite their potential, reliable manufacturing and clinical application still face challenges due to the unavailability of raw data for comparative analysis, coating efficiency, source-dependent differences, and functional stability. To tackle these issues, it is crucial to establish standardized characterization pipelines that ensure consistent performance, allow for comparability across studies, and meet regulatory standards for identity, purity, potency, and safety.

Taha et al. proposed that a solid characterization framework should clearly define and quantify CQAs across various domains, including physicochemical, biological, and functional aspects. Important physicochemical parameters highlighted by Kurtjak et al. are particle size distribution, membrane morphology, surface charge, and general structural integrity, which are typically evaluated using techniques like nanoparticle tracking analysis, dynamic light scattering, high-resolution microscopy (such as cryo-TEM), and atomic force microscopy. On the biological side, CQAs include membrane protein composition, ligand density, lipid profiles, and molecular cargo such as microRNAs or oncogenic proteins that could impact therapeutic outcomes or immunogenicity, particularly for exosomes. ,,

Because biomimetic membranes exhibit significant biological variability, the harmonization of analytical workflows across laboratories is essential. Standardized protocols for membrane extraction, purification, coating, storage, and exosome isolation methods, including ultracentrifugation, size-exclusion chromatography, and microfluidic sorting, are needed to improve reproducibility and enable meaningful cross-study comparisons. Advanced analytical techniques such as mass spectrometry-based proteomics, flow cytometry, and surface marker profiling should be integrated with computational tools, including molecular dynamics simulations-aided profiling, to predict membrane stability, ligand orientation, biodistribution, and batch-to-batch variability. In parallel, the establishment of GMP-aligned workflows, internationally recognized reference materials, validated analytical methods, and consensus reporting standards is critical for regulatory approval and industrial translation. Collaborative efforts among academia, industry, and regulatory agencies are therefore essential to develop standardized, multitiered characterization pipelines that ensure the reproducibility, safety, scalability, and clinical reliability of biomimetic and vesicle-based membrane technologies.

### Pilot Demonstrations of Environmental Membranes at the Relevant Scales

For environmental applications, graphene-based laminates, 2D nanomaterials, and nanocomposite polymer membranes have demonstrated impressive separation capabilities in laboratory environments, such as desalination, pollutant removal, and pathogen filtration. , Despite these advances, large-scale implementation remains constrained by several technical limitations, including structural defects, uncontrolled pore pathways, membrane swelling in aqueous environments, fouling susceptibility, and challenges associated with fabricating mechanically robust, large-area membranes. ,, These issues continue to hinder the practical deployment of high-performance environmental membranes in real-world systems.

To bridge the gap between laboratory research and industrial applications, pilot-scale demonstrations are essential for validating membrane performance under realistic operating conditions involving complex feed streams, variable flow rates, and fluctuating contaminant loads. Such demonstrations should comprehensively assess flux stability, fouling resistance, contaminant removal efficiency, mechanical durability, and long-term operational reliability. Notably, pilot-scale membrane systems for industrial CO2 capture have already demonstrated technical feasibility and extended operational lifespans, supporting their potential for large-scale industrial integration. , Similarly, life cycle assessments incorporating energy consumption, environmental impact, and material recovery are necessary to evaluate overall sustainability and economic viability. The integration of advanced monitoring systems, including smart sensors, predictive analytics, and real-time performance tracking, can further optimize operational efficiency and identify process bottlenecks.

Over the coming years, increased municipal- and industrial-scale pilot studies are expected to generate the critical operational data needed to refine membrane designs, optimize maintenance strategies, and improve cost-effectiveness. Incorporating predictive maintenance approaches within Industry 5.0 frameworks may further enhance system reliability while minimizing operational downtime during scale-up. Collectively, systematic pilot-scale validation will play a pivotal role in accelerating the commercialization, scalability, and widespread adoption of advanced environmental membrane technologies.

### AI-Driven Material Discovery and High-Throughput Optimization

Although AI-driven membrane material optimization remains challenged by limited data sets, data heterogeneity, and algorithmic constraints, AI and ML are rapidly transforming membrane development from conventional trial-and-error approaches into predictive, data-driven design frameworks. By integrating high-throughput experimentation, digital twins, and multimodal data sets, these technologies are accelerating the discovery, optimization, and scalable manufacturing of advanced biomedical and environmental membranes while reducing development time and cost.

In biomedical applications, AI models are increasingly used to predict ligand–receptor interactions, membrane-protein compatibility, immune evasion behavior, and pharmacokinetic performance, thereby improving the design of exosome coatings, biomimetic vesicles, and hybrid nanoparticles. , These predictive approaches enhance membrane stability under physiological stresses, such as enzymatic degradation and shear forces, reducing preclinical failure rates and improving therapeutic precision. For example, biomimetic nanoparticles incorporating pH-responsive gates have demonstrated targeted drug release efficiencies approaching 90% for doxorubicin delivery within tumor microenvironments. Such rational, AI-assisted optimization enables more efficient candidate selection and facilitates the translation of laboratory-scale discoveries into clinically viable therapeutic systems.

In environmental membrane technologies, AI and ML are similarly enhancing membrane design, process monitoring, and operational performance. Edge AI-enabled sensor systems can predict membrane fouling up to 48 h in advance, enabling proactive maintenance and minimizing operational downtime. , Predictive ML models further support real-time process optimization by forecasting fouling behavior, flux decline, contaminant breakthrough, and energy consumption under variable feedwater conditions. These capabilities improve adaptive process control and operational reliability in water and wastewater treatment systems. , In parallel, ML-assisted material screening accelerates the identification of selective membrane surface chemistries optimized for the removal of contaminants such as PFAS, pharmaceuticals, and heavy metals, while simultaneously minimizing fouling and improving long-term membrane stability.

Thus, AI-driven integration of experimental data, microscopy, molecular simulations, and operational data sets is enabling scale-spanning optimization of membrane systems. This convergence supports the emerging concept of “design-on-demand” membranes tailored to specific biomedical and environmental applications with unprecedented precision, adaptability, and efficiency. Consequently, AI-enabled knowledge fusion is expected to play a central role in the development of multifunctional, next-generation membrane technologies optimized across molecular, device, and industrial scales.

### Regulatory-Academic Consortia for Translation

The successful translation of advanced membrane technologies, particularly biomimetic, exosome-derived, and hybrid biological-synthetic membrane systems, requires coordinated collaboration among academia, industry, and regulatory agencies. These membrane platforms possess complex and dynamic biological properties that often fall outside conventional regulatory classifications for biologics, medical devices, and nanomaterials, creating challenges in validating identity, potency, sterility, immunogenicity, and long-term stability. , Consequently, the establishment of regulatory-academic consortia has emerged as a critical strategy for aligning scientific innovation with evolving regulatory expectations and accelerating clinical and industrial translation.

Such consortia can provide unified frameworks for defining CQAs, validating analytical methodologies, and establishing standardized chemistry-manufacturing-control workflows compatible with GMP requirements. For biomimetic and exosome-based systems, relevant CQAs include membrane protein composition, ligand density, vesicle integrity, biological cargo retention, sterility, and functional bioactivity. Similar challenges related to heterogeneity, purity, and manufacturing reproducibility have been reported in nanovaccine production, where advanced approaches such as microfluidic-based processing are being explored to improve CQA control and facilitate progression from early TRL (3–4) toward translational and preclinical readiness (TRL 6–7). Through collaborative benchmarking and interlaboratory validation, these consortia can improve reproducibility across studies and strengthen the reliability of preclinical data sets used for regulatory submissions.

In addition, comparable collaborative frameworks are essential for environmental membrane technologies, which face increasing regulatory scrutiny regarding ecological safety, contaminant removal performance, lifecycle sustainability, and material persistence. Regulatory-academic partnerships can, therefore, support the development of standardized ecological risk assessment protocols, harmonized pollutant removal metrics, and validated environmental performance standards for applications in municipal water treatment, industrial wastewater remediation, and environmental monitoring. Furthermore, shared reference materials, centralized data infrastructures, and consensus reporting standards established through these consortia can reduce variability across laboratories while providing regulators with consistent benchmarks for evaluating emerging membrane technologies. In general, these collaborative initiatives will play a central role in facilitating regulatory acceptance, manufacturing scalability, and safe commercialization of next-generation membrane systems across biomedical and environmental sectors.

### Integration of Sensing and Separation for Adaptive Platforms

The integration of sensing and separation functionalities within a single membrane architecture represents a significant advancement toward intelligent and adaptive membrane platforms. In biomedical applications, particularly cancer theranostics, membrane systems integrated into catheters, implantable reservoirs, or surgical devices can be functionalized with ultrathin sensing layers composed of antibodies, aptamers, molecularly imprinted polymers, or conductive nanomaterials. These interfaces enable real-time detection of biomarkers, such as circulating tumor DNA, inflammatory cytokines, and metabolic indicators, while simultaneously supporting controlled therapeutic delivery. When coupled with programmable release mechanisms responsive to tumor-associated stimuli, including hypoxia, enzymatic activity, pH variation, or redox gradients, these membranes can dynamically regulate drug release according to changes within the tumor microenvironment. The incorporation of AI-assisted analytics further enhances system responsiveness by enabling predictive monitoring, early identification of therapeutic resistance, and personalized treatment optimization.

Environmental membrane technologies are similarly benefiting from the convergence of sensing and separation functions. Smart membranes equipped with electrochemical, chemiresistive, or optical sensing components can detect trace contaminants, monitor fouling progression, and identify contaminant breakthrough events in real time with high sensitivity. , These capabilities support a transition from reactive to predictive maintenance strategies, improving operational efficiency while reducing energy and resource consumption. Integration with edge computing and IoT platforms further enables continuous data acquisition, automated system feedback, and real-time operational adjustments, thereby facilitating closed-loop environmental management systems for water- and air-purification applications.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain, including sensor stability, membrane-sensor compatibility, long-term biocompatibility, and standardized data integration under complex biological and environmental conditions. Nevertheless, ongoing developments in nanomaterial functionalization, stimuli-responsive membrane chemistry, and low-power electronic systems are accelerating the feasibility of adaptive membrane technologies. Emerging multifunctional membranes featuring self-cleaning surfaces, responsive gating mechanisms, and tunable permeability demonstrate the potential of these systems to function not only as selective barriers but also as self-reporting and self-regulating components within advanced biomedical and environmental infrastructures. ,

Generally, the integration of sensing and separation capabilities is paving the way for autonomous and data-driven membrane platforms with broad applications in cancer theranostics, continuous environmental monitoring, and advanced purification systems. By combining analytical functionality with selective transport performance, adaptive membranes represent a transformative step toward responsive technologies that enhance efficiency, safety, and sustainability across the biomedical and environmental sectors.

### Future Applications of Membrane Technologies in Cancer

#### Membrane-Based Drug Delivery Systems for Cancer Therapy

Conventional chemotherapy is often limited by systemic toxicity, poor biodistribution, and insufficient tumor selectivity, which can reduce therapeutic efficacy and compromise patient safety. In response, natural, synthetic, and hybrid membrane platforms are being developed as next-generation nanocarriers and vesicular systems capable of encapsulating anticancer agents and enabling controlled, localized release within the tumor site. , Membrane carriers can protect fragile biomolecules, such as siRNA, from enzymatic degradation, improving stability, circulation time, and bioavailability, thereby strengthening the overall therapeutic performance.

Lipid-bilayer-based carriers, particularly liposomes, have long served as established drug delivery vehicles; however, emerging membrane systems are increasingly being engineered with stimuli-responsive properties, including pH sensitivity, thermoresponsiveness, redox sensitivity, magnetic responsiveness, and enzyme-triggered degradation. These smart features are intended to promote selective drug release in the tumor microenvironment while minimizing off-target exposure. In parallel, membrane functionalization with ligands targeting overexpressed cancer-associated receptors, such as folate, transferrin, and epidermal growth factor receptors, is expected to improve cellular uptake and tumor specificity. The development of dual-ligand and multiligand platforms may further enhance penetration into heterogeneous tumor tissues, thereby increasing the therapeutic index and reducing adverse effects. ,

Beyond single-agent delivery, polymeric and biomimetic membrane systems are increasingly being explored for multiplexed cargo transport, including chemotherapeutic drugs, small interfering RNA (siRNA), CRISPR-Cas components, antisense oligonucleotides, and immunomodulatory agents. Such combinatorial approaches may help overcome tumor heterogeneity and adaptive resistance by enabling the coordinated delivery of therapeutics with complementary mechanisms of action. Membrane-coated nanoparticles, especially CCM-coated systems, offer additional advantages by inheriting tumor-associated antigens that support immune evasion and homotypic targeting. Similarly, Wusiman et al. highlighted biomimetic nanovaccines as a personalized strategy for strengthening antitumor immunity and counteracting resistance. This biomimetic cloaking enhances circulation stability and tumor accumulation while reducing recognition by the immune system, making it a promising strategy for overcoming multidrug resistance in advanced malignancies. Similarly, hybrid exosome-membrane constructs combine the intrinsic biocompatibility of biological vesicles with the design flexibility of engineered systems, creating versatile nanoplatforms that may respond to external triggers such as ultrasound or magnetic fields. ,

Therefore, these innovations position membrane-based drug delivery systems as a cornerstone of next-generation precision oncology, with significant potential to improve therapeutic outcomes while reducing systemic toxicity and treatment resistance.

#### Membrane Technologies in Cancer Diagnostics and Early Detection

The trajectory of modern oncology is increasingly oriented toward precision diagnostics that are capable of identifying malignancies at their earliest stages. In this context, membrane-based platforms offer significant promise, particularly in liquid biopsy applications, where circulating tumor cells, extracellular vesicles, and nucleic acids are isolated from peripheral blood. Membranes engineered with nanoscale pores facilitate both size exclusion and affinity-based separation, enabling high-throughput and minimally invasive cancer detection. For example, functionalized membranes incorporating ligands specific to tumor-derived EVs can markedly enhance diagnostic sensitivity and specificity.

Concurrently, the integration of multifunctional membranes into lab-on-chip systems is advancing point-of-care diagnostic capabilities. Microfluidic platforms incorporating nanoporous membranes enable the simultaneous enrichment, separation, and analysis of cancer biomarkers with high efficiency. The incorporation of biosensing elements, including aptamers, antibodies, and molecularly imprinted polymers onto membrane surfaces, further allows the real-time detection and quantification of cancer-associated biomolecules. ,

In addition, membrane-enabled diagnostic systems are increasingly being adapted for use in wearable and implantable devices such as microneedle arrays, epidermal patches, and implantable biosensors. These membrane-coated devices facilitate continuous monitoring of cancer biomarkers in interstitial fluid or blood, offering opportunities for early relapse detection and real-time therapeutic monitoring. , Such innovations are poised to significantly enhance cancer surveillance and enable dynamic, personalized treatment strategies. Moreover, coupling these systems with wireless communication technologies can support remote health monitoring, thereby improving access to care in resource-limited settings.

Furthermore, nanostructured membranes are central to the development of next-generation diagnostic platforms capable of multiplexed biomarker detection. By enabling the simultaneous analysis of multiple cancer-associated markers, these systems can substantially improve diagnostic accuracy and facilitate the differentiation of cancer subtypes, a critical requirement for precision oncology.

#### MBRs in Cancer Research Models

Membrane bioreactors (MBRs) are emerging as transformative platforms for developing advanced in vitro tumor models that more accurately recapitulate the complexity of the in vivo tumor microenvironment. Traditional 2D cell culture systems inadequately represent tumor heterogeneity and cell–cell interactions, while animal models often lack translational fidelity. In contrast, membrane-supported 3D scaffolds and organ-on-chip systems provide physiologically relevant environments for cancer research, as reported by Guerrero-López et al. Semipermeable membranes integrated within microfluidic devices enable spatial compartmentalization of tumor, stromal, immune, and endothelial cells, while simultaneously facilitating controlled nutrient transport and biochemical signaling. ,

MBRs also hold substantial promise in immuno-oncology by enabling the controlled investigation of immune-tumor interactions. Platforms incorporating selectively permeable membranes allow coculture configurations in which immune and tumor cells can be either physically separated or partially integrated, thereby replicating key aspects of immune infiltration and signaling dynamics. Such systems enhance the evaluation of immunotherapeutic strategies, including chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapies, cancer vaccines, and immune checkpoint inhibitors under physiologically relevant conditions. ,

In addition, MBR-based platforms are well-positioned to advance personalized oncology. The use of patient-derived tumor cells cultured within membrane-enabled systems enables a predictive assessment of therapeutic responses, thereby supporting individualized treatment selection. These platforms can further facilitate adaptive clinical trial designs, where treatment regimens are iteratively refined based on real-time experimental feedback. Integration with AI and ML approaches offers additional potential to improve predictive accuracy and optimize clinical decision-making.

Future MBR systems are expected to increasingly replicate key physicochemical features of the tumor microenvironment, including hypoxia, acidosis, nutrient gradients, and dynamic mechanical stimuli. Such conditions are critical for modeling tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Notably, three-dimensional tumor constructs, including spheroids and patient-derived organoids, have demonstrated strong potential for investigating mechanisms of chemoresistance. Collectively, these advancements are anticipated to accelerate drug discovery by improving the predictive reliability of preclinical models and reducing attrition rates in clinical development.

#### Membrane Technologies for Photothermal and Photodynamic Therapy

Photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) are gaining significant attention as minimally invasive alternatives or adjuncts to conventional cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy. In this context, membrane-based systems have emerged as versatile platforms for the targeted delivery of photothermal agents and photosensitizers to tumor sites. Polymeric and nanostructured membranes can be engineered to encapsulate or immobilize functional nanomaterials, including gold nanoparticles, GO, CNTs, and black phosphorus, which exhibit strong absorption in the near-infrared (NIR) region. Upon NIR irradiation, these materials generate localized heat, enabling selective ablation of cancer cells while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues. , Similarly, membrane-based delivery systems for photosensitizing agents enable the controlled activation of photodynamic processes upon light exposure. These systems can be tailored through precise modulation of membrane properties, such as thickness, porosity, and stimuli responsiveness, to achieve spatiotemporally regulated drug release. This level of control enhances therapeutic efficacy while reducing off-target effects. ,

Moreover, the integration of membrane platforms with imaging functionalities supports the development of theranostic systems that combine diagnosis and therapy within a single framework. Such multifunctional platforms enable real-time monitoring of the treatment response and improved treatment precision. Additionally, coupling membrane-mediated PTT and PDT with immunotherapeutic strategies holds promise for inducing immunogenic cell death, thereby amplifying systemic antitumor immune responses and improving overall therapeutic outcomes.

### Environmental Health Applications of Membrane Technologies

#### Water Purification and Safe Drinking Water Supply

Membrane technologies represent a cornerstone of modern water purification strategies, playing a critical role in ensuring access to safe and clean drinking water. Despite significant advancements, waterborne diseases and chemical contamination remain major global health challenges, particularly in low-resource settings. Pressure-driven membrane processes, including MF, UF, NF, and RO, have been widely deployed to address these issues across diverse treatment scales. MF and UF membranes are highly effective for the removal of suspended solids, bacteria, protozoa, and certain viruses, thereby serving as essential barriers against microbial contamination. Furthermore, RO-based desalination has become a vital solution for water scarcity in arid and semiarid regions, particularly in the Middle East and North Africa, enabling the large-scale production of potable water from saline sources. Their relatively low energy requirements and operational simplicity make them particularly suitable for decentralized water treatment systems in rural and underserved communities, where they contribute to the prevention of waterborne diseases such as cholera and typhoid.

In contrast, NF and RO membranes are designed to remove dissolved contaminants, including salts, heavy metals, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and organic micropollutants. These technologies are increasingly being optimized to target emerging contaminants of concern, such as endocrine-disrupting chemicals, PFAS, and microplastics. , Surface-engineered NF membranes, incorporating enhanced hydrophilicity or functional nanomaterials, demonstrate improved selectivity for organic pollutants while mitigating membrane fouling. Recent advances also include the development of biomimetic membranes, such as aquaporin-based systems, which emulate natural water-transport channels to achieve exceptional permeability and selectivity. Collectively, these innovations highlight the growing role of membrane technologies in ensuring access to clean and safe drinking water. Their continued development is expected to enhance treatment efficiency, reduce operational costs, and strengthen global efforts toward sustainable water management and improved public health outcomes.

#### Wastewater Treatment and Reuse

Membrane technologies are integral to advanced wastewater treatment systems, contributing significantly to pollution control and sustainable water reuse. Conventional biological treatment processes often exhibit limited efficiency in removing trace organic contaminants, antibiotic residues, and persistent pathogens. In this context, MBRs, which integrate MF or UF membranes with activated sludge processes, have emerged as highly effective alternatives. These systems enhance the removal of suspended solids, bacteria, and viruses while offering operational advantages such as a reduced footprint and lower sludge production, making them particularly suitable for urban wastewater treatment applications.

For industrial wastewater, membrane processes such as RO, NF, and FO are widely employed to remove heavy metals, dyes, and toxic organic compounds. By effectively retaining hazardous substances, these technologies mitigate environmental contamination, reduce the risk of bioaccumulation, and protect public health. Water reuse, enabled by advanced membrane-based treatment trains, is increasingly recognized as a cornerstone of sustainable water management. A notable example is Singapore’s NEWater program, where reclaimed wastewater is treated using integrated UF and RO systems, followed by advanced oxidation processes to produce high-quality potable water. Such approaches not only alleviate pressure on freshwater resources but also minimize pollutant discharge into the environment.

Emerging membrane processes, particularly membrane distillation (MD), have gained attention for wastewater reclamation. MD is a thermally driven separation technique that employs hydrophobic membranes and is especially effective for treating hypersaline brines and complex industrial effluents that challenge conventional RO systems. When coupled with renewable energy sources, such as solar thermal energy, MD offers strong potential for decentralized and energy-efficient wastewater treatment in remote or resource-constrained settings. , Overall, the continued advancement of membrane technologies is transforming wastewater treatment from a disposal-oriented process to a resource recovery and water sustainability strategy. These innovations are expected to play an increasingly important role in achieving environmental protection, circular water management, and long-term public health resilience.

#### Air Pollution Control and Indoor Air Quality

Airborne pollutants, including particulate matter (PM), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and bioaerosols, pose significant risks to public health and are closely linked to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. Membrane-based air filtration technologies have emerged as effective solutions for improving both indoor and outdoor air quality. Recent advances in membrane engineering have led to the development of nanofiber-based filtration systems that outperform conventional high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. Unlike traditional HEPA filters, which rely on dense fibrous structures, nanofiber membranes provide enhanced filtration efficiency, lower airflow resistance, improved breathability, and the potential for antimicrobial functionality. , These attributes make them particularly suitable for applications in healthcare environments, residential buildings, and personal protective equipment. Electrospun polymeric membranes, frequently functionalized with nanoparticles such as silver (Ag), TiO2, and GO, exhibit superior performance in capturing ultrafine particles, including PM2.5, while simultaneously inactivating airborne pathogens. , This dual functionality is critical for mitigating the airborne transmission of infectious diseases, including tuberculosis and influenza.

In addition to indoor air purification, membrane technologies play a vital role in industrial air-pollution control. Gas separation membranes are increasingly employed to selectively remove CO2, SO2, and NO x from industrial emissions. , Facilitated transport membranes, which incorporate selective CO2 carriers, have demonstrated strong potential for postcombustion carbon capture, offering an energy-efficient pathway to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Furthermore, catalytic and photocatalytic membrane systems enable the degradation of hazardous VOCs into less harmful byproducts. Membranes integrated with photocatalytic materials can effectively decompose indoor air pollutants such as benzene and formaldehyde, thereby reducing exposure to carcinogenic compounds and improving the overall indoor environmental quality. , Thus, continuation in the development of membrane-based air purification technologies is expected to enhance air quality management, reduce pollution-related disease burdens, and support healthier living and working environments worldwide.

#### Soil Remediation and Groundwater Protection

Membrane-based technologies are increasingly recognized as effective solutions for soil remediation and groundwater protection, particularly in the treatment of contaminated soils, landfills, and polluted agricultural systems. Environmental contamination by heavy metals and toxic chemicals poses significant risks to ecosystems, food security, and human health because of their persistence, mobility, and bioaccumulation potential. In this context, membrane separation processes provide efficient and sustainable strategies for contaminant removal and environmental restoration.

Pressure-driven membrane processes, notably NF and RO, have demonstrated high removal efficiencies for hazardous pollutants such as lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury from soil leachates and contaminated water streams. By limiting the transport of these contaminants into groundwater systems, these technologies play a critical role in safeguarding drinking water resources and preventing their entry into agricultural food chains. In parallel, electro-membrane technologies, including electrodialysis (ED) and membrane capacitive deionization, are emerging as promising alternatives for targeted environmental remediation. These systems enable selective ion removal and are particularly effective at treating saline soils and extracting heavy metals from contaminated environments. Their selectivity makes them especially suitable for mitigating toxic ions such as cadmium and arsenic in agricultural regions, where prolonged soil contamination can adversely affect crop productivity and food safety.

The integration of membrane-based remediation approaches into environmental management frameworks offers significant potential to enhance sustainable agriculture, improve groundwater quality, and reduce long-term human exposure to environmental contaminants. Therefore, continued advancements in membrane materials and process optimization are expected to further strengthen the role of these technologies in global efforts toward pollution control, ecosystem restoration, and public health protection.

#### Pathogen Removal and Control of Emerging Biological Threats

In addition to chemical pollutants, microbial contaminants represent a significant public health challenge because of their role in the transmission of infectious diseases. Membrane filtration technologies serve as critical barriers against bacteria, viruses, and protozoan pathogens. In particular, UF and NF membranes demonstrate high efficiency in retaining viruses, including enteroviruses, rotaviruses, and noroviruses, which are often resistant to conventional chlorination processes. , Amid the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance, membrane-based systems offer a robust nonchemical approach to microbial control, reducing reliance on disinfectants that may generate harmful byproducts.

These technologies are especially valuable in high-risk environments, such as hospitals, laboratories, wastewater treatment facilities, and densely populated urban areas, where the potential for pathogen exposure and transmission is elevated. By enabling efficient physical removal of microorganisms, membrane systems support infection prevention, enhance biosafety, and contribute to improved public health outcomes. Collectively, continued advancements in membrane-based pathogen control are expected to strengthen global preparedness against emerging biological threats while supporting safer water and air systems and more resilient public health infrastructures.

#### Removal of Micropollutants and Emerging Contaminants

Modern water and wastewater systems are increasingly challenged by contaminants of emerging concern, including pharmaceuticals, personal care products, microplastics, PFAS, and engineered nanomaterials. Conventional treatment processes often exhibit limited efficiency against these trace pollutants, making membrane-based separation an important and increasingly versatile remediation strategy. By enabling selective retention and concentration of hazardous compounds, membrane technologies contribute to ecosystem protection and the reduction of chronic human exposure risks.

Among pressure-driven membranes, NF and RO have demonstrated strong performance in removing pharmaceuticals, such as antibiotics, as well as endocrine-disrupting compounds. Their effectiveness can be further improved through advanced composite designs that incorporate controlled pore structures and tailored surface charge characteristics, thereby enhancing selectivity toward specific micropollutants. FO also shows promise for trace contaminant removal when coupled with suitable draw solutions, although efficient draw solute recovery remains a key technical challenge.

Membrane technologies are likewise being adapted to address the escalating problem of microplastic pollution, which threatens aquatic ecosystems and human health. UF and NF membranes can effectively capture microplastics in drinking water and wastewater treatment applications, preventing their release into the environment. In this way, membrane-based systems serve not only as barriers against contamination but also as enabling technologies for more sustainable water management and public health protection. Consequently, ongoing advancements in membrane materials and process design are expected to further strengthen the role of membrane technologies in managing emerging contaminants and supporting sustainable water treatment practices.

#### Resource Recovery and Circular Economy Applications

Membrane technologies are increasingly central to circular economy strategies, as they enable the recovery of valuable resources from waste streams while reducing environmental burdens. In wastewater treatment, membranes are used to reclaim essential nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, which are indispensable for agriculture but can contribute to eutrophication when discharged into natural water bodies. Processes such as FO and ED can selectively concentrate these nutrients into reusable forms, including fertilizer-grade streams, thereby supporting sustainable nutrient recycling. Membranes also contribute to the water-energy nexus through technologies such as pressure-retarded osmosis (PRO) and reverse electrodialysis (RED), which exploit salinity gradients across membranes to generate renewable electricity. Beyond energy production, these approaches support more efficient water resource management and reduce dependence on fossil fuels, with indirect benefits for air quality and climate-related public health outcomes. ,

Resource recovery applications extend beyond water and nutrients to the extraction of valuable metals and industrial chemicals. Membrane systems have been applied in mining wastewater treatment for the recovery of rare-earth elements, as well as in industrial processes for solvent and acid reuse. For example, high-performance membrane systems have demonstrated over 99.7% ion rejection and 33.9% water recovery in gold-mining effluents, underscoring their potential for both environmental protection and process efficiency. , Hence, the integration of membrane technologies into resource recovery frameworks represents a key pathway toward a circular economy, in which waste generation is minimized and materials are continuously reused. By enabling nutrient recycling, renewable energy generation, and the recovery of valuable compounds, these systems provide combined environmental, economic, and public health benefits that support long-term sustainability.

#### Smart Membranes for Environmental Monitoring

Smart and responsive membranes represent an emerging frontier in environmental health monitoring. Unlike conventional membranes, smart membranes integrate sensing, detection, and adaptive response capabilities, enabling the real-time identification and management of environmental contaminants. By the incorporation of functional nanomaterials, biosensors, and molecular recognition elements within the membrane matrix, these systems can detect specific pollutants and respond through selective filtration, changes in permeability, or built-in signaling mechanisms. ,

A major advantage of these systems is their ability to respond dynamically to environmental stimuli. For example, pH-responsive membranes can alter their permeability under acidic or alkaline conditions, making them useful for detecting chemical leaks and industrial discharges. Likewise, fluorescent membranes embedded with quantum dots, molecular probes, or other sensing elements can generate visible or optical signals in the presence of hazardous substances, such as heavy metals and toxic chemicals. , These features support rapid contaminant detection and timely intervention before pollutants reach levels that threaten ecosystems or human health.

The integration of smart membranes with the IoT, AI, and digital health platforms could further expand their utility. Such convergence will enable continuous, decentralized monitoring of air and water quality, generating real-time data for environmental surveillance, risk assessment, and early warning systems. These connected monitoring networks will strengthen evidence-based decision-making by regulators, industries, and communities, while also improving environmental resilience and public health protection. As research advances, smart membranes are expected to play an increasingly important role in the next generation of environmental management. Their combined capabilities for contaminant detection, adaptive filtration, and digital connectivity position them as powerful tools for proactive pollution control and sustainable monitoring.

## Conclusion

This review highlights the growing convergence of membrane science, nanotechnology, and biomimetic engineering as a powerful platform for advancing both cancer therapy and environmental health. In cancer applications, biomimetic nanoparticles, exosome-derived platforms, and ultrathin biosensors promise enhanced precision in therapy and diagnostics, though challenges in scalability and regulatory compliance must be overcome. In environmental health, membranes are redefining water purification, wastewater reuse, and pollution control, with graphene-based composites, NF, and smart sensors offering new levels of efficiency and adaptability. Cross-cutting accelerators, particularly AI, circular economy frameworks, and hybrid integration, are positioning membranes as multifunctional systems that go beyond separation to include sensing, remediation, and energy generation. Besides, barriers remain in cost, fouling resistance, lifecycle impacts, and standardization. Addressing these requires coordinated efforts among scientists, engineers, industry, and regulators. Key priorities for advancing membrane innovation include establishing standardized characterization protocols, conducting pilot-scale demonstrations, strengthening collaboration between regulatory bodies and academia, integrating sensing and separation functionalities seamlessly, and leveraging AI-driven approaches for material design and optimization. By combining the fields of human health and environmental sustainability, next-generation membrane systems have the potential not only to improve existing technologies but also to fundamentally change society’s approaches to addressing some of its most pressing challenges, including cancer treatment, access to clean water, pollution control, and sustainable resource management. Thus, the future of membrane science lies not in isolated technological advances but in the creation of integrated, intelligent, and sustainable systems capable of simultaneously protecting biomedical health and environmental health.
