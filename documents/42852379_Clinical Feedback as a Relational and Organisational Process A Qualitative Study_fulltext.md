# Clinical Feedback as a Relational and Organisational Process: A Qualitative Study of Students’ Experiences in Operating Room Environment

- **Authors:** Farzad Abaszadeh, Ali Asghar Hayat, Mobin Mottahedi, Soolmaz Zare
- **Journal:** Advances in Medical Education and Practice
- **Year:** 2026
- **PMID:** 42852379
- **PMCID:** PMC13648168
- **DOI:** 10.2147/AMEP.S647862
- **Keywords:** clinical feedback; workplace learning; operating room learning environment; psychological safety; feedback practices

## Abstract

Background: Clinical feedback is central to workplace learning in operating room education. While clinical feedback has been conceptualised as relational and context-dependent, less is known about how Operating Room Technology students perceive and experience it within the operating room learning environment.

Methods: A qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with 17 Operating Room Technology students recruited through maximum variation purposive sampling from Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Iran. Data were collected between November 2025 and March 2026 and analysed using Graneheim and Lundman’s inductive content analysis. Trustworthiness was addressed using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria.

Results: Five themes comprising 13 subcategories were identified: structural and executive framework of clinical feedback; perceived consequences of clinical feedback quality; harmful feedback practices and barriers; interaction and professionalism in feedback; and individual and professional competencies of instructors in feedback. Students described clinical feedback as most valuable when it was structured, timely, respectful, and dialogic, and perceived it as contributing to confidence, engagement, and professional identity. Delayed, vague, or humiliating clinical feedback was perceived to undermine confidence and psychological safety. Divergent accounts highlighted tensions between immediate intra-operative clinical feedback and workflow demands, as well as circumstances in which public safety-critical corrections were perceived as necessary.

Conclusions: The findings suggest that clinical feedback in Operating Room Technology education is a multidimensional process perceived to be shaped by structural, relational, organisational, and educator-related factors. Rather than proposing a new conceptualisation, this study provides a context-specific understanding of how established clinical feedback principles are perceived and negotiated within the operating room learning environment. Strengthening educator capabilities, embedding structured clinical feedback approaches, and fostering psychologically safe learning environments may strengthen clinical education in Operating Room Technology programmes.

## Introduction

Feedback is widely defined as information provided to learners about their performance, intended to guide future learning and improve competence.1 Within health professions education, clinical feedback refers specifically to performance-related information provided to learners during clinical practice, where it serves to bridge the gap between current and expected performance in authentic patient-care settings.2 Clinical feedback is therefore not merely a transmission of information; it is an integral component of workplace learning that supports the development of clinical competence, reflective practice, and professional identity.3,4

Contemporary conceptualisations of clinical feedback have moved beyond unidirectional transmission models toward sociocultural, relational, and dialogic perspectives.5,6 From this perspective, clinical feedback is shaped by interactions among learners, educators, workplace culture, and contextual conditions, while its educational value depends in part on how learners perceive, interpret, and respond to the feedback they receive.7–9 Considerable literature has established that clinical feedback is relational and context-dependent and that learner engagement is an important dimension of the clinical feedback process.10–13 Learner-related processes, including the ability to seek clinical feedback,14 evaluate its credibility,15 regulate emotional responses,16 and determine how to respond,17 have also received increasing attention, although these processes have not been explored equally across clinical learning contexts.

These dimensions of clinical feedback may be particularly important within the operating room environment. The operating room is a distinctive clinical learning environment characterised by high patient-safety demands, rapid decision-making, time pressure, complex technical procedures, and hierarchical interprofessional relationships.18–20 Learning in this environment occurs within a highly dynamic workflow in which educational activities must coexist with the immediate priorities of patient care and surgical efficiency.21 Operating Room Technology students are expected to develop technical skills, communication abilities, situational awareness, and teamwork competencies while adapting to the norms and demands of surgical teams.22 Hierarchical relationships, concerns about making mistakes in front of others, and the stressful nature of surgical environments may influence how students perceive and respond to clinical feedback. Studies conducted in surgical and anaesthesia training environments have similarly highlighted the importance of contextual and relational factors in shaping clinical feedback experiences.23–25

Despite this growing body of literature, important gaps remain. Much of the existing research on clinical feedback has focused on feedback delivery practices, perceived effectiveness, or educators’ perspectives within broader clinical education settings.26–29 Less is known about how Operating Room Technology students experience and interpret clinical feedback within the specific context of operating room practice. In particular, there is limited qualitative evidence regarding how time pressure, interprofessional hierarchy, workflow demands, patient-safety considerations, and the characteristics of feedback providers shape students’ experiences of clinical feedback. It is also unclear how established clinical feedback principles, such as timeliness, specificity, respect, and dialogue, are perceived and negotiated under the practical constraints of surgical care. Given these contextual characteristics, findings from other clinical settings may not fully capture the experiences of Operating Room Technology students in the operating room.

Understanding students’ perceptions and experiences is important because learners may interpret and respond to the same clinical feedback differently depending on the relational, emotional, and contextual conditions in which it is delivered.30 The meaning students assign to clinical feedback, together with their engagement with the clinical feedback process, may influence its perceived educational value. These contextual, relational, and experiential dimensions are difficult to capture through quantitative approaches alone. An in-depth qualitative approach is therefore appropriate for examining how students describe, interpret, and assign meaning to clinical feedback based on their experiences of clinical practice.

Therefore, this qualitative study was conducted to explore Operating Room Technology students’ perceptions and experiences of clinical feedback within the operating room learning environment. Using an inductive content analysis approach, the study sought to examine how students describe the structural, relational, organisational, and individual dimensions of clinical feedback, as well as its perceived consequences for learning and motivation. Rather than proposing a new conceptualisation of clinical feedback, this study aimed to provide a context-specific understanding of how established clinical feedback principles, including timeliness, specificity, respect, dialogue, and psychological safety, are perceived, negotiated, and sometimes constrained within the operating room environment. By examining these experiences within the distinctive conditions of surgical practice, the study aims to contribute to a more context-sensitive understanding of clinical feedback in Operating Room Technology education.

## Methods

### Study Design

This qualitative study was conducted using an inductive content analysis approach based on the methodological framework proposed by Graneheim and Lundman (2004).31 This approach was chosen to explore Operating Room Technology students’ perceptions and experiences of clinical feedback in the operating room environment and to allow subcategories and themes to emerge from the data. The reporting of this study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines32 to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of the research process and findings.

It is important to note that although the interview guide was informed by existing literature and included probes related to clinical feedback timing, respect, professionalism, instructor competence, and feedback practices, the analysis was inductive in the sense that codes and subcategories were derived from participants’ accounts rather than from a pre-existing coding framework. We therefore describe the analysis as inductive within a structured, literature-informed interview framework. Domains introduced through the interview guide are distinguished from issues that emerged outside the prompted framework.

During analysis, particular attention was paid to contradictory accounts, negative cases, and circumstances in which otherwise desirable clinical feedback practices became impractical. The analysis also attended to learner-side processes such as seeking clinical feedback, evaluating its credibility, regulating emotional responses, and deciding whether and how to act on it. A total of 312 initial codes were generated and subsequently grouped into 13 subcategories and five themes.

### Research Team and Reflexivity

The interviews were conducted by FA, a male PhD candidate in Medical Education at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences at the time of the study. He held an MSc in Operating Room Technology and had prior clinical and educational experience in the operating room setting. He had also completed formal training in qualitative research methods and semi-structured interviewing. The research team also included AAH, a specialist in medical education with expertise in qualitative methodology; MM, a researcher in Operating Room Technology; and SZ, a senior researcher in clinical education who supervised the study. None of the researchers had a prior supervisory, instructor–student, or evaluative relationship with the participants. FA was not involved in the academic assessment or clinical placement of any participant. Participants were explicitly informed that participation was voluntary, that refusal would have no effect on their academic assessment or clinical placement, and that they could withdraw at any stage without consequences.

Given FA’s professional background in Operating Room Technology and medical education, reflexive practices were systematically employed throughout the study. FA maintained a reflective journal to document assumptions, expectations, and emotional responses during data collection and analysis. Regular research team meetings were held to discuss coding decisions, potential biases, and alternative interpretations. The team also discussed how FA’s familiarity with the operating room environment might shape questioning, interpretation, and participants’ responses, and took steps to encourage participants to describe their experiences in their own words.

### Setting and Participants

The study was conducted at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Shiraz, Iran. Participants were undergraduate students in the Operating Room Technology programme, enrolled in the fifth to eighth academic semesters, who had completed at least one full clinical rotation in the operating room setting. Eligible students were required to have prior experience receiving clinical feedback from clinical instructors during their clinical training. In this programme, tasks such as suturing and tissue handling are performed under direct supervision or as part of assisted or observed practice, depending on the stage of training.

Students were excluded if they had insufficient experience with receiving clinical feedback, were unwilling to participate, declined audio recording of the interview, withdrew from the study at any stage, or provided incomplete responses.

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling with maximum variation in terms of gender, academic semester, number of completed operating room rotations, and range of surgical specialties experienced, to obtain diverse perspectives regarding clinical feedback experiences. Potential participants were approached face-to-face by the researcher during non-clinical hours or after clinical training sessions.

During recruitment, 24 eligible students were approached. Of these, 19 initially agreed to participate, and 17 completed the study. Two students withdrew before the interview due to time constraints. No eligible student who agreed to participate was excluded.

A total of 17 students participated in the study. Data collection was conducted between November 2025 and March 2026. Data saturation was reached after the 15th interview, as no new codes or subcategories emerged from the data. Two additional interviews were subsequently conducted to confirm saturation, resulting in a final sample of 17 participants.

### Data Collection

Data were collected through individual semi-structured face-to-face interviews using an interview guide developed based on the study objectives and relevant literature while maintaining sufficient flexibility to allow participants to freely express issues important to their experiences (Supplementary File). The interview guide included open-ended questions exploring students’ experiences of receiving clinical feedback in the operating room learning environment, situations influencing their acceptance of clinical feedback, and instructor behaviours during clinical feedback. The guide was piloted with two eligible students who were not included in the final sample, and minor wording adjustments were made.

Examples of interview questions included:Can you describe your experiences of receiving clinical feedback in the operating room environment?Can you describe situations in which clinical feedback was easier or harder for you to accept?What kinds of clinical feedback are most helpful for you? Why?What instructor behaviours are important to you when receiving clinical feedback?

Interviews were conducted in Persian in a quiet and private room at the School of Nursing and Midwifery according to participants’ availability and preferences. Each interview lasted approximately 35–55 minutes. No individuals other than the participant and researcher were present during the interviews.

With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded. Field notes were documented immediately after each interview to capture contextual observations, non-verbal cues, and the researcher’s initial reflections. Field notes were used during analysis to contextualise codes and to support reflexive interpretation but were not treated as primary data.

### Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection using the inductive content analysis approach described by Graneheim and Lundman.31 All interviews were transcribed verbatim in Persian immediately after completion and read several times to achieve immersion in the data.

Meaning units related to participants’ perceptions and experiences of clinical feedback were identified, condensed, and labelled with initial codes. Both manifest and latent content were analysed. Manifest content was used to identify explicit descriptions in participants’ accounts, while latent content was interpreted to uncover underlying meanings across subcategories and themes. Codes with conceptual similarities were grouped into subcategories through constant comparison and abstraction. Finally, subcategories were grouped into overarching themes representing the underlying meanings within the data. No separate category level was used; themes represent the highest level of abstraction.

Initial coding was performed by FA. A second researcher (AAH) independently coded 30% of the transcripts. The coding framework was reviewed by the research team, including MM and SZ. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Alternative interpretations were documented and discussed. The diverse disciplinary backgrounds of the research team (medical education, qualitative methodology, operating room technology, and clinical education) were considered throughout the analysis to balance interpretations and reduce potential biases. MAXQDA software (version 2022) was used for data organisation and management.

### Translation Procedures

Interviews were conducted in Persian. All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Persian. Transcripts were then translated into English by a bilingual member of the research team. Translation accuracy was independently checked by a second bilingual researcher. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Coding and analysis were performed on the English transcripts, with periodic checking against the original Persian transcripts to preserve meaning. Quotations were lightly edited for readability without changing their meaning. Original Persian excerpts are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

### Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was established based on the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba,33 including credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with participants and data, continuous comparison of codes and subcategories, peer debriefing, and member checking, in which a summary of the findings was shared with five participants who confirmed that the interpretations reflected their experiences. Dependability and confirmability were supported by an audit trail of analytical decisions, coding processes, and reflective notes. Transferability was strengthened by providing thick descriptions of the study setting, participants, and research process.

#### Theme 1: Structural and Executive Framework of Feedback

This theme addresses the organisational and procedural dimensions of clinical feedback in the operating room as perceived by Operating Room Technology students.

##### Subcategory 1-1: Structural Principles of Feedback

Students emphasised the importance of having a systematic, consistent, and transparent approach to feedback delivery in the operating room. They reported that the lack of a clear and stable structure often led to confusion and reduced the educational value of the feedback they received.There was no clear system for feedback. Sometimes the instructor would tell you something in the middle of the surgery, sometimes after, and sometimes not at all. This inconsistency made me confused about what I was doing right or wrong. We need a structured protocol for feedback, just like we have protocols for everything else in the operating room. (P12, Semester 6)In some rotations, the instructor had a checklist and would sit with us at the end of each day to go through it. That was excellent because it was systematic and I knew what to expect. But most of the time, feedback was random and accidental. (P7, Semester 5)

##### Subcategory 1-2: Timing of Feedback

Timing was considered one of the most influential factors in the effectiveness of feedback. Students generally believed that immediate and timely feedback, adjusted to the complexity of the task, enhanced learning, whereas delayed feedback reduced its usefulness. However, as described later, some participants also described circumstances in which immediate intra-operative feedback created tension with concentration or workflow. Immediate feedback right after I finished suturing was the most useful. I could still remember exactly what I had done. But when they waited until the next day or even the end of the week, I couldn’t connect the feedback to my performance anymore. The delay made it almost useless. (P4, Semester 5)During a difficult case, the surgeon corrected my hand position right at that moment. That instant feedback helped me fix it immediately. But when they waited until the patient was out of the OR, I had already forgotten the details. (P15, Semester 7)

##### Subcategory 1-3: Location and Context of Feedback

Students highlighted that the physical environment and psychological context greatly affect how feedback is received. They described a safe, supportive, and private setting as ideal, while feedback given in stressful or public situations within the operating room was often perceived as ineffective or harmful. Nevertheless, a few participants acknowledged that brief safety-critical corrections in front of the team were sometimes necessary. I hated it when they gave me feedback in front of the whole team, especially in the middle of surgery. Everyone was looking at me and I felt so small. The best feedback was when the instructor took me aside to the scrub room or a quiet corner after the procedure. (P9, Semester 6)The operating room is already stressful. When they criticize you loudly in that environment, you become so anxious that you cannot even learn. A private, calm place makes a huge difference. (P3, Semester 8)

##### Subcategory 1-4: Methods of Providing Feedback

Participants valued various methods of feedback delivery, including specific, example-based, step-by-step, and individualised approaches. Both verbal and non-verbal feedback were mentioned as effective when provided clearly and supportively. The most helpful feedback was specific and example based. Instead of saying ‘you are slow’, she said ‘when you are looking for the artery forceps, keep your eyes on the field instead of the back table; this will save you 20 seconds’. That kind of concrete example really helped me. (P11, Semester 5)I liked when they showed me first, then let me try, and then gave step by step feedback. Also, nonverbal feedback, like nodding when I did something correctly, was encouraging without interrupting the flow of surgery. (P8, Semester 6)

Participants also valued feedback that was realistic and directly applicable to the demands of the operating room. They described realistic feedback as that which reflected the actual conditions of surgical practice rather than idealised or textbook scenarios.When the instructor gave me feedback based on what actually happens in a real surgery, it was much more useful. Sometimes textbook answers do not work in the OR because everything is different. Realistic feedback helped me prepare for the real challenges. (P2, Semester 5)

##### Subcategory 1-5: Stages of Providing Feedback

Students described feedback as most beneficial when delivered across different stages of the surgical process (before, during, and after procedures) as well as in structured sessions such as morning reports. Clear identification of performance gaps and follow-up were seen as important features.Feedback before surgery was very useful. The instructor would ask me about the steps and correct my plan. During surgery, they gave real-time correction, and after surgery we had a short debriefing. When all three stages were present, I learned much more. (P14, Semester 7)Morning report was a good time for general feedback, but the most valuable one was the post-operative discussion when the case was still fresh. We could talk about what went well and what needed improvement while everything was clear in our minds. (P2, Semester 5)

#### Theme 2: Perceived Consequences of Feedback Quality on Learning and Motivation

This theme illustrates how the quality of clinical feedback was perceived by Operating Room Technology students to affect their learning, clinical performance, and motivation. It is important to emphasise that these are participants’ perceptions and retrospective accounts, not objectively measured effects.

##### Subcategory 2-1: Perceived Consequences of Effective Feedback

Students described effective clinical feedback as a powerful facilitator of learning. They reported that constructive and timely clinical feedback enhanced their clinical skills, boosted self-confidence, increased motivation, and they perceived it as contributing to their professional identity. When I received specific and supportive feedback, my confidence increased a lot. For example, after one instructor explained exactly how to handle tissue gently, I performed much better in the next cases. It made me eager to learn more. (P5, Semester 5)Good feedback motivated me to participate actively in surgeries. I felt valued and saw real improvement in my skills. It even made me read more about the procedures at night because I wanted to do better the next day. (P13, Semester 7)

In addition to enhanced confidence and motivation, some participants described effective clinical feedback as helping them manage the stress of the operating room environment and feel more clinically ready. They reported that constructive clinical feedback reduced their anxiety about making mistakes and increased their sense of preparedness for independent practice.When the feedback was supportive and clear, I felt less stressed in the OR. I knew what I was doing and I wasn’t afraid of making mistakes. It also made me feel more ready for when I have to work on my own. (P3, Semester 8)Good feedback prepared me for real clinical situations. After a few sessions of proper feedback, I felt I could handle similar cases with more confidence. It was like a bridge between learning and real practice. (P15, Semester 7)

##### Subcategory 2-2: Perceived Consequences of Ineffective Feedback

Ineffective feedback was reported to have several negative effects on students. Participants mentioned decreased motivation, reduced self-confidence, increased anxiety, reinforcement of mistakes, and sometimes avoidance of active participation in clinical activities.After receiving vague and harsh feedback, I felt so demotivated that I didn’t want to do anything in the operating room. I started to doubt my abilities and became more passive during the following days. (P6, Semester 8)Bad feedback made me anxious and confused. Instead of learning, I became worried about making mistakes again. It created a vicious cycle where I was afraid to try new things. (P10, Semester 6)

#### Theme 3: Harmful Feedback Practices and Barriers

This theme explores the negative aspects, harmful experiences, and existing barriers that students perceived in the clinical feedback process within the operating room environment.

##### Subcategory 3-1: Harmful Feedback Delivery

Students experienced significant emotional and educational harm when feedback was delivered in a humiliating, controlling, or non-constructive manner. Such feedback often led to feelings of worthlessness, fear, and emotional distress.One instructor shouted at me in front of everyone and said I was ‘clumsy and useless’. I felt humiliated and cried after the surgery. For several days, I couldn’t concentrate on learning. (P16, Semester 6)When feedback was given with anger and without any solution, it destroyed my motivation. Instead of helping me improve, it made me feel incompetent and scared of asking questions. (P1, Semester 5)

##### Subcategory 3-2: Structural and Executive Barriers

Students identified several structural and environmental barriers that limited the quality of clinical feedback. These included time constraints, high workload, lack of supervision, and insufficient instructor training.In busy operating rooms, there was simply no time for proper feedback. The instructors were always rushing to the next case, so we rarely received any constructive comments. (P17, Semester 7)Some instructors didn’t know how to give feedback properly. They had excellent surgical skills but couldn’t explain things clearly or supportively. I think they need training on how to teach and give feedback. (P8, Semester 6)

Participants also described how disruptive interventions by other staff members, such as surgeons, anaesthesia personnel, or circulating nurses, interrupted or terminated feedback sessions. These interruptions were often related to workflow demands and patient safety priorities, but they nonetheless reduced the quality and continuity of feedback.Sometimes the instructor started to give me feedback after the surgery, but then another surgeon called him for the next case and he just left. The feedback was never finished. This happened many times. (P10, Semester 6)

#### Theme 4: Interaction and Professionalism in Feedback

This theme describes the significance of interactive processes and professional conduct in the delivery of clinical feedback from the perspective of Operating Room Technology students.

##### Subcategory 4-1: Interaction and Active Participation

Students placed high value on feedback that involved two-way interaction, active participation, and shared responsibility. They found interactive approaches, such as dialogue, questioning, and peer feedback, more engaging and effective for their learning.The best feedback happened when the instructor asked for my opinion and we discussed the case together. It wasn’t just one-way criticism; it was a real conversation that helped me think critically. (P11, Semester 5)I learned much more when the instructor encouraged me to explain my actions and then gave feedback based on my own reasoning. This interactive style made me feel involved and responsible for my learning. (P14, Semester 7)

##### Subcategory 4-2: Professional Ethics in Feedback

Students emphasised the importance of ethical principles such as respect, dignity, fairness, and maintaining a balance between honesty and support. They strongly disliked humiliating or labelling behaviours and viewed feedback as an ethical educational responsibility.Even if I made a mistake, the instructor who corrected me with respect helped me improve without feeling ashamed. Respect is the most important thing when giving feedback. (P3, Semester 8)Some instructors labeled students as ‘weak’ or ‘slow’ in front of others. This kind of behavior is unethical and destroys the student’s motivation. Feedback should never humiliate the learner. (P9, Semester 6)

#### Theme 5: Individual and Professional Competencies of Instructors in Feedback

This theme focuses on the competencies and personal attributes of clinical instructors and surgeons that students considered essential for effective feedback provision.

##### Subcategory 5-1: Scientific and Technical Competence

Students believed that instructors’ scientific knowledge, clinical expertise, and ability to teach skills effectively were fundamental to the credibility and usefulness of the feedback they provided.I trusted the feedback more when it came from an instructor who was really skilled and up-to-date. When they explained the scientific reason behind a technique, I could understand and apply it better. (P15, Semester 7)Some instructors had limited experience themselves. Their feedback was not helpful because they couldn’t demonstrate or explain procedures properly. Clinical competence is very important for a good mentor. (P4, Semester 5)

##### Subcategory 5-2: Personal Characteristics of Instructors

Personal traits such as empathy, patience, calmness, positive attitude, and appropriate authority without creating fear were highly valued by students. These characteristics significantly influenced their willingness to accept and act upon clinical feedback.The instructor who was patient and calm helped me a lot. Even when I made mistakes, she didn’t get angry. Her empathy made me feel safe to ask questions and learn from my errors. (P7, Semester 5)A good instructor combines authority with kindness. When they were supportive and had a sense of humour at the right time, I felt more comfortable and motivated to receive their feedback. (P12, Semester 6)

Participants also emphasised that instructors who paid attention to student motivation were more effective in delivering feedback. They described such instructors as those who recognised when a student was discouraged and adjusted their approach accordingly.The best instructor was the one who noticed when I was losing motivation and tried to encourage me instead of just criticising. She paid attention to how I was feeling, not just what I was doing wrong. (P16, Semester 6)

### Tensions, Divergent Cases, and Negative Cases

Although most participants described immediate feedback as desirable, several accounts revealed tensions between immediate intra-operative correction and other priorities such as concentration, workflow, and patient safety. For example, one participant noted:Sometimes when the surgeon stopped to correct me in the middle of a critical step, I lost my focus completely. I knew he meant well, but the timing was wrong. Later, when the patient was stable, it would have been much better. (P6, Semester 8)

Similarly, while public feedback was predominantly described as harmful, a few participants acknowledged that brief safety-critical corrections in front of the team were sometimes unavoidable and even necessary:When it was about patient safety, the surgeon had to correct me immediately, even if others were watching. I didn’t like it, but I understood it was necessary at that moment.(P13, Semester 7)

These divergent accounts suggest that the acceptability and perceived effectiveness of clinical feedback in the operating room depended on task urgency, patient safety considerations, workflow demands, and learner readiness. The findings therefore should not be read as a uniform catalogue of desirable and undesirable feedback practices, but as an account of how feedback operates within a complex, hierarchical, and time-sensitive workplace.

### Learner Processes and Feedback Literacy

Although the interview guide focused primarily on feedback delivery and instructor behaviours, some participants described learner-side processes such as seeking feedback, evaluating its credibility, regulating emotional responses, and deciding whether and how to act on it. These accounts were less prominent than descriptions of educator behaviour and organisational conditions. For example:I learned to choose who to ask for feedback. Some instructors were more approachable and their feedback was more useful. With others, I just stayed quiet. (P5, Semester 5)After a while, I could tell which feedback was worth acting on and which was just the instructor’s mood. It took me time to learn that. (P14, Semester 7)

These findings suggest that learner processes related to feedback literacy were present in participants’ accounts but were not as analytically developed as the organisational and relational dimensions of feedback. This study therefore focuses primarily on the relational and organisational aspects of clinical feedback in the operating room, while acknowledging that feedback literacy remains an important area for further investigation.

### Scope of Clinical Tasks

In the Operating Room Technology programme, tasks such as suturing and tissue handling are performed under direct supervision or as part of assisted or observed practice, depending on the stage of training. The quotations presented in this section refer to students’ experiences during supervised clinical practice and should be interpreted within this educational scope.

## Discussion

### Summary of Main Findings

This study explored how Operating Room Technology students perceive and experience clinical feedback within the operating room learning environment. The findings suggest that clinical feedback in this context is perceived as a multidimensional process shaped by structural, relational, organisational, and educator-related factors. Students described clinical feedback as most valuable when it was timely, structured, respectful, and dialogic, and when it was provided by clinically credible educators within supportive relationships. Conversely, inconsistent, delayed, vague, or humiliating clinical feedback was perceived to undermine confidence and students’ sense of psychological safety, and to discourage participation.

These findings represent participants’ perceptions and retrospective accounts rather than objectively measured effects. Several domains discussed here, such as timing, respect, and instructor competence, were introduced through the interview guide; therefore, the findings elaborate on rather than independently identify these domains. The theoretical perspectives used in this Discussion, including dialogic feedback and psychological safety, are applied as interpretive lenses rather than as frameworks tested or generated by the study. The contribution of this study lies in providing a context-specific understanding of how established clinical feedback principles are perceived, negotiated, and sometimes constrained within the distinctive conditions of the operating room learning environment.

### Structural, Relational, and Dialogic Dimensions of Clinical Feedback

Students perceived clinical feedback as most valuable when it followed a structured and predictable process. Inconsistent timing and unclear expectations were described as reducing its educational value. This is consistent with evidence from a systematic review indicating that feedback in health professions education is most valuable when delivered immediately by instructors and integrated into structured learning activities.34 Similarly, a study of clinical feedback in an operating room setting in Bhutan found that briefing and debriefing culture was almost absent, and that a structured five-step feedback tool significantly improved the quality of feedback in the operating room.35 These findings suggest that, in some clinical rotations, clinical feedback may be left to chance rather than being intentionally integrated into the learning experience.

Timing emerged as particularly influential: students preferred clinical feedback delivered during or immediately after clinical activities. This aligns with evidence that immediate verbal feedback is more effective than delayed feedback in improving learning gains.36 However, some participants also described circumstances in which immediate intra-operative feedback created tension with concentration or workflow, suggesting that its perceived value depended on task urgency, learner readiness, and clinical priorities rather than being uniformly desirable. These tensions help illustrate why some otherwise desirable clinical feedback practices became difficult to implement in the operating room learning environment. Comparisons with studies in surgical and acute-care training environments highlight that the enactment of dialogic feedback in authentic clinical settings remains challenging,37 and the present findings suggest that these challenges are particularly relevant within the operating room learning environment, where educational activities must coexist with immediate patient-care priorities.

Students’ accounts indicated that the learning environment was important to how they perceived clinical feedback. Private, respectful discussions were described as considerably more beneficial than public criticism. Respect emerged as a fundamental condition: corrective clinical feedback remained acceptable when delivered with professionalism and dignity, whereas disrespectful communication diminished its educational value. These accounts are consistent with evidence that feedback is experienced as a socially negotiated event rather than a purely pedagogical activity, and that public critique may be interpreted as social exposure rather than academic support.38 Similarly, dialogic feedback has been described as an interactive learning conversation in which learners actively construct meaning rather than passively receive information.39 The present findings suggest that the dialogic and relational dimensions of clinical feedback may be particularly significant in the operating room learning environment, where operative workflow constraints, interprofessional hierarchy, time pressure, and patient-safety priorities can shape how clinical feedback is delivered and received.40

Learner processes related to feedback literacy, including seeking clinical feedback, evaluating its credibility, and regulating emotional responses, were present in participants’ accounts but were less analytically developed than the organisational and relational dimensions. This is consistent with evidence that students’ roles in feedback processes tend to be overlooked, with most interventions focusing on how educators deliver feedback rather than on how learners seek, process, and use it.41 This study therefore focuses primarily on the relational and organisational aspects, while acknowledging that feedback literacy remains an important area for further investigation.

### Perceived Consequences and Harmful Practices

Participants described clinical feedback as contributing to their motivation, confidence, and perceptions of professional identity. Constructive clinical feedback was described as encouraging engagement and strengthening confidence, whereas vague, delayed, or overly critical clinical feedback was perceived to generate anxiety and discourage learning. These accounts are consistent with evidence that feedback can evoke emotional responses such as embarrassment and anxiousness, and that these responses may negatively influence subsequent performance.42 Similarly, a qualitative study of midwifery students in a high-stress clinical environment found that constructive feedback was perceived as enhancing learning, whereas destructive feedback diminished the quality of learning.43

Some participants also described effective clinical feedback as helping them manage the stress of the operating room and feel more clinically ready. This is consistent with evidence that training in emotional intelligence, including emotional self-awareness and self-management, may help learners manage emotional responses to feedback, particularly when accompanied by face-to-face coachin.44 However, it is important to reiterate that these are participants’ perceptions and retrospective accounts, not demonstrated effects on professional identity or clinical performance.

Public criticism, humiliating interactions, disrespectful communication, and organisational constraints were perceived to reduce students’ willingness to participate and learn from mistakes. Being reprimanded in front of others generated embarrassment and fear rather than learning. This is consistent with evidence that humiliation is associated with reduced well-being through its effects on psychological safety, and that organisational support moderates this relationship.45 A qualitative study of nursing students similarly found that infrequent, irregular, and biased feedback, combined with perceived barriers, hindered students’ learning in clinical settings.46 Organisational conditions such as heavy workloads, limited supervision, and time-sensitive practice were perceived to reduce opportunities for meaningful educational conversations. This aligns with evidence that restrictive organisational and hierarchical structures, together with clinical environmental pressures, constitute major barriers to communication and learning in the operating room.47 Taken together, these findings suggest that participants viewed psychological safety as an important condition for engaging with clinical feedback in the operating room learning environment.

While most participants described immediate clinical feedback as desirable, some accounts revealed tensions with other priorities. A notable divergent account was that some students described public safety-critical corrections as acceptable when they recognised the clinical necessity. These divergent accounts suggest that the acceptability of clinical feedback depends on task urgency, patient-safety considerations, and learner readiness, rather than following a uniform pattern.

### Instructor Competencies and Implications for Operating Room Education

Participants emphasised that their perceptions of clinical feedback were influenced not only by what was communicated but also by who delivered it. Clinical credibility was viewed as an essential foundation. This is consistent with evidence that clinical instructors’ individual characteristics, teaching approaches, and ability to create a safe learning environment are among the qualities valued by students.48,49 However, participants did not necessarily perceive clinical expertise alone as sufficient, as technically proficient instructors were not always able to explain performance gaps or adapt clinical feedback to learners’ needs. This reinforces the distinction between clinical expertise and teaching expertise, and suggests that faculty development in Operating Room Technology education may need to extend beyond strengthening clinical skills.

These findings have implications at three levels. At the educator level, faculty development may need to extend beyond clinical expertise to include pedagogical knowledge, learner-centred communication, and strategies for fostering psychologically safe learning environments. This is consistent with evidence that educators play a central role in establishing psychological safety and that unprofessional behaviours resulting in learner humiliation can dampen productive discourse.40 At the curricular level, Operating Room Technology education programmes could consider embedding structured clinical feedback processes, including reflective debriefing, throughout clinical placements. Evidence from the operating room suggests that structured feedback tools can facilitate experiential learning and improve feedback quality when a culture of structured feedback is in place.35 At the organisational level, effective clinical feedback may require cultures that support respectful communication and consistent supervision. This aligns with evidence that co-creation approaches involving faculty, clinical teachers, and students can produce contextually relevant feedback tools that support trustful relationships and structured feedback interactions.50

## Strengths and Limitations

This study provides in-depth insights into how Operating Room Technology students perceive clinical feedback within the operating room learning environment. The qualitative design enabled exploration of relational, contextual, and organisational dimensions. Maximum variation sampling supported the inclusion of diverse perspectives across gender, academic semester, clinical rotations, and surgical exposure, while established trustworthiness strategies supported the credibility of the findings.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the study was conducted within a single institutional and national context; therefore, clinical feedback experiences may differ across healthcare and educational systems with different cultural and organisational characteristics. Second, participants’ accounts were retrospective and may have been influenced by recall bias. Third, the interviewer had a professional background in Operating Room Technology and prior clinical and educational experience in this setting, which may have shaped questioning and interpretation. Reflexive journaling, regular team discussions, and independent coding of a portion of the transcripts were used to reduce this potential influence, but it cannot be entirely eliminated. Fourth, participants were recruited from semesters five to eight, and the findings therefore primarily reflect the experiences of students with comparatively substantial clinical exposure; they may not fully represent the experiences of early-stage Operating Room Technology students. Finally, as with qualitative research, the findings aim for analytical transferability rather than statistical generalisability and should be interpreted in light of the specific context in which clinical feedback practices occur.

## Future Research

Future research could investigate how clinical feedback practices can be effectively designed and sustained across diverse workplace learning environments. Multi-centre qualitative and mixed-methods studies involving different health professions could further elucidate how organisational culture and educator characteristics influence clinical feedback processes. Research evaluating faculty development initiatives and structured clinical feedback models is also warranted. Pre- and post-training assessments, in particular, could examine changes in learner engagement and perceived clinical competence following educational interventions in Operating Room Technology education. Longitudinal designs could also explore how students’ feedback literacy develops over time and how it interacts with the clinical learning environment.

## Conclusion

This study suggests that clinical feedback in Operating Room Technology education is a multidimensional process shaped by structural, relational, organisational, and educator-related factors. Participants perceived clinical feedback as most valuable when it was timely, respectful, interactive, and embedded within supportive learning relationships, whereas inconsistent, delayed, or humiliating clinical feedback was perceived to hinder engagement and confidence.

These findings elaborate how established clinical feedback principles operate within the distinctive conditions of the operating room learning environment. The study contributes a context-specific understanding of how clinical feedback is perceived, negotiated, and sometimes constrained within this environment. Strengthening educator capabilities, embedding structured clinical feedback approaches, and fostering psychologically safe learning environments may enhance the quality of clinical education in Operating Room Technology programmes. However, these implications should be interpreted as potential directions for practice and further research rather than as demonstrated effects, and confirmation in other settings is warranted.
